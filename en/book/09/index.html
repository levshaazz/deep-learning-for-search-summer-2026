A week lost to a bug with no stack trace

I'm Séréga, and I once lost a week to a bug that never appeared in a stack trace. The model trained. The loss came down — slowly, stupidly, but down. Every tensor had exactly the shape it was supposed to have, every assertion I had bothered to write passed, and the thing quietly learned nonsense.

The bug was one axis wide. In a re-implemented attention block — the very block we finished weaving in the last chapter — I had normalised the score matrix along the wrong side: instead of every query spreading its attention over the keys, every key spread its attention over the queries. \(n \times n\) went in, \(n \times n\) came out. There was nothing for a type checker to see, nothing for a shape assertion to catch, no NaN, no crash. And the formula I had been staring at for four days — \( \mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V \) — cannot tell you which of the two axes that σ ran along, because the formula has nowhere to write it down.

Then a colleague drew me the picture. In a neural circuit diagram the softmax is a triangle, and the triangle points: it opens along the axis it normalises. Mine was turned a quarter-turn. The bug was visible from across the room, in a notation I had never bothered to learn. That is what this chapter is for — and by the end you will read something else off the same picture: the price.

Three questions the formula cannot answer

Be fair to the formula: it is not lying. \( \mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V \) is a complete, exact recipe for the value of the output. Follow it and you will get the right numbers. It just answers one question — what is computed — and it was never designed to answer the other three, which are the three you actually stand in front of at 2 a.m.

Which axis dies? \(QK^{\top}\) sums over \(d_k\) and that axis is gone forever; the juxtaposition of two letters is the only trace of an operation that deleted a whole dimension of your data. What does σ normalise across? The formula writes softmax once, with no room to say along which side of a square matrix, and that is exactly where my week went. What does it cost? Nothing in those symbols is bigger or heavier than anything else. \(QK^{\top}\) and \(\sqrt{d_k}\) sit in the same typeface, at the same size, and one of them is a division and the other is the thing that will eat your GPU.

A circuit diagram answers all three by drawing them, because in this notation a shape is not an annotation — it is the picture. An axis is a wire you can point at, so you can see it end. A softmax is a triangle that opens in a direction, so its direction is on the page. And every glyph has a size that you can price with one counting rule. The rest of this chapter is that claim, made good.

The lineage, honestly: it was not invented yesterday

Before you spend an evening learning a notation, you are entitled to know where it came from — otherwise this is just another picture off somebody's blog. It has four ancestors, and the honest way to introduce them is by what each one gave and what each one lacked. Penrose diagrams gave the founding idea: a wire is a tensor index, and joining two wires means you have summed over that index. What they had no notion of was a nonlinearity or a learned weight — they were built for algebra, not for models that are fitted. Tensor networks, out of many-body physics, added the thing this chapter is really about: contraction as the primitive operation, and the order of contractions as the cost. That is where the idea that a diagram carries a price comes from. But it is a physicist's language: no softmax, no heads, no mask.

String diagrams, from monoidal categories, contributed rigour of composition — sequential \(\circ\) and parallel \(\otimes\), with laws — which is what turns a drawing from an illustration into an object you can prove things about. Their cost is abstraction: there are no data axes and no shapes in them at all. And Einstein summation contributed the whole contraction rule compressed into one line — an index that appears on the left of the arrow and is dropped on the right has been contracted — and it has the rare distinction of already being implemented, in PyTorch, as einsum. Its weakness is that it is a string: composition is invisible in it, and you cannot see a block by looking at one.

What Abbott's 2024 paper (TMLR, arXiv:2402.05424) does is assemble all four and add exactly what none of them had, which is everything a neural network needs and a tensor does not: softmax across an axis, broadcast, branching, heads, masking. That is the notation this course introduced four slides at a time in Lecture 7 and finishes here. Knowing the lineage is not trivia — it tells you which parts of the notation are load-bearing mathematics with decades behind them, and which parts are a 2024 convention that your colleague has never seen.

A chapter about attention that stands on the delivery legs

Look at the spine before we learn a single glyph, because the map says something you would not have guessed. The subject here is attention — the same attention that put the previous chapter on Get Data and Measure — and yet the stops lit for this hour are Rank and Generate. The reason fits in one sentence: a circuit diagram encodes nothing and measures nothing. It prices. And a price is not a statement about meaning; it is a statement about what you can afford to serve.

So the two lit stops divide the hour by whose bill the picture reads. Rank is the Bridge, where a number turns into a decision: Scout or Judge, how many candidates the second stage may see, how deep a reranker may go before the answer stops arriving in time. That decision is a counting decision, and the counting rule you are about to learn hands you the answer before you build the thing. Generate is the Oracle's bill and it has its own shape: the cache that grows with every token already spoken, the context window you keep paying for, the query that walks into the pipeline twice. Same notation, same glyphs, two places where money is spent.

Now say plainly what the diagram will never do for you, because that silence is the point. It says nothing about Get Data: it draws exactly the same wires for a well-trained encoder and a ruined one, and it has no opinion at all about what a wire is carrying. It says nothing about Measure either: not one glyph on the page can tell you whether the ordering that comes out is any good — only what it cost to produce. A notation that also told you quality would not be a diagram, it would be a model. Take the reading rule with you: an hour that changes what you can afford rather than what you get belongs to the delivery legs, not to the legs where meaning is made and judged.

The grammar: a wire, a box, and a cup

A wire is an axis. Not a number, not a tensor — an axis: the token axis \(n\), the model width \(d\), the head axis \(h\). A tensor is whatever the wires happen to be carrying at that point in the drawing, and its shape is just the list of wires you cross if you cut across the page. That is the whole primitive, and everything else is a rule about what may happen to a wire.

A chipped rectangle is a learned linear operator — \(W_q\), \(W_k\), \(W_v\), \(L_O\), the two matrices of the feed-forward network. The chipped corner is not decoration: it marks which end is the input, because a linear map is not symmetric and neither is the box that draws it. A wire goes in at one width and comes out at another, and the parameters live inside.

A cup is a contraction — the glyph where an axis dies. Two wires carrying the same axis bend towards each other and join, and the axis simply ends there: you multiply term by term along it and add the products up, and it is not on the other side of the joint. This is the only glyph in the notation that can delete a dimension, and — as we will count in a moment — it is also where almost all of your money goes. In Russian we say стягивание, and never «свёртка», which is the word for a convolution and means something else entirely.

Three glyphs, and you can already read attention: project with boxes, join \(Q\) and \(K\) at a cup so that \(d\) dies, then join with \(V\) at a second cup so that the key axis dies. The rest of the vocabulary — the triangle, the hexagon, the dashed region, ⊕ and ◎ — exists to describe the things that do not kill an axis, and they are next.

The third thing that can happen to an axis

A wire says an axis lives; a cup says it dies. Between those two there is a third case, and the chipped rectangle is its only glyph: the axis changes length. One width goes in, another comes out, nothing is summed away — the axis is neither left untouched nor buried, it is re-sized. And the chip is what tells you in which direction that happened. A plain rectangle is symmetric under reflection, so \(W\) and \(W^{\top}\) would draw identically and the whole page could be read backwards; the cut corner breaks that symmetry and hands the drawing a direction the formula never had.

Now the part that is rarely said out loud: the chipped rectangle is the only glyph that holds parameters. A cup has nothing to learn — a contraction is arithmetic, not a weight. Triangles, hexagons and dashed regions own nothing either, and what ⊕ and ◎ carry is a rounding error beside a projection. So the entire parameter count of a model sits inside the boxes, and it does not depend on \(n\) in the slightest. Read that consequence slowly: as your context grows, the weights do not move a single byte, and everything that grows is activations. A longer context is not a bigger model. It is the same model holding more.

That splits the bill into two columns which people merge constantly. The boxes cost flops linear in \(n\) and storage that is flat in \(n\). The cups cost flops quadratic in \(n\) and give birth to an activation that is quadratic in \(n\). So when somebody says the model does not fit, the useful question is which column — a checkpoint that will not load and a sequence that will not run are opposite failures, and the picture separates them by glyph.

One statement, three alphabets. The bottom two panes are the einsum and the formula; the top is the circuit. Watch the letter d. In the einsum it appears on the LEFT of the arrow twice — once in Q’s ‘nd’, once in K’s ‘md’ — and on the RIGHT it does not appear at all. That is the complete definition of a contraction, and it is why the diagram has a CUP exactly there, and why the formula has a Σ exactly there. Nothing is being illustrated here: the three notations are the same object.

Now watch the letters that SURVIVE. n and m are on the right of the arrow, and they are exactly the two wires that leave the circuit. Read them in order and you have the output shape — n×m — with no arithmetic and no guessing. This is the payoff of taking the notation seriously: you never again have to run the code to find out what shape came out.

softmax is the exception that proves the rule. It works ACROSS m — every row is normalised over the key axis — but it does not CONSUME m: n×m goes in, n×m comes out. No letter disappears from the string, so there is no cup. That is exactly why the triangle is its own glyph and not a cup: a cup eats an axis, a triangle merely re-weights along one. Nothing else in attention behaves this way.

The second contraction, and the rule holds without amendment: m is repeated on the left (‘nm’ and ‘md’), absent on the right (‘nd’) — so m dies at a cup, and the formula sums over it. Step back and look at what you can now say without a picture: attention is TWO contractions with a softmax wedged between them. You can read that off the subscript strings alone. That is what it means for the diagram to BE the einsum rather than to illustrate it.

One row, three alphabets

Put all of attention in three columns — the einsum string, the glyph, the line of PyTorch — and read it row by row. Scores: bnd,bmd->bnm. The letter \(d\) appears twice on the left of the arrow and not at all on the right, and that is the whole test: an index that is repeated on the left and dropped on the right has been contracted. On the drawing, two wires meet in a cup and \(d\) is eaten. In code it is one torch.einsum call. Three notations, one statement.

Weights: bnm->bnm. Nothing vanished. No letter left the string, therefore there is no cup, therefore no memory was saved — this is a triangle across \(m\), and in code it is softmax(dim=-1). Stare at that for a second, because the entire difference between a model that works and the week I lost is which integer you put in dim, and the string does not warn you either: bnm->bnm is the same string both ways. Only the triangle's orientation records the choice. Context: bnm,bmd->bnd. Now \(m\) is the letter that disappears — the second cup — and the width \(d\) comes back on the right of the arrow.

Two habits fall out of this, and they are the practical core of the chapter. The output shape is whatever stands to the right of the arrow; you never have to run anything to know it. And the price is the letter the cup ate: contracting over \(d\) in the scores row costs \(2\,n\,m\,d\) flops by the counting rule we are about to meet — which is already the whole attention bill, before any table. The translation is mechanical in every direction: string to picture, picture to string, string to code. That is what makes the diagram an object rather than an illustration.

In fairness: what a formula is genuinely better at

If I only ever tell you what the formula cannot do, this chapter turns into an advertisement, so let me argue the other side properly. A formula composes: you can substitute it into another formula, expand it, differentiate it, prove something about the result. That is algebra, and it is real work that the drawing simply cannot do — a circuit you compose by redrawing it, by hand, and there is no chain rule for pencils. A formula is also dense: \( \mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V \) fits in a paper, on a slide, and inside a spoken sentence. The circuit of the same thing wants half a page and a few minutes of board time.

And a formula is text, which matters more than it sounds. You can search for it, cite it, paste it into a message, and diff it line by line against the paper you are trying to reproduce. A picture supports none of that. Against those three, set the three questions from the start of this chapter — which axis dies, what does σ normalise across, what does it cost — on which the formula is silent, and the circuit answers by drawing. So the conclusion is not «the circuit is better». It is that the circuit is a second view, and the bridge between the two views is mechanical: the einsum string.

One caveat before we go on, because it is the caveat people forget in their first week of enthusiasm: a circuit does not make a model correct. It tells you which axes are contracted and what that costs, and it will render a bad architecture just as neatly as a good one — every axis will line up, the invoice will add up, and the model will still be bad. Checkability is not correctness. The isomorphism holds exactly where einsum holds: tensors and static shapes. Everything data-dependent falls out of the picture, and we will come back to that at the end.

The cup, by hand: where an axis dies

Take the chain L7 already worked end to end — «the cat sat» — and stop at the moment two wires meet. Row 0 of \(Q\) is \((1, 0, 1, 0)\); row 0 of \(K\) is \((1, 0, 1, 0)\). The cup does the only thing a cup ever does: multiply term by term along the shared axis, then add.

\(1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 2\) — and that single number is \(\text{scores}[0][0]\). Four numbers went in and one came out. The axis \(d\) is gone.

Look at what the drawing tells you that the arithmetic does not. The axis \(d\) has ended — you can see the wire stop at the joint — and at the same instant a new axis was born: the score grid, \(n\) queries by \(n\) keys, one cell for every pair. Nothing in \( QK^{\top} \) is drawn any larger than \( \sqrt{d_k} \), yet one of these is a constant and the other is a square in \(n\). On the circuit it is the widest thing on the page.

That is the discipline the notation forces: every time you draw a cup, ask which axis just died — and every time an axis is created, look at how long it is. Both questions have exactly one glyph each, and both have a price. We will put a number on it shortly.

A matching shape proves nothing

Run one check on yourself before we go further. A circuit takes an \(n \times d\) wire and a \(d \times n\) wire into a cup, sends the result through a triangle, then joins it at a second cup with another \(n \times d\) wire; what leaves is \(n \times d\). How many axes died? The tempting answer is none, because the shape that went in is the shape that came out. The right answer is two funerals. The first cup contracted \(d\). The triangle buried nobody — no letter left the einsum string. The second cup killed the key axis, and the query axis walked out of the block alive. Two wires both labelled \(n\) are not one axis, and the \(n\) that leaves is not the \(n\) that entered the second cup.

So the reading rule is not compare the ends, it is follow one axis at a time, from its birth to its funeral. An axis can be born at a cup's output, re-sized by a box, wrapped by a dashed region, re-addressed by a hexagon and buried at another cup — and the two ends of the drawing record none of that. A block that preserves \(n \times m\) is not a block in which nothing happened; it is a block whose two ends agree, which is a far smaller claim than it sounds.

Keep that in hand for the act on debugging, because it is the same sentence read from the other side. Equal shapes at the boundary are consistent with a correct block, with a block that normalised the wrong way round, and with a block that summed over the wrong axis and got away with it because the lengths happened to match. The shape is the last entry in an axis's life. The picture is where the rest of that life is written down.

The triangle points. The hexagon reindexes. The dashes multiply.

A triangle is a softmax across an axis — and the word across is doing all the work. It normalises along that axis: exponentiate, sum, divide. It does not eat it. \(n \times n\) goes in, \(n \times n\) comes out; no letter leaves the einsum string, so there is no cup, and no memory is saved. That is exactly why softmax gets its own glyph instead of a cup: a cup consumes an axis, a triangle only re-weights along one. And since the shape is the same either way, the orientation of the triangle is the only record anywhere in the system of which axis you normalised. Remember that; a whole beat of this chapter is going to hang on it.

A hexagon is a reindex: slice, mask, top-\(k\), concat, transpose, reshape. It moves data around without computing anything — a causal mask is a hexagon, and so is the concat that stitches the heads back together. It costs (almost) no flops and it is where a shocking share of real bugs live, because it is the glyph where you get to say which axis is which, and you can say it wrong.

A dashed region is a broadcast axis — the head axis \(h\), the batch axis \(b\). The circuit inside the dashed frame is drawn once and run once per index along that axis. This is not a stylistic convenience: a broadcast multiplies whatever it wraps, so if the dashed region happens to enclose an \(n \times n\) score grid, you have just quietly bought twelve of them.

Finally ⊕ and ◎ — the residual add and LayerNorm. Both take a shape and return the same shape, and that is not a small remark: it is the entire reason a transformer stacks. Every sublayer is shape-preserving, so blocks compose end to end with no glue at all. Break that property in one sublayer and the tower stops being a tower.

Cut the residual and everything still runs

⊕ and ◎ earn their own glyphs, and it is worth pausing on why, because they look like the two operations in the block that do the least. The residual add joins two wires of the same shape and adds them cell by cell — and it is emphatically not a cup: a cup sums along an axis and buries it, while ⊕ sums two tensors and buries nothing. LayerNorm normalises across the width, one token at a time; like the triangle it re-weights an axis without eating it. Neither computes anything you would bother to put on an invoice.

They are also the only two operations in the block obliged to hand back the shape they were given, and that obligation is the entire reason a tower exists. Every sublayer returns what it received, so a block plugs into the next block with no adapter anywhere on the page — stacking is not an achievement of design, it is a consequence of two shape-preserving glyphs. It also settles an argument usually conducted in words: pre-norm and post-norm are two positions for ◎ on the wire, and once they are drawn you point at the difference instead of describing it.

Now delete the ⊕ and look at what breaks. Nothing does, and that is the point. The shapes still line up, the forward pass still computes, every assertion still passes — and the circuit quietly stops learning, because the one wire the gradient could ride straight back to \(x\) is gone. This is the single place where the drawing says anything about the backward pass, and it says it by the presence or absence of a line. A bug you can point at with a finger is exactly what this notation promised.

Zero flops, one copy

The hexagon computes nothing, and the counting rule prices it at nothing — which is true and misleading in the same breath. A reindex does no arithmetic: no multiply, no add, nothing for \(2 \cdot a \cdot b \cdot c\) to bite on. What it does instead is declare which axis is which, and declaring that usually means moving bytes. A transpose, which a formula writes as a superscript, is inside a machine either a fresh tensor written out in a new order or a promise to read the old one differently — and which of the two you got is in neither the picture, nor the einsum string, nor the formula.

So file the hexagon under memory rather than under flops. The concat that stitches the heads back together allocates the joined tensor. A slice that keeps the top of a ranking may hold everything behind it alive. A reshape the library cannot do by relabelling has to be done by copying. None of that changes the invoice you counted off the cups, and all of it changes the peak — which, as this chapter keeps insisting, is the number that actually stops your run.

And then the second half, which explains why a glyph that costs almost nothing owns a wildly disproportionate share of real bugs. The hexagon is the only glyph where you state the correspondence between axes — this axis of the output is that axis of the input — and a statement is a thing you can get wrong without contradicting anything. Swap two axes in a reshape and the lengths can still agree, the block still runs, and the model learns from a tensor whose rows mean something other than what you believe they mean. Cheap to execute, expensive to be wrong about: that is the hexagon's real line in the ledger.

Contract d, then scale. The token wire (blue) is projected by learned boxes into Q, K, V. Q and K meet at the CUP — the cup is exactly where an axis disappears, and here it eats d, leaving raw dot products. The ÷√dₖ box then divides them by √4 = 2, and the row that reaches softmax is [1, 0, 3]. Now watch the ledger: d died, but an n×n axis was just BORN. Nothing in the picture warns you about it, and that axis is the whole quadratic cost of attention.

softmax over the key axis — and this time you can see inside it. The triangle is not magic: it exponentiates every score, sums the exponentials, and divides each by that sum. The panel under the triangle does exactly that, term by term, with the real numbers. Note what does NOT happen: the shape does not change. n×n goes in, n×n comes out. softmax is free in memory terms — the damage was already done at the cup.

Context. The V wire arrives and a second cup contracts the key axis: the weighted sum turns the big n×n attention matrix back into a compact n×d context (0.958, 0.886). The whole life of the expensive axis is visible in one picture — born at the first cup, killed at the second. Note where this chain STOPS: at the output of ONE head. Putting the h heads back together (concat + L_O) is a separate operation, and it has its own diagram.

Broadcast — and the bill. The head axis h and the batch axis b are broadcasts: woven wires, not a special ‘batch rule’ hidden in the code. But a weave MULTIPLIES whatever it wraps, and what it wraps is the n×n axis. Read the ledger: 12 heads at 512 tokens is 6.3 MB, at 4096 it is 403 MB, at 32768 it is 25.8 GB. The picture never changed shape — only n did.

Now break it. Delete the ÷√dₖ box and feed the SAME dot product, 6, down both branches. Unscaled, softmax(0,0,6) returns 0.995 — all but one-hot, and a saturated softmax has a vanishing gradient, so the layer stops learning. Scaled by √dₖ = 2, the score becomes 3 and softmax(0,0,3) returns a soft 0.909. The circuit still RUNS without that box. It just never learns — which is why the notation is worth having: the failure is a missing glyph, not a mystery.

Heads split the width — they do not add it

Take one head, honestly, before taking twelve. The token matrix \(x\) arrives at its full model width, and the three learned projections do not map it to that width again — they map each vector into a narrower space, \(m/h\) wide. Attention then happens entirely inside that narrow space and hands back a stripe of that width. Say the consequence out loud, because it is the one people get backwards: a head is not a copy of the model, it is a twelfth of it.

Now the broadcast, made concrete. The \(h\) heads are \(h\) literal copies of that same little circuit — same recipe, own weights, own stripe — and the dashed region is the head axis. Look at what does not change across the copies: the same \(x\) enters every one of them. The heads differ only in what each has learned to look for, and nothing about the input is duplicated to make that possible.

Then concat, which is a hexagon — a reindex, not a computation — and it is the exact inverse of the slicing that started this. It lays the \(h\) stripes side by side and they add back up to the model width exactly, at which point \(L_O\) mixes them and the block returns the shape it received. That is why multi-head attention does not cost \(h\) times a single head: the width was folded and unfolded, not multiplied. Had every head kept the full width instead, the head axis really would have cost \(h\) times more — and on the drawing that difference is a rectangle of a visibly different size, not a footnote about tensor shapes.

The mask is a hexagon: free in flops, decisive in meaning

Draw a decoder without a mask and look at what the picture says. The query wire at position \(t\) meets every key wire in the cup, including the keys that come after \(t\) — that is, including the token the model is being trained to predict. The answer is in the input. The loss will look wonderful and the model will have learned nothing you can use, because at generation time those wires do not exist. This is not a subtle failure; it is a leak, and the circuit draws it as an arrow going the wrong way in time.

The repair is a hexagon, and where you put it is the whole point. Slide it in between the cup and the triangle: it sends the forbidden positions to \(-\infty\), and the softmax then normalises over what is left, so the future gets exactly zero weight. The order is not negotiable. A triangle normalises whatever it is handed, so masking after the softmax means you have already spread weight onto the future and are merely rescaling the mess. Hexagon, then triangle — and on the drawing, unlike in the code, you cannot get the order wrong without seeing it.

Now price it, and notice the asymmetry. In flops the hexagon is free: it is a reindex, it computes nothing. In arithmetic you could in principle skip the scores you are about to throw away, which is where «causal attention is half the work» comes from. But the \(n \times n\) box is still allocated whole — every cell is paid for and roughly half of them are used. So the memory bill from the previous act does not move at all. A mask changes what the model may look at; it does not change what the machine must hold.

Position has no wire of its own

A fair question, asked every year at about this point: where is position in the circuit? Look for a position wire and you will not find one, and that is not an omission. Position enters inside the vector, not beside it — \(\tilde{x}_t = x_t + PE_t\) — so the operation is a ⊕ and the shape \(n \times m\) does not grow by a single entry. The one \(PE\) table is handed to every example in the batch: one wire in, every copy served, no new axis anywhere on the page.

That is the general reading of a dashed region, and it is worth stating as a rule: the dashed frame is the axis along which the circuit repeats without a single new weight. Heads repeat that way — the same \(x\) walks into every copy, which is exactly why heads never duplicate the input. The batch repeats that way — same weights, different examples. In all three cases the parameter count is untouched, which is what makes people call a broadcast free.

And that is the half of the sentence that costs money. A broadcast adds no parameters and multiplies every byte inside it. The \(n \times n\) box that was born at the first cup sits inside the head region and inside the batch region, so it is multiplied by \(h\) and then by \(b\) — which is precisely how a score grid that was a rounding error at short lengths becomes the thing that ends your training run. So when someone asks why a glyph that computes nothing is expensive, the answer is one line: it is not doing arithmetic, it is doing copies, and the memory bill counts copies.

Zoom out: the whole encoder is one figure

Pull back far enough and the whole encoder fits on one page, which is worth doing once because it locates every figure you have seen so far. Text enters as \(n\) tokens; an embedding table turns each into a vector; position is added inside those vectors; \(L\) blocks refine them without ever changing the shape. And then pooling — and pooling is a contraction, the most consequential one in this course: the \(n\) token vectors collapse into a single vector of width \(m\). The token axis dies. That is the move that makes a passage indexable at all: a document stops being a sequence and becomes a point.

The second thing the wide shot shows is where an index comes from, and it is not a second machine. Take that same encoder — the same boxes, the same weights — and run it over your \(N\) documents ahead of time. What drops out is one vector per document, and that pile of vectors is the index. Retrieval is affordable for exactly one reason: the expensive part can be run offline. Take that away — make the score depend on the query and the document together, the way a cross-encoder does — and the offline wire vanishes, and with it the index, and you are back to running a network \(N\) times per query.

Below the encoder is the funnel, and everything in it only narrows: the index of \(N\) documents, then a shortlist after retrieval, then a handful after reranking, then one answer after generation. Read it as a column of shapes and the entire course is a few lines long. Every arrow in that column costs money, and every arrow is a place where you trade recall against latency — which is the reason a cascade is shaped the way it is: cheap and broad first, expensive and narrow last. Every other circuit in this chapter is one node of this one.

The cheapest glyph, and you cannot remove it

Between the first cup and the triangle sits the smallest thing on the page: the division by \(\sqrt{d_k}\). It is elementwise, it touches each cell of the score grid exactly once, and next to the two cups it is loose change — on the invoice you counted it does not round to anything at all. Delete it and the circuit still type-checks, still runs, and still returns \(n \times n\). And it never learns.

The mechanism is the counting rule read backwards. The cup contracts over the width, so every score is a sum of that many products; add up more terms of a given size and the result grows with the axis you summed over — which is precisely what the division is there to undo. Hand the triangle scores that grow with the width and the exponentials separate: nearly all the mass lands on one key, the rest rounds away, and the derivative of a saturated softmax is close to zero almost everywhere. The block does not break. It goes numb.

Take the general lesson, because it outlives this glyph. The counting rule prices glyphs; it does not rank them by importance, and the two orders are not even loosely correlated. The cups are where the money goes, and they are hard to get wrong. The scale is free and fatal. What a glyph costs and what a glyph is for are separate questions — the drawing answers the first by its size, and the second only to a reader.

One counting rule, and the picture becomes an invoice

Here is the whole cost model of deep learning, and it fits on one line: an \((a \times b) \cdot (b \times c)\) matmul costs \(2 \cdot a \cdot b \cdot c\) flops — one multiply and one add per triple. That is it. Now walk the block and price each glyph, exactly as you would walk a wiring loom with a clicker.

The chipped rectangles first. \(W_q, W_k, W_v\) are three \((n \times d) \cdot (d \times d)\) matmuls: \(6nd^2\). The output projection \(L_O\) is one more: \(2nd^2\). The feed-forward network is two boxes, out to \(4d\) and back: \(16nd^2\). Add them.

Linear glyphs: \(6nd^2 + 2nd^2 + 16nd^2 = \mathbf{24nd^2}\). The two cups: \(QK^{\top}\) is \((n \times d) \cdot (d \times n) = 2n^2d\), and \(A \cdot V\) is \((n \times n) \cdot (n \times d) = 2n^2d\), so the attention core is \(\mathbf{4n^2d}\).

Look hard at those two lines, because everything else in this chapter is a consequence of them. The boxes are linear in \(n\) and quadratic in the width. The cups — and only the cups — are quadratic in \(n\). Not the softmax, not the mask, not the heads: the two joints where an axis dies. Every warning you have ever heard about attention being quadratic is a statement about two glyphs, and now you can point at them.

Where the square comes from: count the n wires

Walk the block one more time, but do not add anything up. Just count, for each glyph, how many of its wires carry the token axis. The three projections touch \(n\) once each; the output projection once; both feed-forward boxes once. The first cup touches it twice — an \(n \times d\) wire meeting a \(d \times n\) wire — and so does the second. That is the whole derivation of the word quadratic, and it took no arithmetic at all.

The counting rule then makes it exact. A matmul's price is the product of the three lengths on its wires, so a glyph carrying \(n\) on one wire is linear in \(n\), and a glyph carrying \(n\) on two is quadratic in it. The exponent on \(n\) is the number of \(n\) wires the glyph touches, read straight off the picture before a single constant is substituted. Everything else in the block only sets the coefficient.

This is the version of attention is quadratic that survives contact with reality, because it also tells you what is not. The feed-forward network — the largest single expense in a block at the lengths most people actually train at — touches \(n\) once and therefore grows exactly as fast as your text does. Double the context and it doubles. Double the context and the cups quadruple. Two sentences, no profiler, and you already know which half of the block your next change of length is going to hurt.

Where does “quadratic” actually start to bite? At n = 6d.

Everybody knows attention is quadratic. Almost nobody can tell you where the quadratic term starts to matter — and that is not a gap in anyone's knowledge, it is a gap in the notation. The formula has no place to put it. The two lines we counted off the drawing do, and they answer it in one step: set them equal.

\(4n^2d = 24nd^2 \;\iff\; 4n = 24d \;\iff\; \mathbf{n = 6d}\). For BERT-base, \(d = 768\), so the crossover is at \(n = \mathbf{4608}\) tokens — where each half of the block costs about 65.2 GFLOP and neither dominates.

Now put real lengths through it. At \(n = 512\) — the context BERT was actually trained at — the attention core is 10.0 % of the block's flops: the two cups everyone fears are a tenth of the bill, and the boxes nobody draws are the other nine. At \(n = 4096\) it is 47.1 %, still not the whole story. At \(n = 32768\) it is 87.7 %, and the machine has become one enormous cup with a transformer attached.

So the honest summary is not «attention is expensive». It is: below \(6d\) tokens, your model is mostly the feed-forward network — the part with no scary asymptotics and no papers written about it — and above \(6d\), the cup eats the machine. One inequality, read off two glyphs. If you want to know whether the quadratic term is your problem today, you do not need a profiler; you need to know your \(n\) and your \(d\).

Memory bites first — and the box need never exist

The crossover at \(6d\) is about flops. Memory does not wait that long. Between the first cup and the second, the \(n \times n\) score grid has to sit somewhere, and the dashed head-region multiplies it by \(h\). In fp16 that is \(h \cdot n^2 \cdot 2\) bytes, and you can read the whole story off that product without running anything.

12 heads, fp16: at \(n = 512\) the score box is 6.3 MB — nothing. At \(n = 4096\), 403 MB. At \(n = 32768\), 25.8 GB — and it is gone: no consumer card holds that, and this is one block of twelve, in the forward pass alone.

So at \(n = 4096\) the flops say attention is 47.1 % — a nuisance — while the bytes say you are already out of memory. This is the failure mode people actually hit, and it is the axis that is born at a cup, not the flops that flow through it. The picture shows you the box. The formula shows you a product of two matrices, which weighs nothing at all.

And then the trick that makes the notation more than bookkeeping. FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) does not make attention cheaper in flops — go back and count the cups, they are exactly where they were. What it does is never materialise the box: it walks the score grid in tiles, keeping a running softmax, and writes only the \(n \times d\) output. On the circuit, the wire between the two cups is still there — it is the tensor that is gone. Once you have the glyph for "an axis is alive between here and here", that optimisation is not clever, it is obvious. That is what a good notation is for.

Prefill. The whole prompt — n tokens at once — runs through the projections exactly once: L_Q, L_K, L_V. Here the query is a whole n-row block, so attention really does build the full n×n score matrix — and in fp16, across 12 heads, that is 25.8 GB at n=32768. The ledger prices it, and this is the ONE step where that price is real. The K and V rows are not thrown away when the step ends: they are KEPT, and the dashed violet region is that cache — n rows of K and n rows of V, i.e. 2·n·d numbers per layer, per head. Notice what is NOT cached: the queries. A q is used once, this step, and discarded.

Now decode one token. Exactly one token arrives, so exactly one q, one new k row and one new v row are computed — that is every warm thing in the picture, and it is the entire cost of the step. Now look at the old rows: there is no box in front of them any more. The gutter is empty. They are not recomputed, they are READ; the projections that made them ran once, in the prefill, and never run again. Then look at the attention block: exactly ONE query wire enters it, so the scores it forms are 1×(n+1) — a single row against the n+1 keys, kilobytes. Nothing here builds an n×n matrix. The sequence axis has quietly become n+1.

The trade, in shapes. With the cache, one more token costs ONE row of K and ONE row of V; without it you would have to re-run L_K and L_V over all n+1 positions — the entire prefill, on every single token. You buy that compute with memory, and here is the part almost everyone gets backwards: while you are DECODING, the cache is the expensive axis and the attention is the cheap one. The cache holds K and V — 2·n·d per layer per head — and grows with every token you emit, until it is gigabytes. A step's attention is one query row against n+1 keys: 1×(n+1) scores, kilobytes. The n×n figure you saw back at prefill (25.8 GB at n=32768, over 12 heads, in fp16) is the PREFILL bill; a decode step is some four orders of magnitude cheaper than that. The attention work only turns quadratic when you SUM the steps of a whole generation: n(n+1)/2. That inversion is precisely why MQA, GQA and paged attention exist — every one of them shrinks the CACHE.

The axis that grows — and the trick the diagram gives away

During generation the picture changes character completely, and the formula does not change at all. One token arrives; one \(q\) row, one \(k\) row, one \(v\) row are computed. The old \(k\) and \(v\) rows are not recomputed — they are read, out of the KV cache. Look at the circuit and you see it immediately: there is no box in front of those rows any more. The projections that made them ran once, in the prefill.

So you have traded compute for memory, and the cost has moved onto an axis that grows. For BERT-base geometry — 12 layers, 12 heads, fp16 — one token of cache is \(2 \cdot L \cdot h \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot 2\) bytes:

MHA: 12 KV heads → 36.9 KB per token; at \(n = 32768\) that is 1208 MB of cache, for a model whose weights would fit in a fraction of it. MQA (one KV head): 3.1 KB per token → 100.7 MB, a 12× shrink. GQA-4 (four KV groups): 12.3 KB per token → 402.7 MB, a 3× shrink.

Now here is the part I want you to see rather than memorise. Why is MQA free of any change on the query side? Because it removes the head axis from \(K\) and \(V\) only — the query wire keeps all 12 heads, and the broadcast region simply reuses the same \(k\) and \(v\) against each of them. On the circuit that is one dashed frame moved by half a centimetre, and every wire still meets a wire of the same length. Read it in the formula and it is a footnote about tensor shapes. Read it in the picture and it is the whole idea.

The bug this notation exists for

Now we can go back to my lost week and say precisely what happened. I normalised the score matrix along the query axis instead of the key axis. \(n \times n\) in, \(n \times n\) out. Every row still summed to one — just the wrong rows. Instead of «each query distributes its attention over the keys», the model computed «each key distributes its attention over the queries», which is a sentence that means nothing at all.

Count what could have caught it. A type checker: no — the dtype was fine. A shape assertion: no — the shape was identical, that is the entire point. A NaN guard: no. A unit test on output shapes: no. The loss curve? It went down, because a transformer is a stubborn animal and the other eleven blocks will paper over a great deal. The only symptoms were a loss that plateaued too early and an attention-entropy plot that nobody was looking at.

This is the class of bug the notation exists for, and it is worth naming: shape-preserving bugs. They live inside the glyphs that do not change the shape — the triangle, the hexagon, ⊕ and ◎ — precisely because the shape is the thing every other tool in your stack checks. The moment you write the block as a circuit, the mistake is not subtle; the triangle is turned the wrong way, and a turned triangle is visible from the other side of the room.

When the string and the picture disagree

Here is the exercise I would put in front of anyone who says they can read this notation. The code for the second contraction of attention says O = torch.einsum('nm,md->nd', P, V). The circuit drawn beside it joins the \(n\) wire of \(P\) to the \(m\) wire of \(V\), and the wires leaving the cup are \(m\) and \(d\). One of the two is wrong. Before reading on, decide which — and notice that the temptation is to trust whichever one you wrote most recently.

Read the string first, on its own, with the picture covered. On the left: \(n\), \(m\), \(m\), \(d\). On the right: \(n\), \(d\). The letter that appears on the left and not on the right is \(m\) — so the cup must eat \(m\), and the output must be \(n \times d\). The string is self-consistent, and it is exactly the second contraction of attention: the key axis dies and the width comes back. So the drawing is the broken one. Follow the drawing and the cup joins two wires carrying different axes, and what leaves is indexed by \(m\) and \(d\) — an output with no query axis in it at all, which is not a slightly wrong tensor, it is a tensor about nothing.

The habit worth taking away is a small one and it saves hours. A cup is not «symmetric» in the sense of not caring which wires it joins — it joins two wires that carry the same axis, of the same length, and it deletes it. So when two records of the same computation disagree, do not patch the one you touched last: read them both against the axis names and ask which one type-checks. In practice the string is usually right and the drawing has drifted, for the unglamorous reason that the string is the thing the machine actually executed.

Three bugs, and only one of them is silent

Put three broken circuits side by side, because what decides the class of a bug is when it tells you. A shape bug — a cup handed two wires of different lengths, a \(K\) that was never transposed — crashes on the first matmul it reaches. It costs ten minutes, any shape check catches it, and so does one glance at the cup. A composition bug — a block that leaves the head axis dangling instead of stitching it back — crashes too, but one block later, where the tower's shapes fail to meet; still cheap, because a tower only stacks while every sublayer returns what it was handed. A meaning bug — the triangle turned a quarter turn — never crashes at all. Its when column is empty, and not because the table is unfinished.

The reason deserves one flat sentence: a shape check is a type system for axes, not for meaning. It knows the tensor is \(n \times n\). It cannot know, because the type does not say so, that each row of that tensor is obliged to be a distribution over keys rather than over queries. Nothing else in the stack carries the obligation either — not the einsum string, which records which letters die and is silent about which are normalised; not the formula, where the axis lives in the prose around the σ; not the code, where it is one integer no linter reads.

Stronger tensor type systems — named axes, shape types — move that boundary, and it is worth being exact about the direction. They move it further along the same axis, making it harder to confuse two dimensions of different meaning by giving each a name. None of them will ask you which axis you normalised across, because normalising does not change the type. The circuit draws an axis's role and not only its length, and the width of that gap is the width of the job this notation exists to do.

Bug 1 — a SHAPE bug. Q is 3×4 and K is 3×4. To score query against key you must contract the d axis, so K has to arrive transposed: 4×3. It has not. What actually meets at the cup is an axis of size 4 and an axis of size 3, and a cup can only contract axes that MATCH — so there is nothing to contract and the circuit comes apart right there. This bug is the easy kind: a type checker catches it, and so does a glance at the picture.

Bug 2 — a MEANING bug, and this is the one that will ruin your week. Both panels below are the SAME circuit: the same n×n score grid, held between the same two wires — the query axis n running down the left, the key axis m running across the top. Compare them and only ONE thing differs: the softmax triangle. On the right it expands ALONG the key axis, so each ROW is a distribution over the keys — correct, and the Σ=1 chips sit beside the rows. On the left the very same triangle has been turned a quarter turn: it now expands down the QUERY axis, so each COLUMN sums to one. Look at the shapes: n×n before, n×n after, in both. Identical. Every shape assertion passes, nothing crashes, no stack trace ever appears; the model simply learns nonsense and you spend a week bisecting hyperparameters. The triangle is the only thing on the page that can tell you — which is precisely why NCD draws it.

Bug 3 — a COMPOSITION bug. Multi-head attention SPLITS the model dimension: with h=12 heads and m=768, each head works in n×64. That is fine — until you forget to put the axis back. Here the block emits h×n×64: the head axis is still dangling. The next block wants n×768 and will not fit. Blocks compose end to end for exactly one reason — every sublayer preserves the n×m shape — and this one does not. The fix is the bottom row: a concat hexagon (which is precisely the inverse of the split: 12 × 64 = 768) followed by the output projection L_O.

Circuit → einsum → code, in that order

Here is the workflow this chapter is actually for, and it takes about four minutes. Draw the wires you have — one per axis, labelled. Draw the boxes that change a width. Draw a cup wherever two things get multiplied and summed, and say out loud which axis dies there. Draw a triangle wherever you normalise, pointing along the axis you normalise across.

Now read the einsum string straight off the picture: the wires entering are the left of the arrow, the wires leaving are the right, and every letter that appears on the left but not on the right is a cup. You have not run anything, and you already know the output shape and the flop count. Only then write the code — and if the string you typed does not match the string you read, one of the two is wrong, and you know exactly where to look.

This is also, I think, how the good optimisations get found. FlashAttention is a statement about a wire: the axis between the two cups is alive for a moment and then it is dead, so it never needs to exist all at once. MQA is a statement about a dashed region: shrink it on the \(K\)/\(V\) side and the query side never notices. Neither is easy to see in \( \mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V \). Both are hard to miss on the drawing.

Draw your own I: count the encoder boxes

Now use the notation on something that is not a transformer block. Draw a query being scored against a corpus, with the corpus as a broadcast axis \(N\) and the retriever's per-document work drawn inside the dashed region. For a bi-encoder, each document card already carries its vector, computed offline; the only encoder box anywhere on the page sits on the query, and the per-document work is a single dot product. Count the encoder rectangles: one. That single count is the reason this architecture scales to a corpus you cannot fit on one machine.

Now draw a cross-encoder the same way and watch what disappears. The precomputed vectors are gone — where a cached vector would sit there is an empty slot — because the encoder is required to read the query and the document together, so nothing about a document can be computed before the query arrives. Count the rectangles again: \(N\) of them, one per document, per query. Nothing in the accuracy argument changed; the drawing changed completely. And that is the whole reason the accurate model lives only in reranking, downstream of something that has already thrown most of the corpus away.

A third drawing sits between them. Late interaction puts one encoder box on the query again, like the bi-encoder, but moves the contraction later: instead of collapsing each side to a point and joining once, it keeps a vector per document token and contracts token against token. On the page that is a grid of stored vectors and a per-document cup that is no longer a single dot product. You pay in storage what you saved in encoder passes. Three architectures, one figure each, and the trade between them is legible before you have read a single benchmark table — which is the next chapter, drawn in advance.

Draw your own II: only the axis shrank

Exact nearest-neighbour search is the same figure with the broadcast region wrapped around the entire corpus. Every indexed document gets a cup — a dot product against the query — so the work is \(N\) contractions, and it scales with the corpus and nothing else. In exchange you get the one thing exact search can promise and approximate search never can: recall is one by construction. Not measured, not hoped for; guaranteed by the fact that nothing was skipped.

Now draw approximate search. Here is the point of the exercise: not a single glyph changes. Same wires, same cups, same query. What changed is the size of the dashed region — it shrank from the corpus to a candidate set \(C\) reached by a walk through a proximity graph. And read \(C\) carefully, because this is where people underestimate the cost: \(C\) is not just the documents the walk expanded. Their neighbours have cups too — you had to score them in order to choose the next hop. So the work follows \(|C|\), roughly hops times graph degree, still far below \(N\), and the invoice follows the candidate set rather than the corpus.

And now look at what has no cup in front of it at all. The documents outside \(C\) are never scored — not scored badly, not scored approximately: never scored. So a true neighbour that no expanded vertex can see is simply a neighbour that was never evaluated, and that is precisely what “recall below one” means, drawn. The picture makes the honest shape of the deal visible: this is not a bug and not an approximation error in the numerical sense — it is a guarantee traded for a budget, and the axis that shrank is exactly the thing you bought it with.

Draw your own III: the query enters twice

The last circuit to draw is the one the whole second half of the course builds, and it is worth drawing badly once before drawing it well. The first thing most people get wrong is the corpus: they draw it as a box with an arrow coming out of it. It is not a box, it is an axis — the axis the query broadcasts along — so it belongs on the page as a region enclosing \(N\) document wires. Once you draw it that way, the first hexagon has an obvious job: it is the slice that cuts that axis down, \(N \to K\).

Then a second hexagon does it again, \(K \to k\), after a slower and more careful reader has reordered what survived. Say the resulting sentence out loud, because it deflates a great deal of architecture-diagram mystique: a cascade is two hexagons and nothing else. The expensive reader is affordable only because the first slice already threw away everything but \(K\); take the first hexagon out and the second stage is not slow, it is impossible.

And then the one arc that makes it RAG rather than search: the query enters a second time, alongside the \(k\) surviving documents, to condition the generator. That dashed arc is the entire idea. The model is not being asked what it knows; it is being asked what these \(k\) documents say — about precisely the query that fetched them. Everything that can go wrong later in the course goes wrong somewhere along that arc: the documents can be the wrong ones, the model can ignore them, and the answer can sound excellent either way. But you can point at where, on a drawing you can make yourself.

The frontier: a notation you can derive in

Credit first, because the temptation to blur it here is real. FlashAttention is Dao's algorithm and Dao's engineering. It is exact attention — the same mathematics, not an approximation and not a sparsity trick — implemented so that the \(n \times n\) box never materialises in slow memory. Two ideas carry it: tiling, which cuts the token axes into blocks that fit in fast memory, and a streaming softmax that can rewrite its own normalisation as the blocks arrive. That is systems work, and it is theirs.

What Abbott and Zardini added is the part that belongs in this chapter: they showed the same tiling follows from the diagram, almost mechanically. The circuit states outright which axes take part in a contraction and which are free, so it is immediately visible which of them can be cut into blocks without disturbing a single cup. In the notation the whole manoeuvre is one more hexagon — a slice — and nothing else. The wire stays; only the tensor goes. They did not invent the optimisation; they derived it, and the derivation is short enough to fit on a napkin.

That is the strongest claim this chapter makes, so let me also fence it. A notation measures nothing, profiles nothing and writes no CUDA kernel; what it does is narrow the space of things worth trying and make the argument checkable by someone else. But that is not a small service. It moves the drawing out of the category of documentation added after the fact and into the category of medium — the place the algorithm can be found in the first place. «On a napkin» describes the length of the derivation, not the difficulty of the implementation.

What the notation cannot say

A notation that can say everything says nothing, so let me be exact about where this one stops. Control flow is not drawable. An if on a value — beam search, early exit, a mixture-of-experts router that sends different tokens down different boxes — has no glyph, because the wires would have to change depending on what flows through them. Anywhere the shape is data-dependent, the circuit becomes a circuit per batch, and it stops being a picture you can hold.

Training dynamics are not drawn either. The diagram is the forward pass. It will not tell you whether the thing converges, whether the learning rate is sane, or what the optimiser is doing — with one honourable exception: the residual wire is a claim about the backward pass, and deleting it visibly cuts the identity path the gradient rides on. That is one gradient statement, not a theory of optimisation.

Numerics are invisible. fp16 versus bf16, the max-subtraction hidden inside a stable softmax, an overflow in the exponentials, the fact that your \(\div\sqrt{d_k}\) box exists precisely to keep the triangle out of saturation — the picture is serenely indifferent to all of it. Two circuits that are glyph-for-glyph identical can differ by an entire training run.

And a boundary that is not mathematical at all: nobody else on your team reads this notation yet. It is a 2024 paper, not a standard. Draw it on your own whiteboard, draw it when you are hunting a shape-preserving bug, draw it on a napkin when someone claims attention is the expensive part — but do not paste it into a pull request and expect it to be understood. The notation earns its keep in your head first. That is where the week I lost was spent, and that is where you get it back.

When not to draw one

The previous beat fenced what the notation cannot say. This one fences where it is not worth it, which is a question of taste and costs your reader a minute of attention you have to earn. Do not draw a circuit for a model that fits on one line: a linear layer and a softmax produce a diagram longer than the code, and at that point the drawing is a pose. Do not put one in an abstract, which carries a single claim and will drown under five axes. Do not put one on a slide you have half a minute for — reading a circuit honestly takes about a minute, not because it is bad but because it is dense, and the density is the whole value.

And the case that matters most: do not draw one when your audience needs the what and not the how much. Explaining retrieval to somebody who has to decide whether to fund it, the chain of three boxes — retrieve, rerank, generate — is the honest picture, because it answers the question being asked. Do not confuse this with the complaint made earlier, that a block diagram hides the query's second entry. That is a complaint only when you need the second entry.

The mirror column is the one to memorise. Draw one when the axes have got tangled and you are no longer sure which is which. Draw one when you have to count — flops, the score grid, the cache. Draw one when the code will outlive you and go to somebody else. And draw one when the bug does not crash, because then the circuit is your only witness. A block diagram and a wiring diagram answer different questions — the first says what happens, the second says at what price — and the mistake is never picking one. It is not noticing that you are picking.

What a drawing costs you

A notation whose limits are never stated quietly turns into an obligation. People start drawing every function they touch, because that is now How It Is Done, and half an hour goes into a diagram that settles a question one line of code would have settled faster. So here is the honest accounting, in the same spirit as the boundaries slide: the circuit earns its half-hour when an axis is in doubt — what it is called, who consumes it, what you normalise across, where it comes back. It does not earn anything when the doubt is about a value, a version, or a silent shape mismatch that a single print(x.shape) would expose.

The second cost is subtler and worth naming. A diagram is a claim about structure, and a claim can be stale: you draw the block once, the model changes, and the picture keeps telling everyone the old story with the full authority of something that was clearly hand-made. Code at least fails loudly when it rots. A drawing rots silently, which is why the ones worth keeping are the ones attached to something executable — the einsum string they were derived from, a test, a shape assertion.

Neither cost argues against the notation; both argue against drawing on autopilot. Use the circuit where the question is which axis, keep it next to the code it describes, and let the interpreter answer everything else.

The order the circuit hides

Here is a boundary you can watch rather than take on trust. The cup says: contract this axis, sum over the index. Summation on the reals is associative, so the drawing names no order and owes you none. Floating-point addition is not associative, and the gap is not subtle: \((1 + 10^{16}) - 10^{16}\) evaluates to 0 in double precision, while \(1 + (10^{16} - 10^{16})\) evaluates to 1. Same three numbers, same sum, two answers.

So two kernels that draw as the identical circuit — same wires, same cup, same einsum string — may legitimately disagree in their low bits, because one adds in tiles and the other in a reduction tree. This is exactly why the honest phrasing about FlashAttention is mathematically exact, not bit-for-bit identical: it computes the same sum, in a different order, on purpose.

Two things to keep. First, the practical debugging rule: a mismatch in the sixth significant digit is almost always summation order, not a wrong wire — look at the reduction before you re-read the diagram. Second, the honest scope of the notation: it is a language about structure, and arithmetic lives one level below it. A tool that is clear about what it does not model is worth more than one that quietly implies it models everything.

Read the bill off the picture

Take four things away. A wire is an axis — and the shape of any tensor is just the wires you cut across. A cup is where an axis dies, and it is the only glyph that can delete one; the two cups in attention are the only quadratic things in the block. A triangle normalises across an axis and does not eat it, which is why the shape survives — and why the way it is turned is the only record you will ever have of which axis you chose. And ⊕ and ◎ preserve the shape, which is the entire reason a transformer stacks at all.

Then one rule — \(2 \cdot a \cdot b \cdot c\) per matmul — turns the drawing into an invoice: \(24nd^2\) in the boxes, \(4n^2d\) in the cups, and the crossover solves itself at \(n = 6d\). Everything else in this chapter was a consequence: 10.0 % at \(n = 512\), 47.1 % at 4096, 87.7 % at 32768; 4608 tokens for BERT-base; 6.3 MB → 403 MB → 25.8 GB of score box; 36.9 KB of cache per generated token. Not one of those numbers was looked up. They were counted off the picture, and you can now count them yourself.

And the last one is the one I paid for. A formula tells you what to compute. A circuit tells you what it costs and which axis it eats — and, when the axis is one you got backwards, it tells you that too, at a glance, in a notation where a mistake has a shape.
