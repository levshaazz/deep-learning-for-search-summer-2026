After «th», the «e» is almost free

You have just come from the star catalog. There you built an inverted index, scored it with BM25, and inside that score sat a weight called IDF that quietly paid rare words more than common ones — a logarithm nobody explained. It came from this room. Let me sit you down at my telegraph key. It is 1948, Bell Labs, and above the brass sounder I have wired a little gauge — a needle on a dial marked not in volts but in bits. Every letter that clacks through gives the needle a shove. Tap a t, then an h, and watch: the next letter arrives and the needle barely stirs. After th_ it is almost surely e — I could have written it myself before it came. It surprises no one. It is almost free.

That is the whole trick of the gauge: it measures surprise. A symbol I could have guessed moves the needle a hair; a symbol I never saw coming — a q out of nowhere, a z mid-word — slams it to the top. So here is the question this whole chapter turns on: how much is a letter worth? Not in pennies per word, the way the telegraph company charges — but in bits, the honest currency of surprise.

And painted across the face of my gauge is one red line. No message, however cleverly you squeeze it, ever pushes the average needle below that mark. It is the entropy floor — a hard limit you can creep toward but never cross. Everything ahead — Morse counting type, Shannon naming the bit, English measured at about one bit a letter, every language model since — is the long story of that red line. Information is surprise; surprise has a floor.

The dot they gave to E

I sit at my telegraph key in 1948 with a needle that twitches at every surprise — but before anyone could measure surprise, someone had to make a single letter into a thing you could count. A hundred years before me, two men did exactly that: Samuel Morse and his partner Alfred Vail.

They did not count in their heads. They walked into a printer's shop and opened the type-cases of a newspaper — the little wooden compartments of cast-metal letters. Whichever letter the printer stocked the most of was the letter English leaned on the hardest. 'E' — about one letter in eight (near 12.7% by later tallies). So to the commonest letter they handed the shortest possible signal: a single dot. Rarer letters earned longer strings. Frequent → short.

They had no theory and no formula. Yet by hand they built the very rule a whole science would prove much later: spend your shortest codes on your commonest symbols. Huffman would write it down exactly in 1952 — Morse and Vail had done it a century early, just by counting type. That is the moment information became a quantity — something you could weigh right there in the room.

How close did the hand-built code get?

I said two men at a type-case built by hand the rule a science would later prove, and I owe you the grading. It is not hard to grade: take the same letter frequencies, build the best possible assignment of dots and dashes for them, and compare the average cost of a letter under each. Do it and Morse's code comes out close behind — the intuition frequent gets short is worth most of what the theory buys. What the theory adds at the end is not much accuracy. It is a guarantee: Morse could believe his assignment was good, and Huffman's procedure produces one that provably nothing beats.

But now the trap, and it is the same trap this whole chapter keeps setting. How close is close depends entirely on what you agreed to count. Price a dash as a fixed multiple of a dot and you get one answer. Now count the silences too — the small gap the operator must leave between symbols and the larger one between letters — and the answer moves, because the gaps are a tax levied on every letter alike, and a flat tax paid by every code narrows the distance between a good code and a bad one. Same code, same frequencies, two efficiencies, both honest.

So carry the habit out of the type-case: an efficiency is a ratio, and a ratio is a claim about its yardstick. Before you accept that a code, an index, or a model is within a hair of optimal, ask what units the hair was measured in and whether the fixed costs were counted on both sides. It is the identical question that decides, much later in this chapter, whether a machine really out-guessed a human — and the identical question that made two columns of my own table look contradictory when they were merely differently ruled.

Twenty years before my gauge, two Bell men turned 'how much information' into a number — and the whole trick was the logarithm

Long before I ever watched a needle jump in bits, the amount of information already had a number — pinned down by two men a few doors down the same Bell corridors. In 1924 Harry Nyquist noticed that the speed a telegraph line can carry rises not with the number of distinct signal levels you allow, but with its logarithm. Four years later Ralph Hartley hardened it into a formula: a message of \( n \) symbols, each chosen from an alphabet of \( S \) possibilities, carries \( n \cdot \log S \) worth of information. Not a feeling — a quantity you can weigh on a scale.

Why the logarithm, of all things? Because information has to add up. Tap two keys, each one choice out of \( S \), and together they open \( S^2 \) possible messages — the choices multiply. But two taps ought to carry twice the information of one, not \( S \) times as much. And \( \log(S^2) = 2\log S \): the logarithm quietly turns multiplying-choices into adding-information. Send a message twice as long, carry twice the bits. That additivity — nothing fancier — is the whole reason the log is there.

One thing hides in Hartley's count: he weighed every symbol as equally likely, every choice equally wide open. Under that single assumption, \( n \cdot \log S \) is already entropy — Shannon's \( H \) for a perfectly even source, the flat ceiling where the needle sits at its very highest. Nobody had yet asked what happens when E is common and Q is rare — that is where the floor will drop below the ceiling. But the unit, the logarithm, the way information adds: all of it was here, waiting at the key, two decades before I ever plugged the gauge in.

Language is not a coin toss: the next letter leans on the last

Thirty-five years before I first laid my hand on this key, a mathematician in St. Petersburg ran the same experiment with no key, no wire, no gauge — only a pen and patience. In 1913 Andrei Markov took twenty thousand letters from the opening of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin and counted them by hand, one at a time, sorting each into two bins: vowel or consonant. By his tally 43 in every hundred were vowels, 57 consonants — but the tally was never the point.

The point was what came next. Markov did not treat the letters as independent draws from an urn, the way you'd count a bag of marbles. He counted pairs — and found that a vowel is far more likely to be followed by a consonant than by another vowel, and a consonant by a vowel. The next letter leans on the one before it. Language, it turned out, is not a coin toss: it remembers. This was the first Markov chain — a process where the past bends the odds of the future.

And that memory is exactly what my gauge feels. A symbol you half-expected barely moves the needle; only a genuine surprise makes it spike. If each letter is partly foretold by the one before, then most letters are half-expected — the needle rests low, and it rests low because the language has structure. Structure is predictability, and predictability is low entropy. Every n-gram model, every phone keyboard that finishes your word, is a grandchild of that hand-counted page of Pushkin.

If the letters were independent, the tally would have said so

Markov counted pairs, and I want you to see how little machinery he needed to catch the language out. Take his share of vowels, square it, multiply by the number of adjacent pairs on the page: that is how many vowel-vowel pairs a coin with the same bias would have thrown. Then look at what he actually tallied — several times fewer. Not one formula beyond a multiplication, no entropy anywhere in sight, and the dependence is already lying on the table.

That is the shape of every honest measurement in this book: first a null model — as if independent — and then a count that refuses to match it. The gauge does not discover structure; it puts a price on structure the tallies have already found. Had the two numbers agreed, no amount of cleverness with logarithms would have conjured a memory into a text that has none.

Carry the habit into search. Before you compute anything information-theoretic over an index, write down what the count would be if the two things were independent — that null is what your quantity is measured against, whether you named it or not. A few beats from now, mutual information turns out to be exactly the gap between what happened and as if independent, converted into bits. The counts come first. The bits only give the gap a name.

The same −Σ p log p, long before the wire

Shannon did not invent the shape of my gauge; he found it already cast. In the physics of heat, Boltzmann priced the disorder of a state by the logarithm of the number of ways that state could be arranged — and when all those ways are equally likely, that is Hartley's form, the flat ceiling of my dial. Gibbs then wrote the general case, with the states carrying unequal probabilities: \(S = -k\sum_i p_i \ln p_i\). Set the constant to one, take the log in base two, and you are holding \(H\). Not a poetic resemblance — the same functional, discovered in a furnace instead of on a wire.

And since I am about to demolish somebody else's legend, let me start with the one in my own pocket. \(S = k \log W\) in exactly that notation, with the constant standing inside it, is Planck's hand, not Boltzmann's: Boltzmann stated a proportionality and never introduced \(k\) — it was Planck who afterwards named the constant after him. The line is carved on Boltzmann's headstone all the same, cut long after his death. The rule I keep at this desk: a formula belongs to whoever wrote it, not to whoever it is named after.

Now the story you will certainly be told. Von Neumann, they say, advised Shannon to call the quantity entropy — physics has the word already, and besides nobody knows what entropy is, so you will win every argument. In print the quip surfaces only decades later, second-hand, with no contemporaneous record of the conversation: scientific folklore, not documented fact. Keep the two claims apart, because their warranties differ. The origin of the name is a legend. The identity of the formulas is a theorem, and you can check it in an evening.

Eleven years earlier: a contact is a Boolean variable

Before he measured a message, he did the same trick on a switch. At twenty-one, working at MIT on an early mechanical computer, he noticed that its relays had exactly two states — closed and open — and that the algebra of logic he had met in a philosophy course had exactly two values, true and false. In the same years Shannon, Nakashima in Japan and Shestakov in the USSR put the two facts side by side independently. His 1937 master's thesis, A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits, proved they are the same object: two contacts in series compute \(A \cdot B\), two in parallel compute \(A + B\), and therefore any logical expression can be built out of switches.

The payoff runs the other way too, and that is the part engineers cared about. If a circuit is an expression, then simplifying the expression simplifies the hardware: \(A + A\cdot B = A\) deletes an entire branch, contacts and all, and no intuition about wiring is required to see it. A generation of telephone exchanges was designed on that sentence. Howard Gardner would later call the thesis "possibly the most important, and also the most famous, master's thesis of the century".

Keep the method rather than the anecdote, because you are about to watch it a second time. Take a physical thing — a contact clicking, a crackle on a wire — and hunt for the mathematics it is not merely like but identical to. In 1937 the mathematics was Boolean algebra and the physical thing was a relay. In 1948 the mathematics will be probability and the physical thing will be a message. The scale differs; the move does not.

The bit: a needle for surprise

In 1948, at my bench in Bell Labs, a thin sheaf of pages crosses the room — Claude Shannon's A Mathematical Theory of Communication. On the first pages he names the unit I will spend my career watching: the bit. He does not even claim the word; in a footnote he hands it to a colleague down the hall, John Tukey, who folded binary digit into one short syllable. One bit is one clean yes/no — the smallest question the wire can carry, one flick of my needle.

But the needle does not measure the message. It measures surprise. Shannon writes the self-information of an outcome as \(I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)\), and I mount that formula straight onto the gauge. A thing that was certain — \(p=1\) — arrives worth \(-\log_2 1 = 0\) bits: the needle never twitches, because I already knew. A fair coin, \(p=\tfrac12\), lands at exactly \(1\) bit. And a one-in-a-million event, \(p=10^{-6}\), throws the needle to \(-\log_2 10^{-6}\approx 20\) bits — a hard spike I feel in my wrist.

That minus sign is the whole trick: the rarer the symbol, the larger the surprise. Common things — the 'e' that ends a thousand words — barely move me. Rare things carry the news. Information, Shannon is telling me, is not what you say but how much you couldn't have guessed. Hold onto this one atom — surprise, in bits — because in the next beat I average it over a whole language, and it earns a new name: entropy.

Surprise is −log₂ p: a rare outcome is TALL, a common one is short. On this biased coin heads costs 2 bits of surprise, tails only 0.415 — the two bar lengths are the two self-informations, nothing averaged yet.

But you rarely pay the rare price. Weight each bar by how often its outcome actually happens: the tall rare one shrinks by ×p, the short common one by ×(1−p). Surprise you almost never meet barely counts.

Stack the two weighted contributions end to end: their sum IS the entropy H(p), the average surprise per toss. Draw it for every p and you get a cap that peaks at exactly 1 bit for the fair coin, p = 0.5 — maximum ignorance.

Lock the needle at p = 0.25: the coin's entropy is H = 0.8113 bits, and the red floor line is welded onto the dial right there. Below 1 bit means the skew is exploitable — yet a code for a single toss still spends a whole bit; 0.8113 is the floor that codes over blocks of tosses approach.

Now bolt on a WRONG model: q insists the coin is fair. The needle leaves the floor and reads the cross-entropy H(p,q) = 1 bit; the hatched wedge between floor and needle is the KL tax, 0.1887 bits. In perplexity that is 2 against a floor of 1.7548 — a COUNT of equally likely options, not bits; the «1.75 bits» of act 4 is a different number.

Three demands, and the logarithm has no choice

Hartley gave me one reason for the logarithm: information has to add up. Here is the sharper statement. Write down three demands and the logarithm is the only function that can meet them. One — a certainty tells you nothing, \(I(1)=0\). Two — the rarer the outcome the more it tells you, so \(I\) falls as \(p\) rises. Three — independent messages add, \(I(pq) = I(p) + I(q)\). The third is the killer: the only continuous function that turns a product into a sum is the logarithm, and that is a classical result about functional equations, not a preference in notation.

Everything left over is a choice of ruler. The minus sign is there to make a positive quantity out of the logarithm of a number below one. The base is the unit: two gives bits, the natural logarithm gives nats, ten gives an older unit carrying Hartley's own name. Converting between them multiplies by a constant and changes nothing whatever about the source. Hold on to that when the perplexity trap arrives, because that trap is nothing but a base read off the wrong ruler.

And notice which of the three a search engine leans on hardest. Every time a ranker adds the IDF of one query term to the IDF of another, it is invoking additivity — asserting that the two terms are independent evidence. Words in real documents are no such thing: «New» and «York» arrive together. The functional form is forced; the licence to sum is not. That is the crack the objections to the IDF bridge come through, and it was here in the axioms from the start.

Entropy — the surprise I should expect, in bits

One symbol's surprise I already have on the gauge: \( I(x) = -\log_2 p(x) \) — a sure thing barely twitches the needle, a rare one flings it up. But over a whole night at the key, what I care about is not any single jolt; it is the average jolt. Weight each symbol's surprise by how often it truly comes, add them up, and you have the number Shannon named entropy: \( H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x) \), bits per symbol. Expected surprise — the steady reading the needle settles to.

There is a homelier way to read that same number. Entropy is the minimum average number of yes/no questions I must ask to pin down the next symbol — the fewest dots and dashes a perfect code could spend, on average, per letter. A fair coin: one honest question, «heads?», and I know — 1 bit. A fair die needs more: \( \log_2 6 \approx 2.585 \) bits, because six equally-likely faces can't be cornered in two questions, but on average in a hair under three. No cleverness slips beneath that figure; it is the floor.

And the floor stands highest exactly where I know least. A source is hardest to predict — costs the most bits — precisely when every symbol is equally likely: that uniform case is maximum entropy. Tilt the odds even a little — a coin weighted three-to-one — and the average surprise drops below a bit (to about 0.81), because now the needle rests low most of the night and only jumps on the rare face. Every skew is a discount. Predictability is saved bits.

The ceiling, and the exact distance down to the needle

I have twice told you that the flat source is the loudest — that the needle rests highest when every symbol is equally likely — and twice I let it pass as an observation. It is not an observation, it is an identity, and it fits on one line. Write the entropy of a source over \(n\) symbols as \(H(p) = \log_2 n - \sum_x p(x)\log_2\!\big(n\,p(x)\big)\). The first piece is the flat ceiling — the reading of a source where nothing can be guessed. The second is a sum that is never negative and is zero only when every \(p(x)\) equals \(1/n\). So the ceiling is proved and the drop below it is priced, by the same line.

And that second piece is not a stranger. It is the excess-bits functional that arrives in act five with a bill attached — the price of holding a wrong list of odds — here measured against the flattest list there is. Redundancy is that price. Structure in a language is not a vague quality; it is exactly how far the language's own odds have moved away from the flat ones, counted in bits.

Which settles a question the chapter is about to ask three times. Bits per letter cannot be compared across alphabets, because the ceiling \(\log_2 n\) is set by the alphabet before a single word is spoken — count more letters and you have raised the ceiling by arithmetic alone. Redundancy can be compared, because it is the drop expressed as a share of that ceiling, and the share divides the alphabet back out. When you hear that one tongue is more redundant than another, ask which two ceilings were used; when you hear that one carries fewer bits per letter, you have heard nothing about the tongue at all.

The gauge cannot see the labels

Look at what I actually feed the formula. Not letters, not words, not documents — a list of chances. Swap the labels around, hand the odds of E to Q and the odds of Q to E, and the needle does not move by a hair. Entropy is blind to renaming: it knows how the chances are spread and it will never know what they are about. That is not a limitation I am apologising for; it is the property that makes the quantity general enough to measure a coin, a poem and a postings list with one instrument.

But it is also the hidden mechanism under three separate warnings this book gives you, and they are one warning wearing three coats. An optimal code's average length depends only on the list of chances, so which symbol gets which code word is free — two code books can differ everywhere and bill the same. The spread of a ranking says how confidently the engine ordered the documents and can say nothing about whether the right one is in there, because the needle never learned which document was right. And rarity is a property of the list alone, so a rare term is not a useful term — the gauge has no opinion about usefulness and cannot acquire one.

So keep the habit that follows, because it is the whole discipline of this chapter in one question: before you compute an \(H\), say out loud what the event space is. That choice — which outcomes count as distinct, over which population, at which alphabet — is the only place meaning ever enters the number. Everything after it is arithmetic that cannot tell you whether you asked the right question, and will produce a perfectly respectable figure either way.

A crooked coin, by hand: less than a bit

At the key I get a crooked coin — heads comes up only one time in four, tails the other three. My gauge reads surprise in bits, and it already behaves differently for the two faces: heads is the rare event, so it kicks the needle high; tails is what I half-expect, so it barely stirs. The honest question of the telegraph room is this — coded well, how many bits does one flip of this coin really cost me on the wire?

The surprise of each face is its self-information \(I = -\log_2 p\). Heads: \(-\log_2 0.25 = 2\) bits — a real jolt, worth two yes/no questions. Tails: \(-\log_2 0.75 \approx 0.415\) bit — I nearly knew it was coming. Entropy is the average surprise, each face weighted by how often it shows: \(H = 0.25\cdot 2 + 0.75\cdot 0.415 = 0.5 + 0.311 = \mathbf{0.811}\) bit — less than one full bit.

A fair coin would cost a whole bit each flip; this one averages \(0.811\). That missing \(0.189\) bit is not rounding — it is money on the wire. Over a thousand flips a clever code sends the outcomes in about 811 bits, not 1000 — nearly a fifth saved, simply because the coin leans. The lean is the structure, and structure is compressibility: wherever the world tilts away from equal odds, the red entropy floor drops below one bit. And it is a floor — I can creep down toward \(811\) bits, but no code, however cunning, ever slips under it.

Turn the gauge on English itself — and the needle steps down from 4.76 bits, layer by layer, toward a floor it can never cross

In 1951 Shannon set a second paper on my desk — Prediction and Entropy of Printed English — and I turn the gauge on the language itself. Start at the ceiling, pure uniformity: 27 symbols, twenty-six letters and a space. If every one were equally likely the needle would rest at \(F_0 = \log_2 27 \approx 4.76\) bits a letter — the top of the dial, a make-believe English where nothing can be guessed ahead of the key.

But English is not uniform. Let the letter frequencies in — E pours through, Q barely trickles — and the needle already drops to \(F_1 \approx 4.03\) bits. Now let pairs speak (a Q all but hands you its U), then whole words, and it keeps falling, toward \(\approx 2\) bits a letter. Each layer of structure the language already carries, \(F_n\), is one more step down a staircase: regularity the tongue gives away for free.

And here is the part I love at the reading desk: I never opened a book of statistics. A fluent reader already is a trained model of English — decades of print baked into instinct. So I weigh the language through the person: watch how little a native speaker is surprised, and I have measured the entropy of English itself. The red floor slides down with every layer, yet never touches zero — a living tongue always keeps some surprise, or it would carry nothing at all.

Start flat. Twenty-seven equally likely symbols — 26 letters plus the space — and every symbol costs the same. That equal cost IS the ceiling F₀: the most any alphabet of this size can ever charge you per symbol. There is nothing here to compress.

Now pour in the real English letter frequencies. The bars fan out — «e» towers, the tail flattens toward zero — and the floor H drops away from the ceiling H₀ without moving it. Only the top eight letters are labelled: putting all 26 on one row turns them into a smear.

Look at the bar chart: it did not move. The next drop, from F₁ to F₂, is bought by CONTEXT — knowing the previous letter — not by frequency. «q» is rare, yet after «q» the letter that follows is almost free.

Switch to Russian: 33 letters, so the CEILING rises before anything else happens. H is higher here than in English — and that means nothing on its own. A different alphabet is a different scale; only the redundancy R = 1 − H/H₀ compares, and it says 11.2 % against 11.5 %: the two languages waste almost exactly as much.

The whole staircase at once. Each extra letter of context digs the floor deeper — F₀ from frequency alone, then F₁, F₂, F₃ — and with a hundred letters of context a human predictor lands in the shaded band, not at zero. That band is the target every language model is still walking down toward.

Bits per symbol is not bits per letter

There is a trap inside the staircase I have just walked down, and it is worth more to you than the steps. My alphabet counts the space between words as a symbol of its own. Up at the ceiling, where everything is equally likely, that extra symbol raises the ceiling — one more equally likely thing to choose from. But the moment real frequencies enter, the space turns out to be the commonest symbol in English, commoner than E, and it is almost perfectly predictable — so it drags the average down. The same mark, acting in opposite directions at the two ends of the ladder.

Which means the two columns of that ladder are not in the same unit at all. A hundred letters of English are appreciably more than a hundred symbols, once you count the gaps between the words. Shannon supplies the conversion himself: the ratio of the average word length without its space to the length with it. Multiply, and the rows agree to the digit; forget to, and you will «discover» a discrepancy that is nothing but a change of ruler.

So take the lifelong habit out of this beat: bits per symbol means nothing until you have said what a symbol is. Ask it of every bits-per-character in a paper, every perplexity per token, every bits-per-document-id in your own index. The alphabet first, the number second. A couple of beats from now the same question decides whether a modern compressor really did beat me — and it is the same question that made my two columns look contradictory when they were merely differently ruled.

The staircase is a limit, not a measurement

Walk the staircase again and notice that every step is a different quantity. \(F_0\) prices letters as if all were equally likely. \(F_1\) prices them by their true frequencies but as if each arrived alone. \(F_2\) allows each letter to lean on the one before it, and no further. Each rung is the entropy of a model of English — a rather crude model, honestly described — and not the entropy of English. I have been reading models off my dial all along and calling out the name of the language.

The rungs fall, and they must: letting a letter lean on more of its past is conditioning, and conditioning can never raise entropy — the inequality from the conditional-entropy beat, applied all the way down the ladder. So the sequence decreases and it cannot pass below zero, and a decreasing sequence with a floor under it has a limit. That limit is what «the entropy of English» means: not a rung, but the number the rungs are walking toward. No measurement ever lands on it, and none ever will; each one stands honestly above it.

Two things follow, and I want you carrying both. First, whenever anybody announces the entropy of a language, they have announced a rung — their model's reading — and named it after the tongue; read it as an upper bound and ask which model produced it. Second, this shape is not a coincidence with the red line of the next act: the limit the staircase approaches and the floor no code can pierce are the same number seen from two sides, one from above by prediction, one from above by coding. Approach forever, arrive never.

Why the counting had to stop, and a reader took over

Why did I put down the tally sheets and sit a person in front of a hidden page? Arithmetic, and nothing more romantic. To estimate a rung by counting, I need a cell for every context I am conditioning on: an alphabet of \(S\) symbols and a context of \(n\) of them wants \(S^{n}\) cells, and each further letter of memory multiplies the table by \(S\) again. Two or three letters of context and the table is already larger than the text I am measuring.

The trouble is worse than shelf space, though, and this is the part that decides the method. The corpus stays the size it is while the number of cells explodes, so the average count per cell collapses; most contexts are seen once or never. And a context seen once does not produce a noisy estimate — it produces a confident one, declaring that its single continuation happens always and everything else never. Confidence like that reads as no surprise at all, so the estimated entropy drops. My staircase would keep falling beautifully for a reason that has nothing to do with English.

A fluent reader steps around the whole problem. Decades of print have left in that head a model with a very long memory, and nobody ever had to store it as a table. That is the real trick of the guessing game: not to count better, but to borrow an estimator that already exists and was paid for by somebody's childhood. And the modern answer is the third road — keep the long context, refuse the table, and let a model share what it learns across contexts instead of giving each its own cell. Smoothing began that road, and the machines at the end of this chapter finished it.

Every H in this chapter came out of a tally

Stop the needle for a moment and look at where its readings come from. Not one entropy in this chapter was handed to me by a source. Every single one was computed from counts — Markov's two bins, a table of letter frequencies, the gaps in an index — by the obvious recipe: take the observed shares as if they were the probabilities, and put them in the formula. That recipe is an estimate, not a measurement, and its error has a direction you can predict before you run it.

The direction is down. A finite sample never shows a source at its full variety: symbols that exist but did not turn up are handed a probability of zero and contribute nothing at all, and the shares that did turn up are lumpier than the truth simply because a small tally cannot be smooth. Lumpier means less surprise, and less surprise means a lower reading. So a short text always looks more predictable than the language it was drawn from — and the shorter the text against the size of the alphabet, the thicker the flattery.

There is a second turn of the screw, and it catches the redundancy claims. The ceiling is usually computed on the observed alphabet as well, so both ends of «how far below the ceiling» come out of the same small sample, and the comparison is partly talking to itself. The discipline is cheap and I will hold myself to it: say how many observations stand behind each cell, and grow suspicious the moment the alphabet is large next to the corpus. A measured entropy is a number with an error bar — and the most famous ones in this chapter, mine among them, were printed without one.

Four letters in five, I already knew

1951. Shannon returns to the same question — and this time answers it with a parlour game. Sit a native reader down, hide a page, and reveal it one letter at a time. At each step the reader must guess the next letter, guessing again and again until they hit it, and I write down only one thing — how many tries it took. Those tallies are the whole experiment: a hit on the first try means the language told the reader nothing new; a long hunt means real surprise.

Play out a full line and the pattern is startling. In one of Shannon's demonstrations, out of 102 symbols the subject guessed right 79 times on the first try — more than three letters in four — and the needle on my bits gauge barely twitches: the reader already knew them from the words and grammar that came before. The famous “four in five” is a different measurement: Shannon's Table I, a hundred letters of context, 80 right out of 100. Only the odd surprising letter kicks the needle up. Most of a letter, it turns out, is simply not news.

Shannon squeezes those guess counts into a bound — the harder the guessing, the more bits must really be there. With a hundred letters of context, printed English lands between about 0.6 and 1.3 bits per character: call it one bit, down from the \(4.76\) a blind, uniform 27-symbol alphabet would cost. So roughly three-quarters of every letter is redundant — predictable, already spent. The red line on my gauge, the true entropy of English, sits down near one bit, and no clever coding will ever push a letter below it.

One letter of memory is worth a quarter of a bit — measured on Markov's own tally sheet

For eight pages now I have been saying that context removes surprise, and I have not once written down the quantity that says so. Here it is. If \(X\) is the letter I am about to receive and \(Y\) the one already on the tape, the surprise that survives is the conditional entropy \(H(X \mid Y)\): the average of the entropies I would feel inside each possible context, weighted by how often that context turns up. Two facts come with it, and both are worth more than the formula. First the chain rule, \(H(X,Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y)\) — a message's cost adds up letter by letter, which is exactly why a per-symbol price makes sense at all. Second, \(H(X \mid Y) \le H(Y)\), with equality only when the two are independent: knowing the last letter can never cost me bits, only save them.

And I can put a number on it without leaving 1913, because Markov left his counts. Of his twenty thousand letters of Onegin, 8638 were vowels — so a letter arriving blind is very nearly a fair coin, \(H = 0.9866\) bit. Now condition. Of those vowels, only 1104 were followed by another vowel; of the 11 362 consonants, 7534 were followed by a vowel. That is a violent asymmetry, and the gauge feels it: after a vowel the needle reads 0.5514 bit, after a consonant 0.9218, and the weighted average is \(H(X \mid Y) = 0.7618\) bit.

\(H(X) = 0.9866\) · \(H(X \mid Y) = 0.4319\cdot 0.5514 + 0.5681\cdot 0.9218 = 0.7618\) ⇒ the context is worth \(0.9866 - 0.7618 = \mathbf{0.2247}\) bit, a 22.8 % cut. Read from the other side the same number is the mutual information \(I(X;Y)\): how many bits the previous letter tells you about the next one.

A quarter of a bit, for one letter of memory, on a poem written a century before anyone had a word for it. Notice what the instrument did not need: it does not know Russian, it does not know verse, it does not know that these marks are language at all. It knows how often things follow other things. Everything the rest of this book calls a language model is that same trade, run deeper — and every extra letter of context you give it buys another slice of that 0.2247.

The same number, read from the other side

I let that name slip once already; now let me pay for it properly. Mutual information is \(I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\): the bits the previous letter tells you about the next one. There is no new arithmetic here at all — it is the drop I measured on Markov's own counts, renamed. What changes is the side you read it from: not «how much surprise did the context remove» but «how much does one symbol say about its neighbour».

Three readings are worth saying out loud. Symmetry: what the past knows about the future is exactly what the future knows about the past — the quantity does not care which side you condition on. Non-negativity, with zero if and only if the two are independent — so «conditioning cannot raise entropy» and «information is never negative» are one statement in two coats. And \(I(X;Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)\): mutual information is itself a KL, between what actually happened and as if independent. The null model of the Markov beat, finally priced in bits.

This is the workhorse of the search half of the chapter: how many bits does a feature carry about relevance? Feature selection, term association, rescoring — one count underneath all of them. Two cautions travel with it. It is an average, and the pointwise quantity standing under it has no theorem behind it — the very objection waiting for IDF a few beats from now. And it measures dependence, not usefulness: a term perfectly correlated with a quirk of your crawler is, by this count, as informative as a term correlated with the answer.

The same gauge, in Russian

The book you are holding speaks three languages, so it would be a poor instrument that only worked in one. Point the gauge at Russian and the whole staircase repeats: a ceiling where every letter is equally likely, a step down as the real letter frequencies come in, then digrams, then trigrams. Russian starts higher than English for a reason that has nothing to do with Russian — its alphabet simply has more letters, so \(\log_2\) of the alphabet size stands higher before a single word has been measured.

Here is the turn, and it decides how languages may be compared at all. Bits per letter are not comparable across alphabets; redundancy is. Measure how far a language sits below its own ceiling, as a share rather than a count, and Russian and English become hard to tell apart. Whoever announces that Russian is the more redundant language has compared two rulers and reported the difference as a fact about speech. And read the provenance while you are there: the classic Russian ladder was measured on a telegraph alphabet that merges a couple of letters, so its ceiling belongs to that alphabet and not to the one taught in school.

And now an honest gap, because this whole chapter is built on saying what is backed by what. Shannon's guessing game was never run on Russian. The nearest evidence is another Cyrillic language: a recent study sat volunteers down with Ukrainian text and played the same game, and the redundancy came out close to English — the authors' own word is «similar». So the fair claim is that the instrument carries across languages while the number does not. Which is the lesson your own pipeline will hand you the day you point an index tuned on English at a Russian collection — L19 is where we go and do exactly that.

Why a column of guess numbers bounds the entropy

The game leaves me with a column of small whole numbers — how many tries each letter cost. I asked you to accept that Shannon squeezes a bound out of it, and that was a black box on my desk. Open it. The column is not a summary of the page: it is the page, re-coded. Put the same reader at the far end of the wire and the two of us can rebuild the text from the numbers alone — he asks the reader for a first guess and takes it when my number says one, otherwise asks for a second, and so on down. Nothing was thrown away, so the stream of ranks and the stream of letters carry exactly the same information.

Now the upper half of the bracket costs one more sentence. Price the ranks by their own frequencies alone, ignoring how one rank leans on the last, and you have a working code for the page — so its cost stands above the truth, because ignoring dependence can only lose bits and never save them. That is the same inequality I have been leaning on all chapter, in its third costume. The lower half is the subtler one: it leans on the fact that a rational guesser must offer its guesses in order of decreasing probability, which forces the shape of the rank frequencies in a way no arbitrary column could imitate.

And Shannon marked the seam himself, which is why I hand it to you rather than the bracket alone. The lower end is proved for an ideal predictor, while the column was produced by a person at a kitchen table. So the two ends are not two measurements of one thing — one is a theorem and the other is a human being. Keep the habit that follows: when a quantity arrives as a range, ask what each end is made of before you split the difference.

Context helps on average — not in every context

I leaned hard on one inequality: knowing the previous letter cannot cost me bits, only save them. Read the definition again before you carry that away too confidently. \(H(X \mid Y)\) is a weighted average of the surprise left inside each separate context, and an average can fall while some of its terms rise. There are contexts after which the next letter is genuinely less predictable than it was with no context at all — a letter that opens a door rather than closing one. The theorem does not forbid them. It only says the frequent contexts more than pay for the rare ones.

Two things follow, and they are the same shape as the sharpest objection in this whole chapter. The staircase falls step by step because every step is an average over the whole corpus — it is a promise about the source, never about your sentence. And a claim about one item — this query is easy, it has context — does not follow from an average-shaped theorem, exactly as the pointwise \(-\log p\) does not inherit the coding theorem that the average \(H\) is bought with. Averages are results; the individual terms under them are intuitions.

In practice this is the cheapest thing to check and almost nobody checks it. A model given more context can post a lower loss over the corpus while being worse on a whole class of inputs, and the mean will never show you: the class is small, the mean is not. So if what you care about lives in the tail — rare queries, unusual documents, the one language your corpus barely contains — read the distribution of the per-item bills, not their average. The gauge measures your population; it was never asked about the case in front of you.

The same I, one war later: what an unbreakable cipher is

The quantity you just computed on Onegin's letters has a second life, and it is not a metaphor. Write \(M\) for the message and \(C\) for the ciphertext the adversary intercepts. A cipher is perfectly secret exactly when \(I(M;C) = H(M) - H(M \mid C) = 0\): after seeing \(C\), the adversary knows precisely as much about \(M\) as before seeing it. Note how strong that is. It does not say the adversary lacks the computer, or the century, to break it. It says there is nothing in the ciphertext to work on.

Correct decryption together with perfect secrecy forces \(H(K)\ge H(M)\). This necessary entropy bound is not sufficient for secrecy and does not automatically compare raw string lengths. Enough key entropy can coexist with a cipher that reveals part of the message. The one-time pad achieves perfect secrecy using an independent uniformly random key of the message's bit length, used once. Shannon wrote the classified memorandum in 1945 and published Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems in 1949.

The reason it belongs in a search course is the shape of the argument, not the cryptography. One quantity — expected surprise — fixed the floor under compression, the ceiling on a channel, and now the ceiling on secrecy. When you later measure how much a query log, an embedding, or a returned snippet leaks about the document behind it, you will be computing this same \(I\), and zero will mean independence from that document. Whether such privacy is compatible with useful search depends on what is disclosed and which information must remain private.

The floor no code can cross

There is a red line painted across the floor of my gauge, and Mr. Shannon has just told me why the needle can never break through it. Give me any source — letters clacking off the wire, symbols each with their own frequency — and there is a single number, its entropy \(H\), below which no lossless code can ever squeeze the average message. You may draw as close to it as your cleverness allows; you may never once go under. That is the Source Coding Theorem (Shannon 1948), and on my meter it is the floor the needle rests against but cannot pierce.

Let me make it real at the key. Take four symbols with tidy halving odds — \(A\) half the time, \(B\) a quarter, \(C\) and \(D\) an eighth each. Their entropy comes to exactly \(H=1.75\) bits per symbol. Now I hand frequent \(A\) the shortest tap, \(0\); then \(B\to 10\), \(C\to 110\), \(D\to 111\) — a prefix code, no codeword the beginning of another. Average those lengths against the odds and it lands on \(1.75\): not near the floor, but on it. Short codes for the common, long for the rare — Morse's old instinct at his type-cases, now proven optimal to the last bit.

And this is why English folds the way it does. A native reader carries only about one bit of true surprise per letter; the rest is redundancy — spelled-out habit the language could have left unsaid. So a good compressor, chasing that floor, packs printed English down to roughly a quarter of its bulk: about \(4{:}1\), and no better. Every ZIP, every archive on the wire is a coder creeping toward Shannon's red line. None has ever crossed it. None ever will.

Why a floor exists at all: the shelf is exactly one unit long

I told you no code can beat \(H\) and asked you to take it on trust. The reason fits on a shelf. In a prefix-free code — where no codeword is the beginning of another — a codeword of length \(l\) eats \(2^{-l}\) of a shelf whose whole length is one: one bit takes half the shelf, two bits a quarter, three an eighth. Kraft's inequality says the lengths \(l_1,\dots,l_n\) are realisable by a prefix-free code exactly when \(\sum_i 2^{-l_i} \le 1\) — and it runs in both directions, necessary and sufficient.

Now the floor arrives in one move. Take the ideal lengths, each symbol paid its own surprise: \(l_i = -\log_2 p_i\). Then \(\sum_i 2^{-l_i} = \sum_i p_i = 1\) — the shelf is exactly full, not a finger of room left over. Shorten any single codeword and the sum passes one, so a prefix-free code with those lengths does not exist. That is \(\bar L \ge H\); and notice the grammar of the claim — not «hard», not «nobody has managed it yet», but impossible.

What the shelf teaches beyond the theorem is worth more than the theorem. A short codeword is expensive, and somebody else pays for it. Every scheme that hands something a shorter name — a document id in your index, a token in your vocabulary, a bucket in a hash — is spending shelf that has to be taken off another word's length. Compression is not the art of finding shorter names. It is the art of deciding who gets them, and the shelf is the reason that decision has a price at all.

Every code is a model, and the overpayment has a name

The shelf told me why the average length can never fall under \(H\). Now watch the same algebra tell me how much more it pays. Take any prefix-free code with lengths \(l_i\) and read those lengths backwards, as if each were a self-information: the numbers \(2^{-l_i}\) are all positive and, by Kraft, sum to at most one, so once normalised they are a probability distribution. Call it the distribution the code believes in. Subtract the entropy from the average length and the difference splits, exactly, into two non-negative pieces: how far the code's beliefs sit from the source's true odds, and how much of the shelf the code left standing empty.

Both pieces vanish in one case only, and it is precisely the tidy source of the last beat: the code is complete, wasting no shelf, and its implied odds are the true ones, because the true odds happened to be clean powers of two. That is the whole reason the tidy code landed on the floor and the untidy one hovered above it. The overpayment was never «rounding» in any loose sense — it is a wrong price list, measured by the very same excess-bits quantity that will price a wrong language model two acts from now.

So take the sentence out of the room with you: choosing codeword lengths is choosing a distribution. Every scheme in your index that hands something a short name — variable-byte on the gaps, a subword vocabulary, a hash bucket, an identifier assignment — has silently declared what it believes is frequent, and it pays for its mistaken beliefs in this currency and no other. There is no such thing as a code without a model. There are only codes whose model nobody bothered to write down.

Why insisting on prefix-free costs nothing

The shelf argument has a hole in it and I would rather you found it from me. Everything I proved was proved about prefix-free codes — codes in which no word begins another, so the receiver can stop the moment a word ends. But that is a convenience I chose, not a law of the world. A sceptic can ask the obvious question: what if I give up instant reading, allow the decoder to look ahead over several words, and buy shorter code words with the slack? If that worked, my floor would be a floor under my own taste rather than under coding.

It does not work, and the reason is worth more than the theorem. Ask only that the stream be uniquely decodable — that no two different messages ever produce the same string of bits — and the very same shelf sum still cannot pass one. The one-line intuition: raise that sum to the power \(n\) and every term you get is one message of \(n\) symbols, taking up its own share of the shelf; if the sum were greater than one, that count would grow past the number of distinct bit strings of the same length, and two messages would have to collide. Unique decodability alone buys the constraint. Prefix-freeness never entered.

So the two families cost exactly the same, and for any set of lengths a lookahead code can realise there is a prefix code with those same lengths. You may as well take the one you can decode symbol by symbol with no buffer at all — which is why every code in this chapter is prefix-free, and why the variable-length integers in your index are too. And keep the general shape of the argument: the floor was never a consequence of an engineering restriction. It falls out of the single requirement that the message can be read back. Anything that must be recoverable pays the shelf.

The code that lands exactly on the floor

Four symbols pour down my wire with fixed odds — A half the time, B a quarter, C and D an eighth each. Yesterday I proved this source's entropy: \(H=1.75\) bits per symbol, the red line on my gauge. Now the real question at the key — can I actually send it that cheaply? Give each symbol a codeword of noughts and ones; the floor swears no code averages under \(1.75\) bits. Let me build one that reaches down and touches it.

The rule is the one Morse guessed by hand a century early — frequent symbol, short code — made exact: let each codeword be as long as the symbol's surprise. A is nearly a sure thing, \(I(A)=-\log_2\tfrac12=1\) bit, so one tap of the key: 0. B costs \(-\log_2\tfrac14=2\) bits: 10. C and D cost 3 bits each: 110, 111. And notice — no codeword begins another (it is prefix-free), so I send them shoulder to shoulder with no spaces, 0·10·110·111, and the man at the far end splits the stream back apart without a single comma.

Now weigh the wire. Average length \(=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=1.75\) bits per symbol — exactly the entropy. The needle rests dead on the red floor, not a hair above. It lands there because every length matched its own self-information, the dyadic odds falling on clean powers of two. This is Shannon's source-coding theorem you can hold in your hand: \(H\) is not a bound I merely creep toward — here I touch it, and I can never, ever slip beneath.

The tidy code that lands exactly on the floor is the exception — and the way past it is to stop sending letters one at a time

The four-symbol source I just coded was a gift. Its probabilities were a half, a quarter, an eighth, an eighth — every ideal length \(-\log_2 p\) came out a whole number, so the code could pay exactly what each symbol was worth and the average landed on the floor to the digit. Real sources are not so polite. Take one letter off the shelf of English: E at 0.35, T at 0.25, A at 0.20, O at 0.12, I at 0.08. The floor is \(H = 2.1531\) bits. Build the optimal code — merge the two rarest, put the parent back in the queue, repeat — and it pays \(\bar L = 2.2000\).

The overpay is \(0.0469\) bit, 2.2 %, and the reason is in one row of the table: E should cost \(1.5146\) bits and a codeword can only be 1 bit or 2. Rounding up the cheap symbol is what the whole gap is made of. Gallager's 1978 bound says this can never exceed \(P_1 + 0.086 = 0.436\) bit here — we land at a twentieth of that.

So how does anyone reach the floor? By refusing the question as posed. Nobody says a codeword must correspond to one symbol. Tie the biased coin's flips into bundles and code the bundle: one flip costs a full bit and always will, but pairs cost \(0.8438\) bits per flip, triples \(0.8229\), and blocks of eight \(0.8158\) — against a floor of \(0.8113\). That descent is the source coding theorem seen with the eyes: \(H \le \bar L_n/n < H + 1/n\). You never arrive; you get as close as you are willing to pay for.

And the paying is the catch. Blocks of eight already mean a codebook of 256 entries, and the next few hundredths cost exponentially more. That is the whole commercial argument for arithmetic coding, which behaves like an infinitely long block without ever building the book — and it is why the last decade of language models, which price a token against everything before it, are compressors in everything but name.

Five letters, five leaves, sorted by probability: I 0.08, O 0.12, A 0.20, T 0.25, E 0.35. The red rule on the floor is the entropy H = 2.1531 bits — no prefix code may pay less than that. Nothing has been merged yet.

Take the two lightest nodes — I 0.08 and O 0.12 — and hang them under one parent worth 0.20. The rarest letters go deepest, which is the whole idea: a long code for the rare letter, a short one for the common.

The parent goes back into the queue as an ordinary node — that is the whole algorithm. Now the two lightest are A 0.20 and the fresh node 0.20; they merge into 0.40.

Two merges close the tree: T 0.25 with E 0.35 gives 0.60, and the last pair 0.40 + 0.60 gives the root 1.00. Now walk down from the root writing 0 to the left and 1 to the right — E 11, T 10, A 00, O 011, I 010.

The bill: L̄ = 2.2000 bits against H = 2.1531 — a gap of 0.0469 bit, 2.2 %. Look at the table for why: E should pay 1.5146 bits and an integer code can only pay 2. Where the probabilities are dyadic (½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛) the ideal lengths are already whole numbers, so there is nothing to round up and L̄ = H = 1.75 exactly.

The floor under a source that remembers

Walk back over the last two acts and you will find a hole I stepped across twice. The floor was proved for a source that draws one symbol at a time with fixed chances, each draw forgetting the last. English is emphatically not that — the whole point of the Onegin tally was that the next letter leans on the one before it. So by what right does my red line stand under a language at all?

By the same move I used on the crooked coin: stop coding letters one at a time. Tie \(n\) letters into a block and treat the block as one symbol of a much bigger alphabet. Nothing in the proof objects — a block has chances like any other symbol — so the theorem applies to the block source unchanged, and the price per letter of the best block code is the entropy of a whole block divided by \(n\). Now let the blocks grow. Each extra letter of context can only lower the surprise of what follows, so that per-letter quantity falls, and being bounded below it must settle somewhere. That limit is the entropy rate — and it is the only thing the phrase the entropy of English can honestly mean.

Which retires a claim I made earlier without earning it: that the limit of the prediction staircase and the floor under every code are one number. They are, and now you can see the machinery instead of the assertion. A model that predicts \(n\) letters is a code for blocks of \(n\), and a block code is a model — the two staircases are the same sequence, walked from opposite ends. And take the practical residue with you: any floor quoted for your own data is only as good as the memory the model behind it was allowed. A floor computed as if your postings gaps were drawn independently is a floor under a poorer source than the one you actually have — which is exactly why, one act from here, a codec appears to break it.

Cross-Entropy — the Bill I Pay Above the Floor

At the key I never learn English's true odds outright — that red line on the dial, the source's own entropy \(H(p)\), stays behind glass. What I can build is a codebook \(q\): my best guess at how likely each next letter is. And when the real stream clatters in — letters arriving with their true frequencies \(p\), priced by my beliefs \(q\) — the bill the gauge reads, bits per symbol, is the cross-entropy \(H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\,\log_2 q(x)\).

Here is the iron fact, and it is the whole beat: the needle can sink toward the red line but never dip below it — \(H(p,q) \ge H(p)\). The sliver of overpayment, the bits I waste for guessing wrong, has a name of its own — the KL divergence \(D(p\|q) = H(p,q) - H(p) \ge 0\). Guess perfectly, \(q=p\), and the toll is zero: the needle rests exactly on the floor. Guess badly and you pay, letter after letter, for every mis-priced symbol.

So watch what training a language model really is. Turn the knob on \(q\) — nudge the odds, read the gauge, nudge again — driving cross-entropy down toward that red line, closing the KL gap to the true source. That is every language-model loss, drawn straight from the 1948 telegraph room. And perplexity is just this bill made friendly: \(2^{H(p,q)}\), the branching factor — how many equally-likely next letters my uncertainty is worth. One bit per letter means perplexity 2, a coin-flip at every step. The bits-per-character in every modern paper is Shannon's \(F_n\), now measured by a machine — a needle still creeping toward a floor it can never cross.

KL is a bill, not a distance

For two acts I have leaned on \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0\) without proving it, and the proof is one line that needs neither convexity nor Jensen. The logarithm never rises above its tangent at one: \(\ln x \le x - 1\). Put \(x = q_i/p_i\), weight each term by \(p_i\), and the right-hand side becomes \(\sum_i q_i - \sum_i p_i\) — both are distributions, both sum to one, so the whole thing collapses to zero. Equality only where the price list matches the source at every symbol. And note that the same line props up the compression floor of the previous act: one inequality holds up both floors.

Now the trap this beat is really about. It is tempting to read \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\) as a distance between distributions — bigger means further apart, and the order of the arguments is a convention. Take the crooked coin from earlier and the fair one, compute it one way round and then the other, and the two numbers are simply different. KL is neither symmetric nor obedient to the triangle inequality. It is not a metric. It is a bill.

And the order of the arguments is the entire meaning. \(D(p\|q)\) is what you overpay when the data really comes from \(p\) and you priced it with \(q\); reverse them and you have asked a different question about a different overpayment. This is exactly the forward-versus-reverse KL you will meet in training: keeping the data first smears a model across every mode of the source, putting the model first lets it collapse onto one of them. Swap the arguments and you have not made a rounding error — you have changed algorithms.

Perplexity: in whose base?

Perplexity is the friendly face of cross-entropy: raise a base to the power of the loss and read off a branching factor — how many equally likely continuations your uncertainty is worth. The catch hides in the words «a base». The definition is \(\mathrm{PPL} = b^{\,H_b}\), and the \(b\) of the power must be the \(b\) of the logarithm inside the loss. Papers and frameworks report the loss in nats — the standard cross-entropy call returns a natural logarithm — so their perplexity is \(e^{\text{loss}}\), not \(2^{\text{loss}}\).

This mistake is nasty because it is quiet. A nat is a bit multiplied by a constant, so exponentiating in the wrong base raises no exception and overflows nothing: it hands you a plausible number of the same order of magnitude, which then goes into a table and gets compared with somebody else's. Check the base of the loss before you exponentiate — that is the whole of the fix.

And a second incomparability lives under the same roof. Perplexity cannot be compared across tokenizers, nor across normalisations — per token, per word, per character. Two models may be ranked by perplexity only if they share both. That is the earlier act's question in a new costume: bits per what? perplexity per what? The formula is arithmetic; the meaning lives in the unit.

A model is a compressor, and the record is one record

The coding act handed me a code and read a distribution off it. Run the arrow the other way. Give me a model that, at every position, hands me odds for the next symbol, and I have a codebook that is rebuilt at every letter: pay \(-\log q\) for whatever actually arrives. Arithmetic coding does exactly that without ever assembling a table of codewords — it narrows an interval instead — which is how it escapes the whole-number codeword lengths that made my untidy code overpay.

So the bits a model spends on a text are its cross-entropy over that text, multiplied by the text's length; and a compressor's output size is a model's loss, wearing a different coat. There is no second experiment to run and no conversion to argue about. When a paper lowers a bits-per-character and when a compressor takes a benchmark record, the same quantity moved — which is why the two crowds keep breaking each other's numbers, and why the strongest compressor anyone has is a language model that was never written as one.

Two cautions travel with the identity, and both matter downstream. It holds symbol by symbol only if the far end can reproduce the model's state exactly, which is why a compressor must ship its model or the seed that grows it — and why a compression contest insists on counting the decompressor's own bulk. And the identity says nothing whatever about which alphabet the symbols came from: one model, one text, cut into bytes or into letters, gives two different bits-per-symbol. The bridge is exact. The units still have to be argued.

You never see p — and that is exactly why the bridge carries weight

Read the bill again and something should bother you: \(H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 q(x)\) is written in terms of \(p\), and \(p\) is the one thing behind the glass. If I needed the true chances of English to compute my own overpayment, the whole bridge would be decoration. I do not. The text is a sample drawn from \(p\); average \(-\log_2 q\) over the symbols that actually arrive and you have an honest estimate of the bill without ever writing \(p\) down. The source pays its own invoice by supplying data — that, and nothing more mystical, is why this quantity is measurable at all.

Read the same average from the other side and a second discipline falls out of it. Summing \(-\log q\) over the observed symbols is the negative log-likelihood of the text under my code book, divided by its length: minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, two traditions doing one arithmetic. And notice what stays unmeasured. The floor \(H(p)\) is a constant I never learn, so the bill compares two models on the same text and never tells me how far either sits above the truth — the upper-bound habit of this chapter, arriving now from the estimation side.

The catch lives in the word sample, and it is the practical half of the beat. Average the bill over the very text I tuned \(q\) on and I am not measuring the source at all: \(q\) may have memorised that particular page, and my needle flatters me exactly the way a short tally flattered me a few acts ago. So the bill must be read on text held back from the tuning — and this is the same accounting the compression contest enforces when it makes you count the size of the decompressor. A model that carries the answers inside itself has not paid for them; it has moved the payment somewhere the gauge was not looking.

Perplexity is a geometric mean, so you cannot average it

Perplexity raises the base to an average of logarithms, which in the original units is a geometric mean of one-over-the-chances. Say that out loud and the commonest handling error in the trade becomes obvious. Two texts, two perplexities, and someone averages them: the result is the perplexity of nothing at all. The only way to combine them is to go back to bits, average the per-symbol losses weighted by how many symbols each text contributed, and exponentiate once at the very end. An arithmetic mean of exponentials is an arithmetic mistake, and it is silent — the number it produces looks entirely plausible.

The second consequence is sharper: a geometric mean is destroyed by a single zero. One symbol your code book called impossible costs an infinite number of bits, and the whole corpus's bill goes to infinity no matter how gracefully the rest of it went. That is why smoothing is not cosmetics but the price of never issuing a zero — and it is the same move that, in the search act ahead, produces a term weight nobody added by hand. Refusing to say impossible is the one discipline shared by a language model and a retrieval model.

And one habit for reading the number, since its friendliness is the whole point of it. Perplexity is an exponential of the bill, so a difference that reads as modest in bits reads as dramatic in branching factor, and the same gap widens as the base grows. Compare models in bits when you want to know how much better one is; quote perplexity when you want a reader to feel it. Just never let the two units into the same table without saying which one moved.

My 1951 pencil and the neural net read the same gauge

I once measured the entropy of English the only way I could in 1951: by hand. A pencil, a slip of paper, and a native speaker guessing the next letter until it fell — my \(F_n\), the guessing game. Slow, human, faintly ridiculous. But the number that came out — about one bit per letter — was never a measure of the guesser. It measured the source. Anyone, or anything, that predicts the next symbol is reading the very same gauge.

In 1992 Brown and his colleagues at IBM turned the crank with a machine — a word-trigram model over a mountain of text — and reported an upper bound of 1.75 bits per character (Brown et al., 1992). Same quantity, a new hand on the dial: bits-per-character, "bpc" in the papers, is my \(F_n\) with the human guesser swapped for an algorithm. Today's neural nets press the needle further still, down toward the ~1.0-bit floor I first sketched with a pencil.

But watch the red line — the Source Coding Theorem holds it fast. A better model shoves the needle closer to the floor; it can never shove it under. That is the quiet marvel of this beat: my 1951 pencil and a modern transformer are reading the same dial, in the same unit, and both come to rest against the same wall. The gauge outlived the telegraph room — it always did belong to the language, not to me.

Incompatible «bits per character» scales

Now the comparison everybody makes, including people who ought to know better. A modern compressor squeezes a slab of Wikipedia to fewer bits per character than the figure I obtained with a pencil and a patient reader — therefore machines have beaten the human guesser and closed on the entropy of English. Two of those numbers are not on the same scale, and once you see why, you cannot unsee it.

The benchmark file is bytes of raw wiki markup: tags, links, tables, capitalisation, foreign text — an alphabet of a couple of hundred byte values, whose ceiling stands far above mine. Scrub the same data down to letters and a space and you get a second file whose alphabet is literally the one I measured; only its numbers belong in my column. And a compression contest is a third scale again, because it counts the size of the decompressor along with the archive. Three quantities, one name.

The delicious part is that the benchmark's own author warns in print against the comparison while the contest's own FAQ performs it — two official pages of one competition, standing in different places. So switch on the habit from the earlier beat: alphabet first, number second. A machine really has beaten the human guesser, and I will show you where — but that is shown on the scale where both were measured, not by standing two rulers side by side and reading off the shorter one.

Everything I have measured is an upper bound

Line up the readings of this whole chapter. A rung of the staircase. The upper end of the guessing bracket. A word-trigram machine's bits per character. A neural net's loss. The average length of the best code anyone has built for a source. Every single one is a number standing above \(H\), and every single one was produced by some model of the source. Not one of them is a measurement of \(H\) itself, and there is no procedure anywhere in this chapter that would produce one.

That is not a weakness of my instruments; it is what the theorems say. Any model yields a code, any code has an average length no shorter than the floor, and a better model simply lowers the number on the dial. So progress on this quantity will look the same forever: a descending sequence with nothing underneath it that anyone can touch. When somebody announces the entropy of English, what they have announced is their model's bill and named it after the language — and that is true whether the model sits in a machine or in a fluent reader's head. A better estimator moves the estimate. It never moves the floor.

Two rules fall out, and they are the ones I would want you to keep. First: a figure that appears to go under an established floor has not broken a theorem — it has discarded a model of the source that was wrong, exactly as the clustered postings did to the index's floor. Second: the human guesser belongs on the same list as the machines, so «a machine beat the human» is a race between two estimators and says nothing about the red line at all. The line is a property of the source. Nothing I have measured has ever been permitted to touch it.

IDF is Shannon's surprise

Back at my reading desk I turn a query card over in my hand and meet an old friend I did not know I had already built. Twenty-four years from now, in Spärck Jones (1972), search will learn to weight a word by its inverse document frequency, \(idf = \log\frac{N}{df}\) — large for rare words, near zero for common ones. But \(df/N\) is nothing but \(p(\text{term})\): the chance that a document pulled at random from the pile contains the word. So \(idf = -\log p(\text{term})\) — and that is exactly the self-information \(I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)\) my gauge has been reading all chapter. Two people wrote the one formula twenty-four years apart and gave it two names — though the next page puts a label on how far that identity really reaches. (Take the log in base 2 and the unit is a bit; the base only names the ruler.)

Hold the two cards up to the needle and watch. "the" sits in nearly every document, so \(p \approx 1\) and \(-\log_2 p \approx 0\): the needle does not budge — the word is a sure thing, and a sure thing is zero bits. Now a rare term — a word that turns up in just one document in a million — has \(p = 10^{-6}\), and the needle leaps almost off the top of the dial: \(-\log_2(10^{-6}) \approx 19.9\) bits (arithmetic). Rare terms carry many bits and discriminate sharply; "the" carries almost none. That is why every good ranker leans on the rare words and shrugs off the common ones — it is ranking by surprise.

So the weight that makes search work was information theory all along. When BM25 or TF-IDF multiplies a term by its \(idf\), it is paying that term in bits of surprise — and the measure of a useful search word turns out to be the very measure of a startling symbol: how much it tells you that you did not already know. My telegraph gauge, turned away from the wire and onto a corpus, is simply the IDF column of a search index. My needle and Karen Spärck Jones's weight are the same needle.

IDF wears the same formula as self-information — but it did not inherit the theorem that makes the formula mean anything

The bridge I just built is the best mnemonic in this book, and I am going to put a label on it before you carry it out of the room. Stephen Robertson — the man who co-authored BM25 — wrote a whole paper asking whether IDF really is Shannon's self-information, and his answer is that the connection is elusive. Three objections, and none of them is pedantry.

First, the event spaces do not match. \(\mathrm{df}/N\) is the probability that a randomly chosen document contains the term — an event about documents. Shannon's \(p\) is the probability of a symbol in a stream. They are not the same experiment, and inside a Shannon-like formalism you cannot even be sure that adding the IDFs of two terms is a legal operation. Second, nothing in information theory tells you to sum over the query's terms only: every term in the document carries information just as honestly as any other, and the query has no privileged status. It is the probabilistic retrieval model, not Shannon, that gives non-query terms zero weight — by an explicit assumption you can inspect and argue with.

Third, and this is the deep one: pointwise \(-\log p\) has no operational status. Robertson's own example: a binary source with probabilities 0.89 and 0.11 has \(H = 0.4999\) bit, and that number is a promise you can go and test — a coding theorem says the stream compresses to half its length. Its pointwise pieces, \(0.1681\) and \(3.1844\) bits, are behind no theorem at all. Averages are results; summands are intuitions. IDF is a summand.

So keep the bridge — it is historically real and it makes the shape of IDF unforgettable — but call it an analogy, not an identity. The tell is BM25 itself, whose IDF is \(\log\frac{N-\mathrm{df}+0.5}{\mathrm{df}+0.5}\): not \(-\log p\) at all, and cheerfully negative for a term in more than half the collection. And when the language-modelling view arrives, the IDF effect will fall out of collection smoothing without anyone invoking Shannon at all — which is, in the end, a better compliment to the idea than the borrowed theorem was.

The same bill, counted over the index — and the codec that appeared to break the floor

One more place the gauge already hangs, and you built it yourself in L3. A postings list is a sorted run of document ids — \([3, 8, 12, 30]\) — and nobody stores it that way. Store the gaps, \([3, 5, 4, 18]\), and they are small; give each gap a variable-byte code and four ids that cost 128 bits raw come down to 32. Four to one, and it is the same trade as the crooked coin: the gaps are skewed, and skew is compressibility.

So where is the floor here? Not under the list — under the distribution of the gaps. For a random \(n\)-subset of a universe of size \(U\) the counting bound gives roughly \(\log_2(U/n) + 1.4427\) bits per gap, and that second term is \(\log_2 e\) — the very nats-to-bits constant from the perplexity page. One constant props up a language model's loss and the size of your inverted index, which is a good moment to stop thinking of them as different subjects.

And then the fact that should unsettle you. On the Gov2 collection the measured entropy of the gaps is \(3.02\) bits per integer, and binary interpolative coding stores them in \(2.94\). Below the floor. The theorem is not broken; the floor was computed for the wrong source. The bound assumes the integers land at random, and real postings lists cluster — documents about the same thing were crawled together. The codec found structure that the model of the source did not have, so the true floor was always lower than the number we drew.

Gov2, bits per docID: VByte \(8.81\) · Rice \(4.08\) · Elias-δ \(3.74\) · Opt-PFor \(3.63\) · PEF \(3.12\) · gap entropy \(3.02\) · BIC \(2.94\).

Keep that lesson: \(H\) is a property of a model of the source, never of the data. Every time something appears to beat an entropy — a better codec, a bigger language model, a compressor that finally takes the enwik8 record — what it has actually done is adopt a richer model, whose floor sits lower and is just as uncrossable.

What IDF actually was when it was proposed

I keep telling you that IDF is my needle wearing a retrieval uniform. Go and read the paper that proposed it and the formula is not there. Spärck Jones defines a bucket function — \(f(n) = m\) when \(n\) sits between two consecutive powers of two — and weights a term by \(f(N) - f(n) + 1\). No \(\log(N/\mathrm{df})\) anywhere: you are counting doublings, how many times you must double one to reach \(n\), and the weight comes out an integer.

Her own worked examples reproduce to the digit, and they track the smooth logarithm to within a fraction of a bit — which is exactly why the modern formula feels like hers, and why nobody noticed the substitution. But the motivation is the part that matters. The bucketing came from Zipf, from the observation that frequencies fall off geometrically, and not from a theory of communication; a colleague suggested the shape; and decades later she said in as many words that the proposal had not been set within any specific theoretical framework.

Keep the chronology straight, because it decides what you are entitled to claim. The identity with self-information was noticed afterwards, by people who already had both formulas lying on the desk. That makes the bridge a retrofit rather than a derivation — historically real, memorable, and exactly as strong as the objections you read two beats ago. A good mnemonic that knows it is a mnemonic is worth more than a theorem borrowed without reading the loan agreement.

Query difficulty in bits, before a single click

Here is a debt the chapter owes you: can the gauge tell in advance that a query is going to go badly? Clarity says yes, and it is my needle pointed at a result list. Build a language model of the documents the query retrieved, hold it against the language of the collection as a whole, and read the KL between them in bits. A clear query is one whose results talk unlike the corpus at large; a vague one pulls back documents that sound like the collection's general babble. It correlates with average precision well enough to be useful, it beats an average-IDF baseline on most collections though not on every one, and below a threshold in bits a query is more likely than not to finish under the median.

Now the turn, and it comes from the very same paper. The obvious variant does not work. Take the entropy of the distribution over documents — a flat ranking means the engine is unsure, a peaked one means it is confident — and there is no appreciable rank correlation with average precision, except at the extreme ends of the scale, where what you are looking at is degeneracy rather than prediction: a maximally flat result list is near-uniform, and a near-uniform list never yields high precision anyway.

Why one works and the other does not is the lesson of the whole act. The entropy of a ranking describes your scorer — turn a temperature knob and it moves, while the query has not changed at all. Clarity's KL is taken over the vocabulary, against the collection: a property of the query relative to the corpus, and no knob inside your engine can fake it. An information-theoretic formula does not make a measurement meaningful; the event space does — the same warning that was aimed at IDF a few pages back, arriving now from a completely different direction.

Nobody added an IDF: it fell out of the smoothing

Two acts ago I left you a promise: the language-modelling view of retrieval pays the IDF weight back without invoking me at all. Time to pay it. Score a document by minus the KL between the query's model and the document's, \(s(D,Q) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_Q \| \theta_D)\). The query's own entropy is the same for every document, so ranking by \(-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) is ranking by minus the cross-entropy: to the top rises the document under whose model the query is cheapest to encode. Watch the sign — smallest cross-entropy — and watch the scope: the equivalence holds inside one query and stops holding the moment scores are compared across queries, as filtering does.

Now rewrite the query-likelihood score with collection smoothing — every term is given a little probability from the corpus so that one unseen word cannot zero the whole product. Do the algebra and the collection probability lands in a denominator: a common term makes that denominator large and its own contribution small, a rare term does the opposite. Nobody added an IDF. It emerged from the smoothing, as a consequence of refusing to hand any word a probability of zero.

This is the cleanest answer to «why does IDF work», and it is a better answer than the borrowed theorem: in this view the weight is not a heuristic at all, it is what smoothing does. And notice what the chapter has just done to one quantity. The KL that was a tax for the wrong price list in the bridge act is a term weight here. One functional, two jobs, and no coincidence — that is the sign that you have found a mechanism instead of an analogy.

Rare is not the same as useful

The temptation this whole act has been building toward: a rare term carries many bits, therefore the rarer the better — weight by IDF and be happy. Here is the counter-example, out of a single newswire corpus. Two words occur in almost the same number of stories, so their IDF agrees to the second digit. One of them is a keyword somebody would really search for; the other is a hedge word nobody would ever type into a box. My needle cannot tell them apart, because rarity is all my needle measures.

What does tell them apart is burstiness. A real keyword clumps: having occurred once in a document it will occur there again, because the document is about it. A function word is sprinkled evenly, exactly as a random process of the same rate would sprinkle it. Measure the departure from that random model — the null model again, in its third costume this chapter — and the two words separate cleanly. The authors' own conclusion runs squarely against standard practice: the best keywords sit toward the middle of the frequency range, not at the rare end.

So carry two dials out of here, not one. \(-\log p\) ranks rarity; usefulness is about how a term's occurrences bunch together inside documents. This is why BM25 saturates its term-frequency instead of trusting it linearly, and why whole families of burstiness models exist at all. And it is the honest summary of the act, in the third wording I have given it: the formula is fine and the arithmetic is fine — the meaning lives in what you chose to count.

Adding surprises assumes the terms do not travel together

Every lexical scorer you have written adds one weight per query term. Say what that is in the language of this chapter: it adds self-informations. And addition of surprises has a precondition sitting right inside the logarithm — \(-\log p(A) - \log p(B) = -\log\big(p(A)\,p(B)\big)\), and the product on the right is the chance of seeing both only if the two are independent. The moment your terms travel together — a first name and its surname, a city and its river, a brand and its product line — that product is not the joint chance of anything, and the sum is not the surprise of the query.

The failure has a direction, which is what makes it worth naming. Correlated terms double-count: the document that contains the pair is paid twice for one fact about the world, because the second term told you almost nothing you did not already learn from the first. That is the mechanism under phrase and proximity features, under every term-dependence model, and under the small mystery of why repeating a word inside a query moves the score at all. It is also the sharpest form of the objection about event spaces from a few pages back: the sum is not merely a step no theorem licenses, it is a step that carries a named assumption — and a named assumption is something you can go and test on your own collection.

Close it honestly, though. The assumption is usually good enough, which is exactly why the naive sum outlived every attempt to replace it: topic words in a real query are only mildly dependent, and mild dependence costs you a mild over-reward. But when it does break, it breaks the same way every time — redundant evidence rewarded as if it were new — and half the patches in a classical ranker exist to blunt precisely that. The lesson is the one this chapter has been repeating in different clothes: the formula is arithmetic, and the modelling lives entirely in what you had to assume to write it down.

How many bits does it take to name one document?

Turn the gauge on the search itself and ask the crudest possible question: to single out one document from a collection of \(N\), how much information must arrive? The answer is the one my whole chapter has been rehearsing — \(\log_2 N\) bits, the cost of naming one outcome among \(N\) equally likely ones, and no query can pick out a document by supplying less. That is a budget, and for the first time it lets you read a query as a payment rather than as a string.

Now price the payment with the weight from a few pages back. Each matched term contributes its own \(-\log_2\) of how often it occurs — that is what makes a rare word worth many bits and a word that appears in half the collection worth a fraction of one. Line the budget up against the payment and three familiar facts stop being folklore. A couple of genuinely rare terms can already carry more than the whole naming budget, which is why keyword search works at all on collections far larger than anyone's memory. Adding a common word to a query changes almost nothing, because it pays almost nothing toward the budget. And when a query cannot cover \(\log_2 N\) — a single frequent word against a huge index — no ranker can rescue it: the information required to identify the answer was never sent, and everything downstream is guesswork dressed as ordering.

Two warnings, both of which this chapter has earned. The count only adds up if the terms are independent, so a query of clinging terms pays less than its arithmetic suggests, and the budget is the place where that over-count becomes visible. And, as ever, the average is what the theorem covers: \(\log_2 N\) is the cost of naming a document when all of them are equally plausible, while a real collection is lopsided, so some documents are far cheaper to name than others. Read the budget as the reason searching a large collection with a short query is possible at all — not as a promise about the query in front of you.

From a mechanical mouse to your training loop

In 1950 the man who had just defined information built a mouse. Theseus was a magnet on three wheels with a brain of telephone relays: it bumped along the walls of a maze, recorded where the dead ends were, and afterwards ran the route without a mistake. Rebuild the maze and it learned the new one. It is one of the earliest machines about which the verb learned is not a courtesy. The same year he published the first technical paper on programming a computer to play chess — the move tree, the evaluation function, the bounded search depth that every engine still uses. In 1955 he put his name, alongside McCarthy, Minsky and Rochester, on the proposal for the Dartmouth workshop, the document where the phrase artificial intelligence is written for the first time.

So the line from this chapter to the rest of the course is not thematic, it is literal. The cross-entropy that trains a classifier, the perplexity that ranks language models, the information gain a decision tree splits on, the \(\mathrm{idf}\) that weights a rare query term — these are not four formulas that resemble \(H\). They are \(H\), applied four times, and you have now computed it by hand often enough to recognise it in each disguise.

One last thing worth keeping, because it is easy to imagine that theory is written by people unlike you. He rode a unicycle down the corridors of Bell Labs while juggling. He named his house Entropy House. Dragged onto a stage in Brighton in 1985, he said a few words, took three balls from his pockets and juggled instead of lecturing. Asked what drove him: "I was motivated by curiosity. Never by the desire for financial gain. I just wondered how things were put together."

One gauge, one red line

Lift your hand from the key with me and look at the whole gauge. One idea powered every beat: information is surprise. A symbol you saw coming barely twitches the needle; a rare one spikes it — that is self-information, \(I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)\), a sure thing worth 0 bits. Average that surprise over a whole source and you have entropy, \(H = -\sum p\,\log_2 p\) — expected surprise, the fewest yes/no questions per symbol. Morse felt it when he gave E a single dot; Hartley wrote the log that made it add up; in 1948 Shannon gave the unit its name — the bit.

Now watch the one red line. Printed English, once you let context speak, carries only about one bit per letter — in Shannon's guessing game a reader called 79 of 102 letters before ever seeing them. That single bit is a floor with two faces. It is the hard limit of lossless compression — no coder beats \(H\), a good one meets it exactly (\(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18 \Rightarrow H=1.75\), and the optimal code averages 1.75). And it is the target a language model chases: training is minimizing cross-entropy \(H(p,q) \ge H(p)\), closing the KL gap to the true source. The bits-per-character in every LM paper is Shannon's measure, read off by a machine. You can get close to the floor. You can't get under it.

And back at my reading desk, the same quantity wears a search name: the self-information of a query term, \(-\log p(\text{term})\), is IDF — rare words carry many bits and cut the index sharply; the carries almost none. So carry one shape out of this chapter, from the telegraph key to the language model: one gauge, one red line — surprise you can measure, and a floor you can approach but never cross. Next chapter we step out onto the proving grounds and start measuring rankings instead of letters — and \(\log_2\) walks in with us, this time as the discount inside nDCG that makes a hit further down the list count for less. Same arithmetic of surprise, a new dial. Thank you for sitting at the key with me.
