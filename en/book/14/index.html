The Curved Map — geometry of meaning: anisotropy & hubness

The ruler reads 0.99 between strangers

I'm Séréga, and today I'm the cartographer of a warped world. On my drafting table lies a map of meaning: every word a town, every town placed by an embedding model. A visitor points to two towns at opposite corners of the sheet — thermodynamics and ukulele. Strangers, surely. I lay my ruler across them — cosine — and the dial reads \( \cos \approx 0.99 \). Neighbours. The map swears they are all but the same place.

The ruler isn't broken; the map is. Just as Mercator flattens a round globe onto flat paper, my model has projected a round world of meaning onto a flat sheet — and the projection is savagely distorted. It has squeezed almost every town into one narrow wedge, a thin cone off in the corner (on a transformer's last layer even random words sit at cosine near 0.99 — Ethayarajh 2019). When everyone lives in the same slice, straight-line distance lies: the ruler reads 0.99 between total strangers, and its honest range has all but collapsed.

In L13 we made the search fast — HNSW lanes, IVF cells, a compressed index that answers in milliseconds. But it answers along this map: if the projection is warped, speed only delivers the wrong neighbours sooner. So this whole chapter is a re-projection job. First I'll survey the damage — the narrow cone (anisotropy) and the handful of «everyone's-neighbour» towns where all roads lead (hubness). Then I re-draw: re-center, un-stretch, un-shear the grid so distances mean what they should, and charge a crowding toll so no town looks close just because it's crowded. The map of meaning is warped. Let's straighten it.

Three complaints in the logs, two diseases in the map

Before I unroll the drafting paper, listen to the three complaints that arrive from the retrieval logs. One: every score huddles against the top of the scale, so a cut-off threshold has nowhere to sit — wherever you draw the line it either keeps everything or keeps nothing. Two: the same small handful of passages comes back for queries that share nothing with each other. Three: there is a document sitting in the index that is never found at all.

An engineer sees three bugs. A cartographer sees two diseases of the paper. The first complaint is the cone: the wedge has crushed the scale and the ruler has lost its range. The second and the third are hubness: the roads have drained into a few Romes, and the towns out on the margins are left without a single road. Neither is repaired by swapping the model or nudging the threshold — what gets repaired is the projection itself.

Keep those three lines with you to the end of the chapter, because each has its own diagnosis and its own measurement. A crushed scale is read off the mean cosine of random pairs. Over-visited Romes are read off the skew of the reverse-neighbour count. And the third line is the treacherous one: no quality metric will ever show it to you, because "the document did not come back" is not an event and leaves no line in any log. You measure it over the index rather than over the results — as the fraction of towns with \(N_k = 0\).

Why the ruler and the order, and never the surveying

Three complaints, two diseases — and before the drafting paper comes out, two stops on the spine. This hour lights Measure and Rank, and the pair is not a hedge: the two legs split the work along the line between a reading and the damage a bad reading does. Say which is which now and every cure later in the chapter will arrive pre-sorted.

Measure is the leg this hour was born on, because the patient is the instrument. Sir Cosine's ruler is not broken in the sense of returning garbage — it returns a number that has quietly lost its range, because the cone crushed the scale until every honest pair and every stranger read almost the same. Subtracting the shared mean, whitening the paper, tightening a score against a local neighbourhood: each of those re-cuts the ruler so that a difference in meaning again produces a difference in reading. Rank is where the second disease lives, and hubness is not a property of a pair at all — it is a property of a list and of an index. A few Romes standing at the top of results for queries with nothing in common, and towns off in the margins that no road reaches: you cannot see either by looking at one comparison, only by counting over the whole store. One leg is the reading; the other is where the wrong readings do their harm.

And now the dark neighbour, the one you would reach for first. Get Data stays dark on purpose. There is a fix that lives there — retrain the encoder so its space comes out round, and the cone never forms — and this chapter deliberately does not take it. Everything it hands you applies to a model you did not train, cannot retrain, and are perhaps only renting: centre it, whiten it, tax the hubs, all downstream of vectors that already exist. The map arrives as it arrives; we repair the reading of it, not the surveying. Keep that as a rule for placing any repair you meet later: if it changes how the vectors are produced, it belongs to the Archives; if it changes how they are read and ordered afterwards, it belongs here.

Every town crowds into one wedge

This morning I laid two fresh vectors on the drafting table — two words plucked at random from a page, strangers to each other — and set my compass to measure the angle between them. The needle barely opened. Cosine \(\approx 0.99\). On an honest map two random towns should sit at all sorts of angles; here they huddle as if they were the very same place.

That is anisotropy, and it is the first lie my map tells. The contextual vectors a Transformer draws do not spread out to fill the sphere — they crowd into one narrow cone, a single thin wedge of directions. Ethayarajh measured it: in GPT-2's last layer two random words average a cosine near \(0.99\), while an honest, isotropic space would average \(0\). Everything the model knows is crammed into one sliver of the paper.

Picture a Mercator that squashes every town into the same slim wedge. My ruler still reports a distance, but its usable range has collapsed — from a whole circle down to a hair. When every town points nearly the same way, every town looks close to every other, and 'nearest neighbour' stops meaning anything. Before I can trust a single distance on this map, I have to learn why the paper got folded so tight.

The knob is at zero: no shared axis at all. The cloud sits around the origin, the wedge it subtends is the whole plane, and two random points meet at about 90° — cosine 0.00. This is isotropy, and it is the only setting where cosine spends its full range.

Turn it to c/σ ≈ 1.22 — the shared axis is only slightly louder than each word's own identity. The cloud has already drifted off the origin, and the average pair reads cos ≈ 0.61, about 53°. This is GPT-2's middle layers (Ethayarajh 2019 writes ≈0.6 in the text).

Turn it to c/σ ≈ 9.95 — the shared axis is ten times louder than the meaning. The cloud is a small ball far from the origin, the wedge is a sliver, and the average pair reads cos ≈ 0.99, about 8°. This is GPT-2's last layer. Nothing about the model changed; one ratio did.

Now hold the knob at c/σ = 3 and sweep the dimension instead: d = 2 → 10 → 100 → 768. Two and ten dimensions are still too few for the asymptotics and read a little high, but from d = 100 onward the number is pinned at 0.90 and never moves again — exactly what E[cos] = c²/(c²+σ²) predicts, because d cancels out of the formula. Anisotropy is a signal-to-noise story. Hubness, next act, is the one that really is about d.

One last turn — subtract the mean μ. The cloud slides back over the origin, the wedge springs open to the full plane, and the same 0.90 collapses to ≈0. That is the whole of act 02 in one gesture: the cone was never a property of the words, only of where the crowd was standing.

The famous reading is one cell of a table

Now a confession about my own ruler. The reading I keep quoting between strangers was not taken on "contextual models" in general — it was taken on one model, at one layer. An independent replication laid several presses side by side and measured the same quantity on each: one architecture crushes its towns into what is almost a single point; the famous reading belongs to the final layer of another; step one layer down that same press and the wedge is already much wider; and the model whose repair fills the next act of this survey reads about half the famous figure.

Then look further down that table, at the maps drawn before the presses existed at all. Static vectors — one fixed point per word, no context — come out very nearly isotropic: no wedge worth the name. That single row settles what kind of thing the cone is. It is not a property of embeddings; it is something the training stack does to them, layer by layer, and it does it in different amounts to different presses.

So the practical habit: never quote a cone without naming the model and the layer it was measured on. Picking which layer you read the map from is a calibration decision, not an implementation detail — and it is the cheapest one available to you, because it costs nothing but a different index into the stack.

It is not the dimension — it is the shared mean

Here you should stop me, because L13 taught you the opposite. There you learned that as the paper gains dimensions, distances concentrate and two random directions come out very nearly perpendicular. So how can two strangers read almost the top of my dial? Two things hide in that objection. L13 measured the concentration of Euclidean distances — it measured nothing at all about cosine. And its cloud was not "random directions": every coordinate was positive, so the whole crowd shares an enormous mean vector, and a crowd with a shared mean is exactly a cone.

Model a town as a shared axis plus its own individuality, \(x = \mu + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\), and the average cosine of two independent draws comes out as \(\mathbb{E}[\cos] \approx \lVert\mu\rVert^{2} / (\lVert\mu\rVert^{2} + d\sigma^{2})\); the left-hand form holds for any shared \(\mu\); put in the model substitution \(\mu = c\,\mathbf{1}_d\) — a shared \(c\) in every coordinate — and it tidies into \(c^{2}/(c^{2}+\sigma^{2})\). Read the right-hand side: there is no \(d\) in it. The cone is a signal-to-noise reading — how loud the thing every town has in common is against the thing that makes each town itself — and it would be just as tight on a small sheet.

So carry three separations, not one. Concentration is about \(d\). The cone is about \(c/\sigma\). And hubness — the roads, the next act — is about \(d\) again. The cleanest proof is a subtraction: take the very uniform cloud L13 measured its contrast on, remove its shared mean, and it drops straight onto the near-perpendicular curve you were promised. The contradiction was never in the geometry; it was in measuring two different things and calling both "high dimension".

The ruler squints at the spelling, not just the place

Come stand at my drafting table. I hand you my ruler — plain \(\cos\) — and ask you to measure two towns that share nothing: pick two words at random, and the needle reads \(0.99\). Two more strangers? \(0.99\) again. On this squashed map every distance has been herded into one narrow wedge, so the ruler has lost its range. The gap between a true neighbour and a total stranger — which ought to span the whole width of the page — is now the last hair on the dial. Near-ties everywhere; false neighbours by the cartload.

Worse than dull: it over-reads the surface. Li and colleagues (2020, Table 6) measured how hard similarity scores cling to the surface of two sentences — how many letters you must edit to turn one string into the other. Human gold judgements of similarity track that surface only mildly: Spearman \(\rho \approx -24.61\). Raw BERT cosine clings twice as hard: \(\rho \approx -50.49\). Read those two numbers carefully, because the obvious reading is wrong. Both are negative — more edits, less similar — so the human judgements lean on spelling too, and legitimately: two sentences that share most of their words usually do mean much the same thing. The finding is not that my instrument glances at the spelling; it is the ratio, (50.49 / 24.61 = 2.05). My instrument does see where the towns sit — but it glances at the spelling of their names about twice as often as an honest surveyor would. And it is fixable: run the same measurement on Li's flow-corrected vectors and the number falls to (-28.01), almost down to the human level.

That is the quiet disaster of the curved map. When cosine rewards string overlap beyond what meaning warrants, nearest neighbour favours the passage that merely looks like the query over the one that answers it. Before I trust a single distance again, I must fix the projection itself — flatten the wedge, make the ground even. That is the next leg of the survey.

Turn the sheet on the table

A shape measurement should survive a turn of the drafting sheet. Mean cosine passes that test: for an orthogonal rotation \(Q\), \(\cos(Qx,Qy)=\cos(x,y)\). Average the same pairs before and after the turn and you get the same answer. IsoScore passes too. The distinction begins when we slide the cloud relative to the origin.

Hold covariance fixed and move an isotropic Gaussian away from zero: average pair cosine can rise while its covariance remains isotropic. Cosine describes angles from the origin, so it is sensitive to the mean. In Rudman et al.'s Table 1, AvgRandCosSim passes rotation invariance but fails five other tests. Table 3 reports variation in the sampled score 1 minus the absolute mean cosine, not a change in the geometric identity. Its experimental cause requires checking the protocol, not concluding that rotation changes cosine.

Keep both measurements with their definitions. A high average cosine describes pair angles; covariance isotropy describes the spread across principal directions. Centering directly addresses the mean offset, but it does not by itself equalise covariance eigenvalues. That is why measuring cosine, centering and measuring IsoScore answer different questions.

Who folded the map into a wedge? The nightly press did — and its tallest ridges secretly measure how often, not what.

So who bent my map into a wedge? Not I — the press did, every night it trained. Each time it guesses the next word, the press rewards the one true answer and shoves down every word that wasn't it. But almost every word is rare, and almost every night it's the wrong answer — so the rare towns get shoved, again and again, in the very same direction. Gao and his crew (2019) call this representation degeneration: a shared negative pull that herds the whole long tail of rare words into one common corner.

And the press has a habit that seals the fate: after every layer it runs a LayerNorm, pinning each town onto one thin shell and re-centring it. With everyone stuck on that shell and shoved the same way, the geometry all but guarantees they pack into a narrow cone. It isn't bad luck — it's the near-certain outcome of how the thing was trained.

When I finally surveyed the finished map and asked what its biggest ridges actually measure, the answer stung. Mu and Viswanath (2018) found the top few axes don't encode meaning at all — they sort towns by how often each is spoken. Frequency, dressed up as geography. That's the quiet trick behind all-but-the-top: lop off the top \(d/100\) directions and you haven't lost meaning, you've just deleted the frequency ridges that were fooling the ruler. On my own small survey the arithmetic is blunt: two unrelated towns read \(0.8985\) before, and project out a single ridge — the top principal component of the raw, un-centred cloud — and they read \(-0.0313\). One direction. And a third number says why one is enough: that ridge lines up with the cone's own axis at \(0.9997\).

The wedge is not made of rare words

There is a tidy story about who folded my map, and I have just told you a version of it: the press shoves the rare towns, night after night, in the same direction, and the long tail packs into a corner. From there it is one short step to the comfortable conclusion — the cone is a frequency artefact of the vocabulary, so what I am treating is a rare-word problem. Hold on to the first half; the conclusion does not survive.

It fails on its own terms, in three places. Presses whose vocabulary has no rare-piece tail at all — the ones that read plain characters, or bytes — show the same crushed cosine. So do presses trained on speech rather than text, in every layer. And an untrained transformer block already lifts the reading, before a single gradient has been applied to anything. A cause that is absent while the effect is present was never the cause. The better reading is less comforting and more useful: a shared direction is what you get from a mechanism built to make a few positions dominate — the price of sharp attention, not a bookkeeping error in the vocabulary.

And now hold both findings at once, because they are compatible and people usually drop one. On a trained language press the tallest ridges really do sort towns by how often each is spoken — that is what the shared direction ends up encoding here. It is not why a shared direction exists. Keep the distinction, because it changes the prescription: if the wedge were purely a frequency ledger you could delete the ledger and be done, and the next beat is about how far that actually gets you.

The wedge breaks the threshold, not the sort

Now let me be honest about how much damage the wedge really does, because a surveyor who cries about every distortion gets ignored. Rank the candidates of a single query and the cone is largely harmless: the shared shift squeezes every reading in nearly the same direction, and a near-monotone squeeze leaves the order of the top of the list mostly intact. If all you ever need from the ruler is which town comes first, you can live on a warped map for years.

The damage starts wherever the score is used as a number rather than as a sort key. A cut-off rule — return it if the reading passes \(\tau\) — sits on a scale crushed into a sliver, and \(\tau\) does not carry across models or even across layers. Calibrating a reading into a probability inherits the same crushing. Blending with a lexical score (the hybrid you built earlier) mixes two rulers whose ranges no longer match — which is precisely why rank-based fusion dodges the problem. And any comparison between queries — deduplication, deciding there is no answer at all, escalating to a human — is a comparison of two crushed scales at once.

One line to keep: the cone is a disease of the scale, not of the sort. So "my ranking metric did not move" proves nothing about whether there is something to repair — look where the score is read as a quantity. And hold the contrast, because the second half of this chapter is the mirror image: hubness does not squeeze the scale, it changes who is in the list at all.

A few coordinates are doing all the talking

Open my ruler and look inside it. Cosine is a sum over coordinates, \(\cos(x,y) \propto \sum_i x_i y_i\), and nothing whatsoever obliges that sum to be supplied evenly. On a real press it is not. A handful of coordinates — sometimes one — carries almost the entire expected reading between two strangers, because its activation sits far above every other coordinate's and stays there for every town on the map. Call them the rogue dimensions: not directions the survey discovered, but a few named columns that hijacked the total.

Two measurements make the point stick. Project out a small, fixed set of those columns and the crushed reading between strangers collapses — across every layer of every press examined, not just the famous one. And part of what my dial was reading was never geometry at all: two tokens sitting at the very start of their sequences read far higher than the same tokens anywhere else, so a slice of the famous figure is a position artefact wearing a shape's clothing.

Now the reason this belongs in the survey and not in a footnote: it changes the repair. Deleting a few named columns and flattening a covariance are not the same operation. The first is a projection of small rank — you can name what you removed, audit it, and put it back. The second re-weights every direction at once, including the ones a modern press spent on meaning, and it cannot be inspected column by column. That is the sharpest argument for keeping the ladder of repairs apart, and it explains something you will otherwise find puzzling: why the cheapest rung so often recovers most of the benefit of the widest one.

Length carries frequency, and cosine throws it away

Everything I have measured so far is a statement about directions, because my ruler divides the length out by construction. So let me ask what I threw away. On a raw press the length is not empty at all: the norm of a town's vector grows with how rare its word is, and the rare towns sit both further from the centre of the crowd and further from each other. Rarity is written on the map in the one channel cosine deletes first.

That has a consequence I would rather you learn here than in production. Cosine erases the very signal that could have told you this reading involves a rare town, treat it with suspicion. A ranking built on the plain inner product does not erase it — the norm stays inside the score, so a longer vector is preferred, which means rarer content is systematically preferred. Same vectors, two metrics, two different orderings; and the index you chose in L13 committed you to one of them before any of this chapter's advice arrives.

So two habits. Know which quantity your index actually maximises before you read a single recommendation of mine onto it — and remember that normalising to unit length at write time makes that choice permanent and invisible. And take the free diagnostic while it is on the table: plot the norm of each vector against how common its content is in your corpus. If the frequency ridge is there, it shows up in that plot without any decomposition of the covariance at all, and you have learned something about your press before touching a single repair.

The wedge bills you twice, and the second bill is storage

So far the wedge has been a complaint about a ruler. Turn it into a complaint about a warehouse. Every town on this map carries the same shared component — that is what the cone is — and the ruler merely reads it. But everything downstream of the ruler spends a fixed budget per coordinate, and a budget spent on what every town carries buys you nothing. The fast index you built in the previous lecture does not keep your vectors as they are: it cuts them into pieces and replaces each piece with the nearest entry of a codebook fitted to the crowd. Fit a codebook to a crowd that all leans one way, and most of its entries go on describing the lean.

The same arithmetic runs through every other budget on the map. Keep only the top axes of an uncentred cloud and the very first axis you keep is essentially the direction everybody shares — you paid your widest, most expensive coordinate for a fact that separates nobody. Move the crowd onto its own centre first and the identical budget goes to the differences instead. That is a second reason, independent of the ruler, why subtract the mean sits at the cheap end of the ladder and is almost always right: the first reason was that the reading lies, this one is that the storage is wasted.

Two practical edges follow, and both cut. Centring changes what is written down, so it is an index-level decision on the same terms as the rest of the contract — the crowd's centre must be estimated, shipped and versioned before the codebook is fitted, never bolted on afterwards. And a codebook fitted on raw vectors, then fed centred ones, is the silent failure again in a new costume: nothing errors, every distance is slightly wrong, and the only symptom is a recall you cannot explain.

The mean direction is a stop word with no name

Read the diagnosis of the last few pages in the language of the lexical index and it stops being exotic. The tallest ridges of a raw press secretly measure how often, not what — and a component present in every document, by construction, tells you nothing about which document. That sentence is not new to you. It is the whole justification of a stop list, and the reason a term that appears everywhere is worth almost nothing in the index you built two lectures before this one.

So centring is not a curiosity of linear algebra: it is the dense analogue of dropping the stop words. Same principle — subtract what everybody says — applied to a map where words no longer have their own columns. The one difference is the one that matters in practice. On the sparse side you can name the thing you removed, print the list and argue with it; here the removed thing is a direction with no name, so the only way to argue with the repair is to measure it. That is the whole reason this chapter keeps handing you measurements instead of rules.

Now the turn, because the analogy also tells you where it ends. A stop list built on one corpus over-removes on another — the word that carries no information in the news carries plenty in a corpus of contracts — and the crowd's centre inherits exactly that fragility: it is fitted to your crowd and expires with it. And the analogy dies precisely at the second rung of the ladder. The deeper axes are not stop words; they are where a contrastively trained press keeps meaning. Subtracting what everyone shares is stop-word removal; flattening every axis is deleting the vocabulary — which is why the rule that survives is remove the mean direction, do not whiten the covariance.

Re-project the map: center, then whiten

The diagnosis is drawn; now I re-project. The first move is the humblest — I center the sheet. The cone of towns does not even point through the origin: every town leans off in one shared direction, like a map whose whole continent has drifted into a corner. So I find the mean vector \( \mu \), the center of gravity of the entire cloud, and subtract it — \( x - \mu \). Now the origin sits inside the crowd instead of off in the margin, and 'opposite' finally means opposite again.

But a centered wedge is still a wedge — stretched long in a few directions, crushed flat in most. To make it round I whiten. I take the covariance matrix \( \Sigma \), the ledger of which directions stretch and which shear, and split it into its natural axes: \( \Sigma = U \Lambda U^{\top} \). Then I build one map-maker's matrix \( W = U \Lambda^{-1/2} \) and re-draw every town as \( \tilde{x} = (x - \mu)\,W \). Read it as two turns of the wrist: \( U \) rotates the paper onto the cloud's true axes, and \( \Lambda^{-1/2} \) rescales each axis by \( 1/\sqrt{\lambda} \) until it carries exactly unit variance. Un-stretch, un-shear — the covariance becomes the identity, and one centimetre finally means the same distance in every direction: an equal-area grid.

And the squashed wedge pops open into a round, even field. Cosine's collapsed range springs back, because two strangers are no longer forced to share a heading. Best of all it costs no retraining — pure linear algebra on vectors you already own, fitted once on a sample. Su et al. (2021) whiten raw BERT and watch STS-B climb from 59.04 to 71.34, and as a parting gift the transform even lets you shrink the vector — keep the top axes, drop the near-flat ones. I re-projected the world, and the ruler stopped lying.

Four cities, A (9,7) and B (7,9) as far apart in meaning as C and D. Raw, every one of the six cosines sits above 0.88 and the average is 0.9504. Even the loosest pair, C·D, reads 0.8824. This is a cone: seen from the origin, the whole map fits inside a narrow wedge.

Subtract the centre of mass μ = (6,6). The whole ledger changes sign: the average cosine drops from 0.9504 to −0.3333, and the two antipodal pairs finally read a truthful −1. But look at the strangers A·B: 0.60. Still positive, still wrong. Centring moved the map; it did not un-shear it.

Here is what is left, written down: Σ = [[5,3],[3,5]]. The off-diagonal 3 is the shear — the two axes are correlated, so a step along x is partly a step along y. Its eigenvalues are λ₁ = 8 and λ₂ = 2, a condition number of 4: the cloud is four times longer one way than the other. That ratio is exactly the 0.60 you just saw.

The first turn of the wrist: U. Rotate the paper onto the cloud's own axes — the dashed lines (1,1)/√2 and (1,−1)/√2. Nothing about the cities has changed, only which way the grid is written. The rectangle now stands square to the page, long axis horizontal, short axis vertical.

The second turn: Λ^(−1/2). Divide along the long axis by √8 = 2.8284 and along the short one by √2 = 1.4142. Every city lands on (±1, ±1) — the rectangle is now a perfect square, and the covariance is the identity. This is the whole of W = UΛ^(−1/2), done by hand on four points.

The ledger after: the strangers A·B read exactly 0.0000. So do A·D, B·C and C·D. Only the two genuine antipodes keep −1, which is the truth. Read the whole walk in one line: 0.9504 → strangers 0.60 → strangers 0.0000. Centring is half the repair; Λ^(−1/2) does the other half.

A re-projection is a contract, not a batch job

The centre \(\mu\) and the matrix \(W\) are estimated once, from a sample. Then comes the question that decides whether this works at all: applied to what? To every vector that will ever be laid under the ruler — the towns as they enter the index, and the query as it arrives, in the same request. A re-projection is not a clean-up pass over stored data; it is a contract between the two arms, exactly the shape of the rule a lexical index enforces through its analyzer.

Three consequences, and none of them shows up as an error message. The transform travels with the model, not with the batch that produced it, because a query re-projected by a different \(W\) than its documents is being measured with a different ruler. Re-fitting is therefore a re-index, not a config change: every stored vector was written under the old fit and is stale the moment you replace it. And mixing vectors from two fits in one index corrupts silently — cosine still returns a perfectly presentable number for a pair that no longer lives in the same space.

Which is why I keep \(\mu\) and \(W\) versioned beside the encoder and never beside the pipeline run. Ask of any repair in this chapter: is this a property of the map, so it must be pinned and shipped, or a property of the reading, so it can be recomputed at will? Centring and whitening are the first kind. The toll of act four is the second kind, and the difference is exactly why they cost their money in different places.

Centre, then re-normalise — the order is not free

Both halves of the repair look like one-line operations, so nobody writes down the order in which they run — and the two do not commute. Normalise every town to unit length and then subtract the crowd's centre, and you get one cloud; subtract the centre of the raw crowd and then re-normalise, and you get another. They are different maps, and there is no rate at which one converges to the other. Worse, the second one changes what my ruler even means: after the centre comes off, the towns no longer sit on the unit sphere, so a cosine now reads the angle between residuals — a different quantity from the cosine on which every threshold in your system was set.

Three rules keep this from biting. Estimate the centre on the same representation your index stores: if you write unit-length vectors, the crowd whose centre you want is the crowd of unit-length vectors, and a centre fitted on raw ones is a centre of a crowd that does not exist in your warehouse. If you re-normalise after centring, re-normalise on both arms, because the query is not exempt from anything. And know which quantity your engine maximises before you choose, because a plain inner product keeps the residual length inside the score while a cosine divides it away — the same fork the length-and-frequency beat left you with, now decided by the order of two lines of code.

So write the pipeline down as an ordered list — normalise, centre, re-normalise, project — and version the list next to the encoder, exactly as you version the centre and the matrix themselves. The commonest silent break in this whole chapter is not a wrong formula: it is a centre fitted before normalisation and applied after it. Nothing fails, every score is a little wrong, and the audit you run next quarter will read the damage as a property of the model.

The flattest axes are the least trustworthy ones

Look once more at what the re-projection actually does: \( W = U \Lambda^{-1/2} \) divides every axis by the square root of its own spread. Now put the two halves of that sentence together. The flattest axes are the ones with the smallest \( \lambda \) — and they are also the ones your sample knows least about, because a direction along which the crowd barely moves is a direction whose spread you measured from almost no signal. Whitening therefore divides your noisiest estimate by your smallest number. Whatever error sat in that axis comes out multiplied.

That is why every re-projection that works in the wild is truncated or damped: keep the top axes and drop the near-flat tail, or add a floor to \( \Lambda \) before inverting it. The chapter offered dropping the flat tail as a bonus — the wedge is so narrow that the dead columns cost almost nothing to throw away. Read it now as a necessity: those columns are not merely cheap, they are the ones you cannot estimate. And the same argument gives you the sample size rule for free. Centring asks your data for one vector's worth of numbers; whitening asks for a whole covariance, which grows with the square of the width. Give it a crowd smaller than the paper is wide and the matrix is not merely noisy — it is singular, and the inverse root you wrote down does not exist.

So the ladder of risk has a second reading, and it is arithmetic rather than taste. Each rung asks you to estimate more from the same crowd: a direction, a handful of axes, an entire shape. The rung that fixes the most is the rung that assumes the most about a sample you did not choose — which is the same reason it ages worst when the corpus moves under it. Before you argue about whether whitening helps your encoder, ask how many vectors you fitted it on.

Does the re-projection help? The scorecard

Back at the drafting table, I have re-projected the squashed wedge onto an even, equal-area grid — centred, un-stretched, un-sheared. Now the only honest question is: does the ruler stop lying? So I lay the same two strangers under it and read the number.

The scorecard is kind. Su's 2021 BERT-whitening lifts STS-B — how well my cosine ruler agrees with people on are these two sentences alike? — from \(59.04\) to \(71.34\). Except that I have to read the small print on my own scorecard. That \(71.34\) is the row Su marks (target): the centre \(\mu\) and the matrix \(W\) were fitted on the very sentences the score is then computed over — a transductive measurement. Fit them on a different corpus instead, as anyone deploying this must, and the honest number is \(68.19\): \(3.15\) points lower. Still a large gain for a training-free linear re-projection — but the gap is exactly what knowing the test set is worth, and I would rather write it on the card than let it flatter me. And there is a rebate: the wedge was so thin that dropping the dead columns costs almost nothing.

There is a learned cousin, too. Li's 2020 BERT-flow trains a smooth, invertible map that pours the narrow cone into a round Gaussian bell, and it adds \(+8.16\) to average STS across the suite. Two roads, one destination: the towns that once all read \(0.99\) now sit honestly far apart, and the neighbours who truly belong together finally measure close.

The flow, and whitening as its linear shadow

Whitening has a learned cousin, and looking at it properly makes the whole family legible. Instead of solving for one matrix, you train an invertible map \(f\) that takes the press's cloud onto a standard round Gaussian. Two design choices carry the entire idea. Invertibility means nothing can be discarded — the repair is permitted to re-express the map but never to delete part of it. And the destination is round by construction: you do not measure how uneven the sheet is and nudge it, you declare where it must arrive and learn the road there.

Now read whitening again with that in hand. Whitening is the same construction with \(f\) restricted to a linear map — one matrix, closed form, no training run. So these are not two rival methods to choose between; they are one idea and its linear shadow. The nonlinear version can bend different regions of the sheet by different amounts, which is strictly more expressive and buys correspondingly more on similarity tasks. It charges you for it: a training run, a saved model, and a second artefact to version and ship alongside the encoder.

Two honest readings before you pick. First, the encouraging one: both re-projections lift the similarity tasks and close most of the gap where my ruler was over-reading the spelling of a name rather than the position of a town. Two symptoms, one repair, one disease — the cleanest evidence in this whole survey that the crushed scale and the surface leak are the same bend in the paper. Second, the sobering one: both are still repairs applied afterwards, to a map somebody else drew. The turn waiting further along this survey lands on the flow exactly as hard as on the matrix.

Four repairs, one ladder of risk

Before you reach into the satchel, notice that what everybody calls "just normalise your embeddings" is really four different operations, and confusing them is the commonest way to break a working index. Subtract the shared mean — that removes the first moment, the sheer offset of the whole crowd from the origin. Project out a few of the tallest ridges — all-but-the-top, which deletes the handful of named axes that hoard the variance. Flatten every axis to the same width — that is whitening, and it touches all of them at once. And draw the map honest from the first day — contrastive training, which repairs nothing afterwards because nothing was bent.

Read them top to bottom as a ladder of risk: the wider the operation, the more it fixes and the more it can take with it. Removing the mean direction is nearly free and nearly always helps. All-but-the-top is the cheapest real repair and the bluntest — it kills exactly the axes you named by hand, along with whatever else those axes were carrying, and on some word-similarity surveys the score goes down afterwards, which is the honest evidence that the top ridges are not purely frequency. Whitening spends the most: it buys even spread across the sheet by giving up how tightly true pairs sit on the same spot. Contrastive training is the only rung that improves both at once, and it charges you a training run for the privilege.

The reason to keep the rungs apart is that they age differently. Centring is a statement about where the crowd sits; whitening is a statement about the shape of the crowd, and the shape is where a modern press has been putting its meaning. That is the turn waiting a little further along this survey — and it is why "I centred, therefore I whitened" is not a small slip of vocabulary but a decision to run the widest tool on the ladder without knowing it.

Two rules for an honest survey

Whitening is a repair: I take a map already drawn on a lying projection and re-work every line until the grid comes out even. But there is a bolder cartography — don't draw the crooked map at all. Survey the world true from the first day, so isotropy is woven into the paper instead of ironed in afterwards. That is what a contrastive loss does: it teaches the vectors, while they are still forming, to spread across the whole globe.

Wang & Isola (2020) named the two commandments of such a survey. Alignment — a place and its own likeness (two readings of the same passage, a query and its true answer) must land on the same dot, so I pull every matched pair together. Uniformity — every other town should be spread evenly over the sphere, never crowded into a wedge; and even spread over a sphere is exactly isotropy. Pull matches together; push everything else apart, evenly. SimCSE (Gao 2021) manages it with a trick so cheap it feels like cheating: run one sentence through the encoder twice with different dropout, call those two the matched pair, and treat every other sentence in the batch as a stranger to push away.

And it works from the inside. SimCSE lifts unsupervised sentence similarity to 76.25 and supervised to 81.57 on STS (Gao 2021). Compare each against its own previous best, though: \(72.05\) without a teacher, \(79.39\) with one — so the honest gains are \(+4.2\) and \(+2.2\), not the \(9.52\) you get by putting the supervised number next to the unsupervised baseline. And here is the twist a mapmaker should not skip: SimCSE does flatten the singular spectrum — but so do BERT-flow and BERT-whitening, harder, and they still lose by ten points. So flattening is not what wins. Whitening buys uniformity by spending alignment, and alignment is what the task measures. Isotropy is a diagnostic, not a goal.

The same repair, the opposite sign

Now I owe you the hardest sentence in my whole survey, and it is about my favourite tool. Back when the maps came off a raw press, the tallest ridges really were frequency artefacts — bookkeeping the press had scribbled over the geography — so erasing them cost nothing and the ruler got its range back for free. Since then the presses changed. A contrastively trained encoder has already been taught to spread its towns over the sheet, and in the course of that training it spent those same dominant directions on meaning. Erase them now and you are not cleaning the map, you are shaving off what the press worked hardest to draw.

The measurements say it plainly. Run full whitening across today's deployed embedders and the score comes out negative on every one of them, and the loss grows the wider the sheet is. The one niche where it still wins is the family that was never trained contrastively at all — some code encoders, for instance — and even there it needs a regulariser to stop it collapsing the space, and the same transductive question applies: whose corpus were \(\mu\) and \(W\) fitted on? Fit them on the very sentences you then score, and you have measured your own head start.

So the rule that survives 2026 is narrower than the one 2021 taught: remove the mean direction; do not whiten the covariance. The first moment is still an artefact on almost any map. The shape is not. And take the general lesson with you, because it outlives this chapter: a repair is not a property of the map, it is a property of the press the map came off. Ask what drew your encoder before you decide what to undo.

Two ways to order the same axes

A reader who has followed the survey this far should now object. I told you the tallest ridges of the map sort towns by how often each is spoken — so the leading axes are the least meaningful thing on the sheet. Yet the modern habit is matryoshka representation learning: chop a vector down to its first coordinates, keep only the head of it, and retrieval still works. Both cannot be true of the same axes.

They are not the same axes. There are two different ways to order one space, and they answer different questions. Principal components order by variance — an ordering discovered after the fact, on a map already drawn, which is why its leading directions can turn out to be bookkeeping. The matryoshka ordering is imposed during the drawing: the press is told, on every pass, that a short prefix of each vector must stand on its own, so it packs the coarse distinctions in first and the fine ones after. The leading coordinates are not found important; they are made important. One space, two orderings, no contradiction.

Two practical notes and one open corner. Truncation is a compression dial, and it is not free: cut a little and you lose a little, cut hard and the loss steepens — and after cutting you must re-normalise to unit length, or your ruler drifts on the very vectors you shortened. And the open corner, which is genuinely unclaimed: nobody has yet surveyed what the leading matryoshka coordinates actually encode. We know they carry the coarse-grained distinctions; we do not know in what language. That measurement is a good evening's work and nobody has done it.

Put your own encoder on the plane

Alignment and uniformity are not just vocabulary for reading somebody else's paper — they are two numbers you can compute on your own press this afternoon. Alignment is the mean squared distance between the two halves of a pair that ought to coincide, \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,x^+)}\lVert f(x)-f(x^+)\rVert^{2}\); smaller is better. Uniformity is \(\log \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\, e^{-t\lVert f(x)-f(y)\rVert^{2}}\) over random pairs; more negative means better spread. Both on unit-length vectors, both a few lines, and together they place your encoder as a single point on one plane.

Read the quadrant and the diagnosis reads itself. Poor uniformity with good alignment is the raw-press corner — the crushed wedge, true pairs sitting tight, everything else piled on top of them. That is the corner where a re-projection has room to help, because it is buying the thing you are short of. Good uniformity with poor alignment is the opposite corner, and there a re-projection actively hurts: it spends alignment to buy spread you already have. What that corner needs is supervision — pairs that tell the press which towns belong together — and no amount of linear algebra will substitute for them.

And this is finally why the tasks and the diagnostics kept disagreeing. A re-projection can flatten the spectrum harder than a contrastively trained press does and still lose to it by a wide margin, because the task is scoring alignment while the diagnostic is scoring spread. So the plane, not a single evenness number, is the instrument for the only question that matters here: does this repair apply to me? Measure your two coordinates first; the ladder of repairs is a menu, and the plane tells you which half of it you are allowed to order from.

All roads lead to Rome

The wedge was one lie of the paper. Here is the second, and it hides in the roads, not the land. Pick a town \(x\) and ask a stranger's question: of all the other towns, how many list \(x\) among their \(k\) nearest? Count that — Radovanović called it \(N_k(x)\), the reverse-neighbour tally — and you have measured not how many friends a town keeps, but how many strangers keep it.

On a small map the count is fair: everyone is somebody's neighbour a handful of times, no more. But as I add dimensions to the paper, the tally goes crooked. A few towns start turning up on everyone's shortlist — the hubs — while a long tail of towns appear on none of these lists — the anti-hubs, marooned in the margins. And the skew is not gentle: on plain uniform land it climbs from \(0.121\) at \(d=3\), to \(1.541\) at \(d=20\), to \(5.445\) at \(d=100\). All roads, it seems, lead to Rome. Read the small print with me, though, because it decides how much of this you should fear: those are \(S_{N_5}\) — neighbour lists of length five — measured on \(10\,000\) points under Euclidean distance, and Radovanović flags cosine as the exception in the very same figure. On normalised vectors the skew is markedly gentler. Gentler, not gone: measured on real cosine-space embeddings it still reads \(0.939\), which is quite enough to ruin a result list.

And Rome earns nothing to deserve it. In high dimensions every town is flung out onto a thin shell around the map's centroid — the mean of the whole crowd — all sitting at nearly the same radius. Whoever happens to fall closest to that centre is, by pure geometry, close to all of them at once; it becomes everyone's neighbour without meaning a thing to any of them. For retrieval this is poison: a handful of passages come back for every query I pose, while low-count documents warrant a coverage check on real queries; their sample counts alone do not imply lost recall.

Two dimensions, plain nearest-neighbour lookup. Every point keeps k = 5 neighbours, so the average N_k is exactly 5 whatever we do — only the SHAPE can break. Here it has not: the histogram is nearly symmetric, skew 0.0299, the busiest point sits on 9 lists, and just one point in 120 is on nobody's.

The same cloud drawing, the same k, only d = 20. The right tail grows a limb: skew 2.5904, one point now sits on 39 lists — nearly eight times the mean — and 12.5% of the corpus is on no list at all. Those hollow circles occur in none of these neighbour lists; a new query can still retrieve them.

Meet the hub: point #18, on 39 of 120 lists. It is not important, it is CENTRAL — the correlation between N_k and closeness to the centroid climbs from 0.45 at d = 2 to 0.81 here. In high dimension everything sits on a thin shell at nearly equal radius, so a sliver of extra centrality is enough to win everyone's list.

Zoom in on one query, point #19. Its raw top-1 is the hub #18 at distance 4.8211; the honest candidate #58 is at 5.0820. The hub genuinely IS closer — by 0.2609. On raw distance there is nothing to argue with, and that is exactly how a hub eats a result slot it did not earn.

Now charge the toll. The hub's own neighbourhood is 1.8829 tighter than the candidate's — r̄ = 3.6884 against 5.5713 — so it pays the bigger charge: CSLS 0.8979 for the hub against −0.4631 for #58, and lower wins. The rank flips by a margin of 1.3611. The hub did not move away; its crowd was billed.

The same rule over the whole cloud. The skew of N_k falls 2.5904 → 0.3923, the busiest point drops from 39 lists to 13, the anti-hub tail from 12.5% to 2.5%, and the hub, which used to be the top-1 answer for 9 queries out of 119, is now the top-1 for 3. Nothing was retrained; one line of the ranking rule changed.

The ledger of roads is conserved

Before I go chasing Rome, one line of bookkeeping that governs how every hubness number should be read. Each of my \(n\) towns writes exactly \(k\) names into its own list of neighbours — no more, no fewer, that is what \(k\) means. So the total number of listings on the whole map is \(\sum_{x} N_k(x) = k\,n\), always, whatever the geometry, whatever the width of the paper. Divide through and the mean of \(N_k\) is exactly \(k\). The average cannot move. It was fixed the moment I chose \(k\).

Which means hubness never creates traffic; it only redistributes a fixed total. Every extra road a hub collects is a road some other town no longer has. Rome and the ghost towns are the two ends of one ledger, and you cannot have the first without the second — the anti-hubs are not a separate misfortune that happens to coexist with hubs, they are the arithmetic shadow the hubs cast. That is the sharpest way to say why the silent loss and the loud error are one phenomenon rather than two.

Two consequences worth keeping on the card by the drafting table. Any statistic you quote must be about the shape of this distribution — its skew, its tail, its share of empty tallies — and never about its centre, because the centre is a constant you set yourself. And a cure that merely demotes hubs after the lists are drawn moves nothing in this ledger at all: the same listings, re-sorted. To change the ledger you have to change who gets listed, which is a different intervention entirely — and it is the trap the toll gate walks into a few beats from now.

Not the width of the paper — the width the data actually uses

The skew climbs with dimension — but look at which dimension the tally was taken over. Those readings come from points scattered with no structure at all, so the number of columns is the number of directions the crowd uses. Your index is not like that. The width of your vector is a line in a config file; the crowd inside it lives on far fewer directions than that, because real content is not scattered — it clusters, it repeats, it leans. Name the honest quantity: the intrinsic dimension, how many directions the cloud actually spends its spread on. That, and not the number in the config, is the quantity the roads answer to.

This joins two things the chapter has already told you separately. It is why the skew found in nature is spread over orders of magnitude between indexes whose vectors are all the same nominal width — they are not the same width where it counts. And it is the hidden machinery under the warning waiting a few beats ahead: flattening every axis hands the spread back to the directions the press had nearly erased, so the crowd starts using more of the paper than it did, and the roads get worse in exactly the proportion the geometry got rounder. Re-projection does not add a single column to the sheet; it changes how many of them the towns are willing to use.

Two habits fall out. Never predict your own skew from a model card — the width printed there is the one quantity guaranteed not to answer the question. And when you compare two encoders, remember that the wider one is not automatically the more hub-ridden one: the thin ridge of a wide sheet can behave like a narrow one, which is precisely why this chapter keeps sending you back to measure your own index instead of reading a table.

Why normalising softens the hubs — and what it leaves standing

I told you the skew reads milder on normalised vectors and asked you to take it on trust. Here is the mechanism, because it decides what you should expect to be left with. The road-count has two channels into it. One is radius: on a thin shell everything is nearly equidistant, so the town that happens to sit a sliver nearer the centre of mass is a sliver nearer to everyone, and it wins neighbour slots by the dozen. The other is direction: whom you point at. Divide every vector by its own length and the first channel is gone outright — every town is now exactly the same distance from the centre, and the sliver that decided everything cannot exist.

What survives is the second channel, and this is the part worth carrying out of the chapter. Ask which town has the highest average similarity to the whole crowd once lengths are equal: it is the one whose direction sits closest to the crowd's mean direction, because the average of a similarity over the crowd is a similarity to the average. So the hubs that remain on a normalised map are the towns aligned with the shared axis — which is the wedge of the first half of this chapter, read by a different instrument. The two diseases were never fully separate: one squeezes the scale, the other decides the membership, but on the unit sphere they are both answering to the same shared mean.

Three practical consequences, and none of them is a formula. Measure the skew on the vectors your index actually stores, because a Euclidean reading and a normalised one are not two estimates of one number. Expect the repair of the first act to move the reading of the third — subtract the crowd's centre and you have moved the very thing the surviving hubs were aligned to. And when you go looking for who your hubs are, start with the towns nearest the mean direction: my first ruler already flagged them, several pages before we had a name for the roads.

Anti-hubs: the ghost towns no road remembers

The hubs I have already found — all roads lead to their Rome. Now I turn to the other corner of the parchment, to the far margin, where a little town sits that no road remembers. I count its reverse-neighbours — \(N_k(x) = 0\): I run through every other town's list of its ten nearest, and this one's name appears on none of them.

The same skew that mints a Rome breeds these ghost towns too. In high dimensions almost every town is pressed onto a thin shell around the map's centre; the ones huddled near that centre become everyone's neighbour — the hubs — and the ones shoved out to the rim become no one's — the anti-hubs. Two tails of a single lopsided distribution.

An anti-hub is absent from these neighbour lists, not unreachable by all possible queries. A query near that document can still return it. If a known relevant document is missed, recall records the loss; incomplete judgments may hide it. Radovanović's Euclidean uniform-data example has \(S_{N_5}\) rising \(0.121\to1.541\to5.445\) for \(d=3\to20\to100\). Those finite-sample results motivate a diagnostic, not a theorem that every high-dimensional cosine index loses the same documents.

The ghost towns are the long tail

Who ends up in the margins is an empirical question. In some Euclidean clouds, peripheral points have fewer reverse neighbours. But an anti-hub is defined by a neighbour count, not by rarity, semantic correctness or distance from the centroid. Check those associations rather than assuming them.

Rare topics and unusual registers are worth inspecting among low-count documents. If they are overrepresented, test queries that should retrieve them. A document can have no reverse neighbours in a document sample and still be the best match for a new query. The risk to long-tail coverage must be measured on that query distribution.

Incomplete pooled judgments can miss relevant documents that participating systems never retrieved. Complete qrels expose known misses as false negatives and lower recall. Count N_k over the index sample as an additional diagnostic, record k and the metric, and combine the count with relevance judgments. Neither neighbour counts nor returned-result logs alone establish the entire loss.

Every road leads there — and it's the wrong town

Not every hub is a villain. A hub that truly means many things — a great capital that honestly trades with every province — has earned its roads. The danger is the bad hub: a town every traveller's compass points to, yet whose meaning has nothing to do with most of the people who arrive. It is close to everyone because it sits near the centroid; and it is wrong for almost everyone because its meaning mismatches theirs.

Watch what a bad hub does from my drafting table. In kNN it lands in the neighbour-list of hundreds of towns, and in each one it casts a vote — so it hands its own wrong label to hundreds at once, a single confident lie copied all across the map. The same poison seeps into retrieval: a handful of hub-passages surface for almost every query, no matter what was asked. Even a classifier trained on these neighbourhoods inherits the error.

A correct answer can be among the low-count documents, but its correctness is not implied by anti-hub status. The asymmetry concerns observability: returned wrong answers are easy to inspect, while misses need a relevance reference. Check N_k together with query-level recall. A local-crowding correction such as CSLS may improve this balance; its benefit still needs evaluation.

A hub is not an important town

There is a comfortable way to read a hub, and it is wrong. It goes: if a passage turns up in everybody's neighbour list, it is probably genuinely useful to everybody — let it come back, that is just PageRank with vectors instead of links. I have caught myself thinking it at this table. It is worth taking apart, because the whole toll gate of the next act depends on refusing it.

PageRank counts links somebody chose to draw: a citation, a recommendation, an act of endorsement by another town. The reverse-neighbour count \(N_k\) counts nothing of the sort. It counts who happened to fall nearest the centre of mass — an accident of where the crowd's middle lies, on a sheet nobody surveyed with that in mind. On my own toy the link between a town's busy-ness and its closeness to the centroid tightens as I add dimensions to the paper, until being a hub and being central are barely distinguishable statements. Centrality is not authority. And it shows in the damage: measured on real indexes, a natural hub genuinely does answer a small handful of queries and is returned for several times that many, so most of its traffic is simply wrong.

There is a darker corollary I would rather you heard from me than from an incident report. Because hubness is pure geometry, a hub can be manufactured: a vector built on purpose to sit near the middle of the crowd, planted in your index, will answer almost anything asked of it — far beyond what any naturally occurring hub achieves. Hubness is an attack surface, not just a quality defect. And the tolls of the next act are not a defence: they blunt the towns that are close to everyone, while a hub tuned to one concept survives them nearly intact.

The same hubs carry the traffic

Follow the roads into the fast index you built in L13 and you find the skew has already moved in ahead of you. A navigable graph is assembled out of neighbour lists — the very lists whose \(N_k\) we have just been counting — so a lopsided tally is literally a lopsided count of incoming roads. Hubs swell into super-nodes; a greedy walk stumbles onto that network within the first few percent of its steps and rides it. And the anti-hubs are the same sentence with the sign flipped: a town with almost no incoming roads is a town the walk never arrives at, which is why lowering the search effort loses the ghost towns first.

Here is the turn worth the whole act. That network of hubs is not only a defect — it is doing the work of the index's layer hierarchy. On large real collections you can remove the hierarchy outright, keep a single flat graph, and peak build memory drops sharply at the same recall and the same latency, because the hubs already supply the long jumps the upper layers were built to provide. Put crudely: in low dimension the hierarchy exists in order to manufacture hubs, because the geometry gives you too few. In high dimension the paper hands them over for nothing. The phenomenon that ruins the result list is the phenomenon that carries the traversal.

Which shows where the repair does NOT belong: cutting hubs out of the graph removes the highway your own search was riding. The full bill for that trade — memory, recall, latency — I will present in the beat on why retrieval breaks. What matters here is the second face of the coin: the stranded anti-hubs, the ones no road reaches, are also where an index's ugliest latency tails come from — a greedy walk that cannot get there wanders a long time before it gives up.

Hubness belongs to the index, not to the document

"This passage is a hub" sounds like a fact about the passage, and I want to break that habit before it costs you an afternoon. The reverse-neighbour tally is defined against the current crowd: it counts how many other towns in this index wrote this one into their lists. Delete a thousand documents, pour in a new domain, and the tally moves — while not a single vector has changed. The town did not move; the crowd around it did.

Three things follow, and each one has bitten somebody. An audit expires: the survey you ran last quarter describes a map that no longer exists. A corpus that grows unevenly manufactures hubs — pour in a new domain and its own centre of gravity drags the whole crowd's centre with it, so towns that happen to sit near the new middle start collecting roads they never had. And a hub identified on a public benchmark is not a hub in your index: the phenomenon transfers, the identities never do.

Which reframes the whole act, and this is the turn worth carrying out of it. Hubness is not a defect the model vendor can fix for you, because it is not a property of the model — it is a property of your corpus under this metric. No model card can promise its absence, and no vendor can test for it, because they do not have your crowd. So the diagnosis has exactly the same shelf life as the cure, and both live on your side of the fence. Before believing any statement about your map, ask which crowd was standing on it when the measurement was taken.

Anything you average drifts toward the centre — and the centre is where hubs are made

Hold the mechanism in one line: a hub is not an important town, it is the town nearest the crowd's centre. Now ask a question the chapter has not asked yet — who ends up near that centre by construction? Anything you built by averaging. Take a long, mixed document, cut it into chunks, encode each, and store the mean of the pieces as the document's vector: the idiosyncrasies of the parts point in different directions and partly cancel, while whatever they share survives the sum intact. What comes out sits closer to the middle of the crowd than any piece of it did. You did not choose a hub; you manufactured one with a pooling rule.

So the representation policy you set before the index exists is already a hubness policy. Store long heterogeneous documents whole, as an average, and they become the passages that answer everything — the loudest complaint in the logs, arriving from a decision made in the ingestion script rather than in the encoder. Keep the same document as separate chunks and each piece stays out on its own direction, where it can be wrong about a query instead of vaguely right about all of them. The same reasoning applies to every other averaging on the map: a centroid standing for a cluster, a user profile pooled over a history, a query expanded into the mean of its variants.

That gives the audit a second question to ask, and it costs nothing. When the skew comes back high, look at who the hubs are before reaching for the toll: if they are your longest and most mixed documents, the cheap repair is chunking, not a correction to the score — the crowding toll charges a symptom you can stop producing. This is also the seam where the next lecture picks the map up: everything RAG retrieves it retrieves with this ruler, and how it cut its texts up decided half of the geometry before a single query arrived.

CSLS: a toll for crowded towns

I stand over the map and follow the roads. They all seem to lead to Rome — a handful of hub towns that every other town writes into its list of neighbours. But Rome is not truly near each of them; it is merely crowded, planted where the land is dense, so the naïve ruler keeps electing it. Raw cosine rewards popularity. So I bolt a toll booth to every town's gate, and I set the toll by how busy that town's own doorstep is.

For each town \(x\) I first measure its local crowding: \(r(x)\), the mean cosine to its \(K=10\) nearest neighbours — large for a bustling hub, small for a quiet town in the margin. Then I re-price every road between \(x\) and \(y\) as \( \mathrm{CSLS}(x,y) = 2\cos(x,y) - r_T(x) - r_S(y) \): twice the road's plain closeness, minus each endpoint's toll. Conneau's cross-domain local scaling, 2018. A hub, whose \(r\) is large, now pays a heavy toll on every road it touches — it can no longer be close just because it is crowded. A genuinely near pair, both from quiet quarters, pays almost nothing and keeps its place.

Same globe, same vectors — I only changed how I read the ruler, correcting each reading for how packed its two ends are. In cross-lingual sentence retrieval — 2 000 query sentences against 200 000 candidates on Europarl — the score of a match climbed from \(P@1 = 42.6\) to \(66.1\) (Conneau 2018, Table 3); on the genuine word-translation pairs of their Table 1 the same swap buys a more modest \(77.4 \to 81.4\) for en-es — and some documents absent from the raw neighbour lists can enter the corrected lists. Note where this sits: whitening re-projected the paper to un-squash the cone; CSLS re-prices the roads to tame the neighbour graph. Two different repairs — one for the metric, one for the crowd — and I keep both in the satchel.

The toll, charged by hand: one rank, flipped

A formula you have not run on your own data is a rumour. So let me charge the toll once, by hand, on the same small survey I have been drawing all chapter — \(120\) towns, neighbour lists of five, and the density window \(K = 10\) that CSLS asks for.

Take one query town, and two candidates: the hub, and an ordinary town out in a quiet quarter. Raw distance says the hub wins — \(4.8211\) against \(5.0820\). It is genuinely closer, by \(0.2609\), and on a plain ruler there is nothing to argue with. That is precisely how a hub eats a slot it did not earn.

Now measure how packed each one's own doorstep is. The query's neighbourhood sits at \(\bar{r} = 5.0559\), the quiet town's at \(5.5713\) — and the hub's at \(3.6884\), which for context is nearly the tightest spot on the whole map (the mean across all towns is \(4.7083\); the tightest is \(3.6305\)). The hub is not merely near me. It is near everyone, because it is standing in the middle of the crowd.

So I re-price both roads: \( 2\,d(q,y) - \bar{r}(q) - \bar{r}(y) \), and now lower wins. The hub pays \(9.6422 - 8.7443 = 0.8979\); the quiet town pays \(10.1640 - 10.6272 = -0.4631\). The rank flips, with \(1.3611\) to spare. Watch how that margin was assembled: \(0.2609\) of distance in the hub's favour against \(1.8829\) of crowding against it. The hub did not lose by moving away. It lost because it lives in a crowd.

Run the same rule over every town and the whole shape of the map changes: the skew of the neighbour counts falls \(2.5904 \to 0.3923\), the busiest town drops from \(39\) lists to \(13\), and the marooned tail — towns on nobody's list at all — shrinks from \(12.5\%\) to \(2.5\%\). Nothing was retrained. Not one vector moved. I changed a single line of how I read the ruler.

Half the toll is inert — and that is a saving

Look again at the toll I wrote on the gate: \( 2\cos(x,y) - r_T(x) - r_S(y) \). It is symmetric, and symmetry reads like a promise that both charges are doing work. Rank the candidates of a single query and one of them is doing nothing at all. The query's own toll is the same number for every candidate on the list — subtracted from all of them alike — and a constant subtracted from every score cannot move a single place in the order. Formally, \(\arg\max\) over the full expression is the \(\arg\max\) over \( 2\cos(q,\cdot) - r(\cdot) \).

The query term is not decoration, though; it is load-bearing exactly where scores are compared across queries. Cut-off thresholds, deciding there is no acceptable answer at all, matching two sets against each other in both directions, inducing a dictionary between two languages — that last one is the setting the formula was written for, where both sides are crowds and both crowds need normalising. Notice where that lands us: the query-side charge is scale calibration, which is precisely the thing the cone was breaking in the first half of this chapter. Two acts, one defect, seen from opposite ends.

So the engineering consequence is a pleasant one for once. In an ordinary retriever — one query in, one ranked list out — precompute only the document-side toll over the corpus and skip the per-query density pass entirely. You lose nothing in the ordering, and you save a neighbour search on every single request.

One idea, a family of names

The toll gate is not one invention but a family, and every member charges the same idea: re-price a similarity by how crowded its two ends are. Local scaling divides each reading by the local scale of each side instead of subtracting it. DisSimLocal subtracts the squared local scale. Mutual proximity asks a different question entirely — what is the probability that two towns are each other's neighbours, rather than one merely listing the other. And the mutual-kNN graph does not touch the reading at all: it keeps a road between two towns only if both wrote the other into their list.

They differ in what they cost and in where they act. Most are quadratic in the size of the corpus, which is affordable as an offline pass; mutual proximity is cubic and simply does not survive a real index. The mutual-kNN graph is the odd one out and the most interesting for us, because it is a single symmetrisation sweep over the neighbour graph you already built in L13 — a repair of the roads themselves rather than of how I read the ruler. It is the only one that repairs the roads themselves, changing which edges exist rather than which candidates get re-sorted. The others reach an anti-hub only when applied <em>before</em> the candidates are picked — the next beat is about that.

A comprehensive comparison across dozens of collections leaves three habits worth keeping. There is no single winner: one method leads on the hubness statistic itself, another on neighbour-based classification, and which you want depends on what you are measuring. The skew found in the wild spans orders of magnitude, so the same cure is a rescue on one corpus and a no-op on the next. And below a modest threshold of skew, every member of the family buys you nothing at all — which is why the survey of your own index comes before the choice of cure, and why you must compare skews measured at the same neighbour-list length \(k\). A skew at one \(k\) and a skew at another are simply different quantities wearing the same name.

When the toll gate costs more than it collects

Every toll on this map is set from an estimate, and the estimate has an assumption inside it: who actually travels these roads. Measure the crowding with a bank of queries drawn from your own traffic and the gate earns its keep. Draw that bank from a foreign domain — someone else's queries, a different kind of question — and the corrected ranking comes out below doing nothing at all. Same formula, same code, worse than the bare ruler, because the crowd it was calibrated against never comes to town.

Then there are three bills the gate sends you in production, and none of them appears in the formula. One: the document-side toll is a full neighbour pass over the whole corpus — cheap the first time, a standing budget line every week after. Two: it goes stale. Add documents and the neighbourhoods shift underneath it, so the toll needs a refresh schedule like any other offline feature; there is no compute-and-forget. Three: the corrected score is no longer a cosine maximisation, and the graph and cell indexes of L13 cannot search it natively — they were built to walk toward the largest reading, and this is not that reading.

Which leaves two honest routes and one trap. Either rerank the raw top of the list after the index has spoken, or fold the document's toll into the stored vector or a per-document offset, if your engine will hold one. The trap lives in the first route: the anti-hubs are by definition the towns that never enter the raw shortlist, so a rerank-only deployment demotes the loud hubs and leaves the ghost towns exactly where they were. Half of what the toll is worth exists only where it touches the choice of candidates — which is the half a reranker can never reach.

Two repairs, one map

Back at the drafting table, I finally saw that my map had two faults, not one — and no single tool mends both. The cone is a fault of shape: the whole world crushed into a thin wedge, so my cosine ruler reads 0.99 between strangers. The hubs are a fault of the road network: a handful of 'Rome' towns every road bends toward. One is about how far apart I draw the towns; the other is about who gets listed as a neighbour.

Whitening — and contrastive training like SimCSE — is my re-projection. I center, un-stretch, un-shear until the grid is equal-area again, \( \tilde{x} = (x-\mu)W \). The wedge springs open into a full disk and the ruler recovers its range. But re-projecting does nothing about Rome: even on a perfectly even map, the town nearest the centroid is still near everyone.

So CSLS is a second, separate repair — a local-crowding toll, \( 2\cos(x,y) - r_T(x) - r_S(y) \), that charges a town for being popular so hubs stop swallowing every query. The part I was slow to accept: whitening can even raise hubness, because giving the space its dimensions back gives high-\(d\) geometry more room to breed hubs. So the honest map-work isn't 'pick one' — I re-project the paper and tax the crowds. Two fixes, two jobs, complementary.

Two repairs, one line of scoring

Let me put both repairs into a single line of scoring and see what each of them touches: \( s(q,d)=\cos(\tilde q,\tilde d)-\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\,r_K(\tilde q)+r_K(\tilde d)\,\big] \), with \(\tilde x=(x-\mu)W\). That is the toll of the last few beats with every term halved — which leaves the order of the list untouched — but taken on re-projected vectors. Both repairs are in; notice where each one entered. The re-projection sits inside the arguments; the toll sits outside the cosine.

That is the whole reason neither can stand in for the other. \(W\) is one and the same linear map for every point on the map; it never asks who a town's neighbours are, so it is applied once, at index time, and baked straight into the stored vector. \(r_K\) is a function of a neighbourhood, not of a point: two vectors standing in the very same place, but in regions of different density, are charged differently, and that reading does not exist at all until the neighbours are found. No fixed linear map of the space can reproduce a per-point offset that depends on the crowd. So read the line right to left: \(W\) fixes what is compared, \(r_K\) fixes how.

Two things follow, and I keep them on a card by the drafting table. Order: re-project first, measure crowding after — densities measured in the raw space belong to a space you are no longer searching, so a toll priced there is priced from the wrong map. And the symmetry of failure: zero out either term and exactly one of my two complaints walks back in. Drop \(r_K\) and every road bends toward Rome again; drop \(W\) and the ruler goes back to reading almost the same number between total strangers.

Mend one fault, inflame the other

I said the two repairs are complementary, and they are. Here is the footnote I owe you, and it is not a small one: re-projecting the paper can make the roads worse. Whitening rescales every axis to the same width — including the nearly-flat ones the press had squashed almost out of existence. Give those directions their variance back and the cloud's effective width goes up. I did not add a single column to the sheet, but the crowd now behaves as though I had.

And hubness grows with the width of the paper. That separation was drawn earlier in this survey and it pays for itself here: concentration and hubness are stories about \(d\), while the wedge is a story about how loud the shared part is against the individual part. So the operation that cures my crushed ruler hands high-dimensional geometry more room to breed Romes. Two faults on one map, and a repair that carries a plus sign for the first and a minus for the second.

Two rules come out of that, and I keep them on the same card as the ordering rule. Measure the skew after re-projection, never before — a skew read off the raw cloud belongs to a space you are no longer searching, exactly as the crowding toll must be priced on the map you actually search. And read the ladder of repairs with this in your hand: the widest tool moves the most in both directions at once. Which is one more reason the survey of your own index comes before the choice of cure, and not the other way round.

Three broken searches, one warped map

Come back to the drafting table with me, because now I can finally name what has been going wrong downstream. Every dense retriever we built reads relevance straight off this warped map. The bi-encoder of L10 scores a query against a passage with cosine — a ruler laid across the paper. But on my anisotropic Mercator every town is crushed into the same narrow wedge, so the ruler reads about \( 0.99 \) between total strangers. The honest signal — is this passage actually the answer? — survives only as a razor-thin sliver riding on that huge common offset. Rank by it and the true match and a hundred impostors sit within a hair of each other: false neighbours, near-ties, a score range too thin to trust.

It gets worse where the roads go. Hubness hands me a fistful of Rome towns that every route passes through — a few passages that appear in many sampled neighbor lists, relevant or not — while anti-hubs appear in none of those lists at the chosen k, which motivates testing whether real queries miss relevant documents. And the fast index of L13 doesn't escape this; it inherits it. The navigable graph you build for ANN wires its edges by these very distances, so hubs swell into over-connected super-nodes many walks may visit, and poorly connected regions may need more exploration. HNSW is not an exact kNN graph; sample N_k alone does not establish unreachability. There is a twist worth carrying, though: those over-connected super-nodes are also the graph's highway. Munyampirwa and colleagues (2024) showed the hubs do the same job HNSW's layer hierarchy does — drop the hierarchy and peak build memory falls by \(18\)–\(39\%\) with no loss of recall or speed. So do not go cutting hubs out of your index; charge them a toll at scoring time and leave the roads alone. Even the hard-negative mining of L11 drinks from the same poisoned well: mine your top-k with the current model and you keep dredging up the same hubs, training on the crowding artefact instead of on meaning.

The useful connection is the map: anisotropy, hubness and search errors can interact, but they are not one proven cause with one guaranteed cure. Whitening changes the covariance structure; CSLS adjusts scores for local crowding. Either can change the neighbor lists and let some previously absent documents enter them. That is a hypothesis to test, not a promise that all anti-hubs or false neighbors disappear. Measure relevance, recall and latency before and after the change, and check rare-topic queries separately. A more uniform map is not automatically a more useful search engine.

Audit your own index in twenty lines

Everything in this chapter is a claim about your map, and none of it is worth a thing until you have measured yours. So here is the survey, small enough to run this afternoon. Sample \(10\,000\) vectors from the index. Then four numbers.

One — mean cosine of random pairs. Sample pairs and average, recording the sampling uncertainty. High mean cosine can compress the score scale. It is invariant to a common rotation but can change under translation at fixed covariance. Keep it distinct from covariance isotropy and from the paper's score \(1-|\overline{\cos}|\), whose Table 3 estimates span \(0.97\)–\(0.99\) while IsoScore is \(0.216\).

Two — the reverse-neighbour counts. For each sampled vector, ask how many of the others list it among their \(10\) nearest. That is \(N_k\), and its mean is exactly \(10\) no matter what — so look at the shape, never the average.

Three — the skew of that distribution, \(S_{N_{10}}\). This is your one actionable number. Across \(50\) real datasets it runs from \(-0.1156\) to \(15.5188\), and the comparison of twelve correction methods found a plain threshold: below about \(1.4\) there is nothing worth treating. Above it, a density correction at query time is likely to pay. Note the subscript and keep it: the threshold is stated for lists of ten, while the skews quoted earlier in this chapter are \(S_{N_5}\) — skews only compare at equal \(k\), which is why step two counts the ten nearest and not the five.

Four — the fraction with \(N_k=0\). These documents occur in none of the sampled neighbour lists at this k. A future query can still retrieve them. Inspect their topics and compare with representative query judgments. Known missed relevant documents reduce recall; incomplete judged pools may hide others. This coverage diagnostic complements retrieval metrics.

Four numbers, one afternoon. Then, and only then, decide whether to touch anything — because on a modern contrastive embedder the answer is quite often no, and the most expensive mistake in this chapter is applying a 2021 repair to a 2026 map.

Audit the traffic, not only the map

Read the survey I just handed you one more time and notice whom it asked. Every road in it runs from a document to a document: I counted how many other towns of the index keep each town on their list. But nobody searches with a document. The roads that decide your evenings run from a query to a document, and a query is not a sample from the crowd you measured — it is short where documents are long, it is written in the voice of a person asking rather than a person explaining, and your encoder was trained to put those two kinds of text in the same space precisely because they do not look alike.

So a town can be a hub among documents and a nobody under real traffic, and — the direction that costs you money — a town can be perfectly ordinary among documents and still be the answer your live queries keep landing on. The fix is not a new theory, it is a change of probe: take a bank of real queries out of your logs, run the same count with those as the starting points, and read which towns collect the roads that actually exist. That is the same bank the crowding toll wants for its own calibration, which is convenient — one artefact, two uses — and it comes with the same warning: a bank from someone else's domain describes traffic that never arrives at your gate.

Keep both readings, because they answer different questions and the pair is the honest picture. The document-to-document count is a property of the map, which is what you must re-measure after any repair; the query-to-document count is a property of the map plus the way it is used, which is what your users experience and what moves when the product changes without a single vector moving. And the silent half survives the change of probe unaltered: a town no query reaches is still invisible, still not an event, still countable only from the index side.

Straighten the map

Stand back with me at the drafting table and take in the whole map at once. The space of meaning is not the flat paper it pretends to be — it is a warped projection, and two distortions live in the warp. Anisotropy squashes every town into one narrow wedge, so the cosine ruler reads a smug \(0.99\) between total strangers; hubness funnels all the roads into a few Rome towns that turn up as everyone's neighbour, while lonely anti-hubs sit unvisited in the margins. On a map this curved, straight-line distance simply lies.

So I stop trusting the ruler and fix the map. Re-project it: center it (subtract the mean), then whiten — un-stretch and un-shear the axes until the grid is even and distances mean what they say. Or train it honest from the start: a contrastive loss (SimCSE) flattens the spectrum and builds isotropy in. And because whitening straightens the cone but not the neighbour graph, I add CSLS — a local-crowding toll, \(2\cos(x,y) - r_T(x) - r_S(y)\) — so a town is never close just because it is crowded. Popularity is not closeness.

Straighten the map and the geometry finally tells the truth — and everything downstream wakes up. The bi-encoders of L10, the ANN index of L13, the hard-negative training of L11 all lean on one quiet promise: that near means similar. Center, whiten, tax the crowding, and that promise holds. And the next chapter stands directly on the straightened sheet: in L15 the passages this map hands back go to an Oracle, and an Oracle can only answer from what the map let through. Straight-line distance lies on a curved map — so straighten the map. Thank you for surveying it with me.
