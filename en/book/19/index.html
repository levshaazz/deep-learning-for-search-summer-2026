The answer is in the corpus, and search walks past it

A reader comes to me asking about kittens: котята играют — do we have anything? Of course we do; the answer is right there in the corpus, a line that reads котёнок играет с клубком — a kitten plays with a ball of yarn. Same meaning, plainly. Yet send that query to the index and the retriever sails straight past the answer, as if it were invisible.

Here is the sting. In English I would have gotten away with this: play and plays share the stem play, so a keyword index mostly matches. But Russian does not forgive. котята and котёнок share no surface string at all — the root itself changes letters — and neither do играют and играет. To a bag-of-strings scorer these two texts are strangers. The answer exists, and search cannot see it.

That is the whole ache of this chapter, and the whole cure. Everything we tuned up to L18 — the chunk boundaries, the moment we pool, the fused arms — quietly assumed a language whose words hold still. Russian does not, and it charges for the assumption at every stage of the same pipeline. I don't need a smarter model — I need to stop pretending Russian is English. Fold the forms to one shape, pick a model that saw Russian, and mind a thousand small orthographic cuts. Morphology strikes back; the same move beats it three times.

One decision at the door, the bill at the exit

Before the grammar of the language, the grammar of the map. This hour lights Get Data and Rank, and those two are not two topics — they are one causal chain seen at its two ends. Russian morphology makes you decide something at the door of the Archives, and the whole price of that decision is collected later, at the moment results come back. Hold the chain and the three acts stop looking like a tour of unrelated troubles.

Get Data is the door. Before anything can be found, morphology forces an answer to a question English let you skip: what counts as a term at all? How many pieces a word breaks into, whether котята and котёнок are filed as one entry or two, which normalizer folds the forms and how far it folds them — every one of those is a decision about what the Archives will store. Rank is the exit, where that decision is paid for. An index that files the forms apart has nothing to bring back and therefore nothing to order; a model that never saw Russian scores the wrong passage above the right one; and the third act's cures — an embedder that has read the language, both arms normalized the same way, the arms fused, a reranker put over the pair — are all decisions about what comes back and in what order. Same chain: a choice about terms upstream, an ordering downstream.

Now the neighbour that looks nearest and stays dark. A whole act of this chapter is about benchmarks — translated test sets, the length regime, the leaderboard row you must read the fine print of — and that is exactly where a reader wants to light Measure. Don't. Measure is lit by an hour that hands you a new instrument or a new way of judging a system; this hour hands you neither. It teaches you to distrust a reading taken with the instrument you already own, on a corpus that reached you through a translator. Fine print on a ruler is not a new ruler. Generate stays dark for a plainer reason: nothing here answers anybody — we are still only finding. Keep the two tests together: does the hour change what the Archives hold, or what order the results arrive in? If neither, look harder before you light a stop.

Russian is fusional: the ending carries the grammar

The reason is a fact about the language. Russian is fusional: a single ending fuses several grammatical values at once. The -ами in запросами encodes both the instrumental case and the plural in one morpheme, and stems alternate as they inflect (котёнок → котята). A noun spans 6 cases × 2 numbers; a verb adds person, tense, aspect and mood — one verb, dozens of forms.

English is weakly inflected with a visible stem — cat/cats, play/played/playing — which is exactly why lexical search mostly works in English and quietly fails in Russian. In Russian the root often hides behind the ending, so the string a user types and the string the corpus stored are simply different.

Everything downstream inherits this one fact. The tokenizer trips on the endings (the token tax), the keyword index cannot match across forms (the surface-form miss), and even the choice of embedder turns on whether the model ever saw those endings in training. Three bills, one cause.

One lexeme, one dictionary entry, and an empty index. In English this word would be one or two index terms. Watch what Russian does to it.

For a noun: six cases times two numbers. Two cells repeat — nominative and accusative coincide in both numbers — so twelve slots yield ten different strings. Ten, from one dictionary entry. Switch the part of speech and it only gets worse.

The index does not know they are relatives. Each string becomes its own postings list, and each list holds exactly one document. Count the terms one lexeme costs — and every one of them carries a fraction of the frequency it should.

A user types the word in one oblique form. It matches exactly one list — and every other document, about exactly the same thing, stays invisible.

Lemmatisation on. Every form is looked up in a dictionary and written into the index under one and the same lemma — the ten lists converge onto a single one.

The same query, normalised the same way, now reaches every document. One term per lexeme instead of a whole handful — that is the whole trick, and the segment control shows a verb and an adjective are worse still.

The paradigm is bigger than the table

The grid you just climbed — six cases by two numbers — is a teaching floor, not the language. Russian keeps live forms outside that box. The locative, the second prepositional: в лесу is not о лесе, one cell of the table and two different strings. The partitive: налить чаю stands beside вкус чая, a quantity form living next to the genitive. And a productive colloquial vocative — Саш, мам — which is exactly what people type into a search box and a chat window.

And the noun is the easy case here. The verb играть lists more distinct forms than the paradigm above, and the adjective новый more still, once you count gender, number, case, short forms and degrees of comparison. So the count of index terms one lexeme turns into is a lower bound, not a total — and the inverse document frequency splinters further than the table led you to believe.

This is why the cure cannot be a list of endings. You will never finish that list; you will only ship a stemmer that knows the cases you happened to remember, while the residue keeps leaking recall quietly. Fold to the lemma and the residue folds with it — one rule instead of an enumeration you cannot close.

Three ways to carry grammar

Fusionality is not difficulty, it is a packing scheme — and this book happens to be written in three languages that pack grammar three different ways. Fusional Russian melts several values into one ending you cannot take apart: the -ами of запрос·ами is instrumental and plural at once, so the whole ending has to come off in a single move. Agglutinative Tatar strings them instead: китап·лар·ым·да is book + plural + my + locative, one meaning per morpheme, peeled off left to right by rule. Analytic English hands the grammar to separate words — of the book, in my books — which is exactly why lexical search just works there.

Now read the same three rows through the index and the tokenizer. English smears a lexeme's document frequency over a form or two, Russian over about a dozen, and Tatar produces more forms still — and yet a rule-based stripper does noticeably better on Tatar, because its seams are regular and sub-word merges land on real morphemes more often. Many forms and unsplittable forms are two different diseases. That contrast, not the form count, is why lemmatization beats naive tail-chopping in Russian.

So name your language's packing scheme before you reach for a tool. This course is taught in Innopolis, where the language next door is Tatar — not exotica, just a neighbour that breaks differently: more forms, cleaner seams, a larger token bill, an easier stemmer. The diagnosis is per language; the discipline is one for all of them.

Agreement multiplies the miss

One more thing the paradigm table hides, and it is the thing that turns an inconvenience into a hole. In Russian a noun phrase inflects as a unit: the adjective takes the case of its noun, the noun takes the case the sentence assigns it, and often the verb agrees in number and gender on top. The grammatical choice is made once, for the whole phrase — and then written onto every word in it.

Read what that does to a query. A surface match needs every query term to appear as the same string in the document, \(q \cap d = q\), which looks like a product of independent risks, \(\prod_{t \in q} p_t\). It is not. The draws are perfectly correlated: if the document's phrase stands in a different case than yours, every term misses, in the same instant, for the same reason. So the failure is not a product of small independent slips — it is one slip, copied across the phrase.

And this reverses an intuition you probably carry from English. There, a longer query degrades gracefully: some terms match, the sum keeps a few contributions, the document holds a rank. Here it degrades off a cliff — the more words you add to be precise, the more of them fall together. The cure inherits the same shape and that is the good news: fold each token to its lemma and the whole phrase folds at once, because what varied was one decision, not many. Lemmatization buys more on a long query than a word-by-word view predicts.

The token tax

The first bill is the token tax. BPE (from L2) learns its merges by pair frequency in a training corpus that is overwhelmingly English, so it accumulates rich English sub-word merges and few Russian ones. When it meets a Russian word it has no merges for, it falls back toward characters — and Cyrillic's two-byte UTF-8 encoding makes a byte-level tokenizer start from even smaller pieces.

I trained a tiny character BPE on a 9:1 English-to-Russian mix and ran it on a parallel sentence. English search stayed one token; Russian запроса fell all the way to its seven characters. Across the sentence, English cost 1.0 tokens per word and Russian 5.8 — a ×2.9 tax on the same content. The exact ratio is our toy's; production multilingual tokenizers pay a smaller but real premium, often around two tokens per word.

This tax is quiet but real. More tokens per word means less meaning fits a context window, higher per-token API cost, and quadratic attention latency — all borne by the Russian user for the same query. It is a price signal, not an accuracy verdict, but it makes the tokenizer a first-class criterion when you pick a model for Russian.

The vocabulary arc

Before you accept that bill, look at how it moved. The tax is not a law of nature — it is a vocabulary budget, and three OpenAI tokenizers measured on the same parallel corpus (FLORES-200) show it being repaid. GPT-2's r50k spends 7.773 tokens on a Russian word against 1.227 on an English one — a ×5.74 premium. cl100k_base (GPT-3.5/4) cuts that to 3.388 against 1.233, ×2.49. o200k_base (GPT-4o) cuts it again to 1.915 against 1.220, ×1.42. Two vocabulary refreshes, and three quarters of the tax is gone.

Open the vocabulary and the mechanism is embarrassingly literal. In r50k_base exactly 17 tokens contain a Cyrillic character, and not one of them holds two Cyrillic characters in a row — the entire Russian budget is seventeen lonely letters. cl100k has 729 such tokens; o200k has 14,211. And the fallback to letters starts from twice the material: the same content needs only ×1.089 as many characters in Russian as in English, but ×1.984 as many bytes, because every Cyrillic letter costs two of them in UTF-8.

Two rows of that table are worth carrying in your head. XLM-R's 250k vocabulary — shared verbatim by BGE-M3 and multilingual-E5 — pays ×1.17, so one number covers all three and ties this act to the dense encoders of act three. And ruBERT, trained on Russian, pays ×0.67: there Russian is cheaper than English. A native tokenizer does not soften the tax, it inverts it.

One honesty note before you price anything. These are measurements on FLORES-200, cross-checked against Petrov et al. (arXiv:2305.15425) and the byte-premium study of Arnett et al. (arXiv:2403.00686). Claude publishes no tokenizer, and its own documentation warns that counting with tiktoken under-counts — most of all outside English. Do not extrapolate this table onto a model whose vocabulary you cannot open.

It is not the Cyrillic

The single most common belief about Russian search is that the alphabet is to blame — and it is a wrong diagnosis, which is worse than no diagnosis, because it buys a wrong cure: a transliteration pipeline, a forced ASCII folding, an encoding audit that finds nothing. BM25 is alphabet-agnostic. It tests string equality and does not care which bytes sit inside the string.

Falsifying the myth takes a minute, and it is worth doing once with your own hands. Romanize the corpus and the query together and run the whole thing again: kotyata still misses kotenok, exactly as котята missed котёнок, and the token tax does not go away either — those letter sequences are not English words either. Two mechanisms broke your search, and neither of them is the script: the skew of the tokenizer's training mix, and the fusionality of the language.

Keep those two apart, because they are cured at different stages by different people. The vocabulary is decided when you choose a model — this act, and in practice a procurement decision. The forms are folded when you build the index — the next act, and in practice one line in an analyzer. The alphabet changes the bytes, not the retrieval.

When the tax doesn’t bite

Before you carry the token tax into every design meeting, mark where it stops mattering. A two-word query costs a few extra tokens and nothing else; the tax bites on long documents, large corpora and per-token billing, not on one-word search. And more tokens is not worse understanding — a model can read Russian perfectly well while paying more to read it. The tax is a price signal, not an accuracy verdict, and confusing the two will make you swap a good model for a cheap one.

One piece of hygiene, while we are being honest about bills. The fifteen-fold premium that circulates next to every discussion of Russian tokenizers is not Russia's number: in the paper everyone cites it is the worst case across all languages, and it belongs to Shan, while the Russian row sits in an appendix. Quote it beside a Russian corpus and you overstate the bill several times over — a strange thing to do right after watching that same bill shrink from one vocabulary to the next.

And fragmentation has an upside that the third act will come back to collect. When one word breaks into pieces, its own inflected forms end up sharing pieces — and a retriever that scores by vectors instead of strings uses exactly that. Tokenosaurus's mouthful of crumbs turns out to be a soft match waiting to happen. So Act 1 closes with a bill, not a wound; the real damage of morphology is next, and it is an index that cannot match forms at all.

The same tax was paid during training

The token tax is always told as an inference bill — context window, price per token, quadratic attention. But look at the order in which a model is built. The tokenizer is frozen first, and only then does the training run begin, reading the corpus through it. So the same fragmentation was applied to every page the model ever learned from, long before you sent it a query.

And a training budget is counted in tokens. A language that costs several tokens per word therefore buys proportionally less text out of the same budget: fewer sentences, fewer facts, fewer chances to see a construction twice. Worse, the pieces themselves are unstable — when a word has no merges of its own, where it breaks depends on what surrounds it, so the model gets fewer repetitions of any single unit to attach a meaning to. That is a data problem wearing the costume of a cost problem.

Which is why the tokenizer belongs in the first round of model selection and not in the capacity-planning spreadsheet. Open the vocabulary before you run a single evaluation and you already know something about whether this encoder had a chance to learn the language. Hold that thought: act three states the same fact from the other end — the encoder must have seen Russian — and this is the mechanism by which it either did or did not.

Fertility is a ratio — read its denominator

You are told to measure the fertility of your own corpus with your own tokenizer, and that is right — but a ratio is only as honest as its bottom half. Fertility is tokens per word, and word is not a primitive: it is itself the output of a segmentation you chose. Split on whitespace and every punctuation mark rides along with its neighbour. Split the way a regular-expression library defines a word and hyphenated compounds come apart, so интернет-магазин counts as two words and the same token count is divided by a bigger number. The ratio moves, and the tokenizer has not changed at all.

The numerator carries assumptions too. Most tokenizers mark a leading space on the piece that starts a word, so how you treat the boundary between words changes the count; the special tokens at the ends of a sequence are a fixed surcharge that inflates the ratio on short texts and disappears on long ones. And the number is not a property of a language but of a text: officialese full of long derived nouns, chat full of clipped forms and Latin brand names, and a product catalogue that code-switches every other line will each report a different fertility from one and the same tokenizer, because they are made of different words.

So measure it the way you are going to spend it. If the bill you care about is a context window, count tokens per document of your own corpus and forget words entirely — the denominator you actually pay against is the passage, not the lexeme. Quote a per-word ratio only when you are comparing two tokenizers on the same text, which is the one job it does well. A fertility number without its corpus and its word rule attached is the same species of claim as a leaderboard row without its split: it names a quantity, and not the one you are about to spend.

Surface forms miss: BM25 rewards only a shared string

The second bill is worse, and it lands in BM25 (from L3). BM25 sums the contribution of terms the query and document share — its whole score is a sum over t ∈ q ∩ d. No shared string, no term, no contribution. On a fusional language the query and the answer routinely share nothing, so the true answer scores exactly zero and never even enters the candidate set.

Worse than a miss: the wrong document wins. A passage that happens to repeat the query's exact surface form — even one that is entirely off-topic — out-ranks the real answer. And inverse document frequency splinters: ten forms of one word become ten rare terms, so the frequency statistics smear and the weights lie.

The cure is to normalize before you index, symmetrically on both sides, so the query and the corpus fold to a common form and their terms can finally match. The next beats measure exactly how much that changes, by hand.

It is not rank two — it is not a candidate

Let me say the previous claim the honest way, because the sloppy version is the one that survives into an exam answer. Retrieval is two stages, not one. First candidate generation: the engine unions the postings lists of the query's terms, \(\mathcal{C}(q) = \bigcup_{t \in q} \mathrm{postings}(t)\). Only then does the scorer run, and it runs over that set.

Now put the fusional miss into that machine. The query term is котята; on surface forms no document in the index ever produced that string, so \(\mathrm{df}(\text{котята}) = 0\), so \(\mathrm{postings}(\text{котята}) = \varnothing\). The gold document is not scored low — it is not in \(\mathcal{C}(q)\) at all, so it has no rank to be low at. When a toy run reports it at "rank two", that is a stable sort among documents that all scored exactly nothing; the honest word is not retrieved, and it is the stronger story.

Two things follow that are worth more than the wording. No setting of \(k_1\) or \(b\) can rescue this, because those tune how a shared term is weighted, and here there is no shared term to weight — you cannot re-price an empty sum. And normalization does not edit the ranking formula at all: it edits which strings become dictionary terms, upstream of both stages. That is why the repair belongs in the analyzer and not in the scorer, and why it must run identically on both arms — a term that exists in one arm's dictionary and not the other's is an empty postings list all over again.

Normalization does not touch the formula — it rewrites its evidence

The previous beat said normalization edits which strings become dictionary terms, upstream of both stages. Now follow that edit downstream, because it does not stop at the postings lists. BM25 is a function of three measured quantities: how often a term occurs in a document, in how many documents it occurs at all, and how long the documents are. Folding the forms moves all three, and not one character of the ranking formula changes.

Take the document frequency first, because that is where the miss stops being a gap and becomes a lie. Inverse document frequency falls as \(\mathrm{df}\) rises: a term found in few documents is treated as informative. Split one lexeme across a dozen forms and every form is rarer than the lexeme is, so each of them is handed an idf it has not earned — and a document that happens to use an unusual case form outranks one that uses the ordinary form, for a reason that has nothing to do with what either of them says. Fold, and the frequencies of the forms add up into the frequency of the lexeme; the weight falls back to the one the word deserves. Then the length: dropping stop words and chopping endings shortens every document, so \(|d|\) and the average document length both move, and the length normalization that \(b\) governs is now computed against a corpus that no longer exists in the shape it was tuned on.

Two consequences you can act on. A \(b\) or a \(k_1\) fitted before you changed the analyzer is fitted to statistics the change deleted — re-tune after, never before, and treat that pair as part of the normalization stack rather than as constants. And the general form, worth more than either: normalization is not a filter standing in front of the ranking, it is a change to the evidence the ranking stands on. The function is the same function. You replaced its inputs with measurements taken in a coarser alphabet, and no amount of staring at the formula will show you that, because the formula looks identical either way.

Two ways to make the strings meet

The miss is a failure of two sets to intersect, and an intersection can be repaired from either side. The move this chapter takes is folding: send every form of a lexeme to one term, at index time and at query time, through the same code. There is a second, older move worth naming, because it is the one you reach for when folding is unavailable — expansion: leave the index in surface forms and rewrite the query into the forms of each word, котёнок alongside котёнка, котята, котятам, so the intersection is repaired by making the query wider instead of making the terms coarser.

They are not equivalent, and the previous beat says why. Folding produces one term carrying the lexeme's honest document frequency. Expansion produces a disjunction of terms that each keep their splintered frequency, so the rare form still carries an unearned weight and the score becomes sensitive to which forms you happened to generate. Expansion also grows with the paradigm — a phrase in which every word inflects turns into a product of alternatives — and it needs exactly the morphological knowledge the folding would have needed. It is not the cheaper option. It is the later one.

What it buys is that the index is left alone, and that matters precisely when you cannot rebuild it: a corpus you do not own, an analyzer you may not change, a field that must stay verbatim so quotations and highlighting still work. Read it as the general shape of the rule rather than an exception to it. The requirement was never fold — it was make both sides meet in one alphabet, and either side may walk towards the other. Fold when you own the index; expand when you do not; do neither and the intersection stays empty; do one of them on one side only and you are in the anti-cure a later beat measures.

Stemming vs lemmatization

There are two ways to fold the forms. Stemming applies rules to chop an ending, producing a stem that need not be a real word (Snowball/Porter for Russian): cheap, context-blind, and prone to over- or under-stemming that conflates unrelated words. Lemmatization maps to the dictionary form using a morphological dictionary, and it can disambiguate homonyms — стали is steel in one reading and became in another.

For BM25, stemming is the fast default and lemmatization the precision option, worth its cost when exact meaning matters and homonymy must be resolved. Russian has a mature, CPU-cheap toolbox for both: Snowball, pymorphy2, Mystem, the spaCy/Stanza pipelines, and the built-in Elasticsearch/OpenSearch russian analyzer.

The one non-negotiable rule, whichever you pick: apply the same normalization to the query and to the corpus. Normalize the index but not the query — or the reverse — and you reintroduce the very mismatch you were trying to remove.

Inside the stemmer

Let me open the cheap tool and show you its insides, because chop the ending is not an algorithm. Snowball Russian first marks three regions — RV (everything after the first vowel), R1 (after the first non-vowel that follows a vowel) and R2 (the same rule applied inside R1) — and then runs four steps: a perfective gerund, else a reflexive plus an adjectival, verbal or noun ending; then a stray и; then the derivational ост / ость but only if it lies in R2; then a doubled н, a superlative, or a final soft sign. Take запросами: RV is просами, R1 is росами, R2 is ами. Step one finds the noun ending ами inside RV, removes it, and the word lands on запрос — the lemma, for free, with no dictionary.

Now the counterexamples, all of them computed by our own port of the algorithm rather than remembered. One lexeme splits into three stems: котёнок → котенок, котята → котят, котят → кот. And unrelated lexemes fuse: стал, сталь and стали all collapse onto стал, while the infinitive стать wanders off to стат. The same tool glues different words together and tears one word apart, in the same pass. Note also that the textbook example everybody repeats — that aggressive stemming merges мир, мировой and мирный — is simply false: Snowball returns мир, миров, мирн, three distinct stems. The real merges are quieter: мир and мириться both become мир, and небо and нёбо both become неб.

By design Snowball leans towards under-cutting (under-stemming) — its authors say plainly that over-stemming hurts more. Segalovich measured the price of that lean in 2003: on the same corpus Snowball loses 487 valid conflations where Mystem loses 41. That is roughly a factor of twelve of recall left on the table, in exchange for not merging the bank with the jar.

And do not assume the dictionary lemmatizer is the cure, because it is blind to context. Run pymorphy3 on the isolated form том and the top parse is тот with probability 0.896 — the book, the volume, is the third reading at 0.006; стали becomes стать at 0.975. Build the index on top-1 lemmas and the query «первый том» is destroyed silently. The size of that blindness has been measured: PyMorphy2 scores 91.23 on the RNC test set, and PyMorphy2\* — an oracle allowed to pick the right parse from all the ones it produced — scores 94.08. Three points, and every one of them lies in disambiguation.

Folding is a one-way map

Whichever tool you picked, notice what kind of object it is. Normalization is a map \(\varphi\) from forms to terms, and it is many-to-one by design — that is the entire point, since a dozen forms have to land on one string before they can match. But a many-to-one map has no inverse. The moment the postings list stores \(\varphi(w)\), the form \(w\) is gone from the index for good.

So the index you search and the text you show a human are two different objects, and everything that needs the original string has to get it from somewhere else. Phrase queries need to know the words stood adjacent in that order; highlighting and snippets need the characters the user will actually read; a did you mean needs the misspelling, not its stem. The engineering answer is the same one every mature analyzer already implements: carry the token's position and character offsets alongside the normalized term, and keep the stored source document as the single truth for display.

And there is a deeper consequence that is easy to miss. After folding, every downstream statistic is computed in a coarser alphabet: document frequency counts terms, not forms, so two documents a human would tell apart by their wording are, to the ranker, the same evidence. That is the price you agreed to pay for recall — and it is exactly why the untouched exact-match field is not a nicety bolted on at the end but the other half of the design, the place where the forms you deliberately destroyed are still kept.

Choose the normalizer by four columns

The last few beats argued about two ideas; the shelf in front of you holds tools, and a tool is not chosen by whose lemma looks prettier. Four columns decide it, and exactly one of them is linguistic. Does it see the sentence, or only the word? Under what licence? Is anyone still maintaining it? And how many words a second does it manage? Write those four across the top and the Russian shelf sorts itself out: rule stemmers at one end, dictionary lemmatizers in the middle, and full syntactic pipelines — the only kind that reads context — at the far end.

Three traps hide in that table, and none of them appears in a tutorial. The most-cited Russian morphology library has an abandoned version and a maintained successor whose names differ by a single digit; teams copy the old import out of somebody's blog post and inherit code that stopped receiving fixes years ago. The fastest lemmatizer of the lot is a non-free binary whose licence forbids commercial use — it will sail through your benchmark and stop at the lawyer's desk. And Elasticsearch ships no Russian lemmatizer at all: the built-in russian analyzer is Snowball, a stemmer, so «we lemmatize, it is built into the engine» is almost always a sentence about stemming.

The speed column is the one people mistake for a footnote, and it is a design decision. A rule stemmer runs at tens of thousands of words a second, a dictionary lemmatizer several times slower, and a pipeline that genuinely parses the sentence slower again by an order of magnitude; that gap is the price of the context column. Now recall that the analyzer has to be one and the same in both arms, and the consequence falls into place: the query arm, where a live human is waiting, sets the budget for the index arm too. You cannot buy context for the corpus and economize on the query — that breaks the chapter's only rule. So pick the slowest tool you can afford on the query, and only then ask whose lemma is prettier.

The top parse is a prior, not a decision

Lemmatization was sold to you with one advantage a stemmer cannot have: it resolves homonymy — стали is both сталь and стать. Take an ordinary dictionary analyzer at its word and look at what the call actually returns. Not a lemma. A ranked list of parses, each with its own weight, and the weights were counted on a tagged corpus. Reaching for the first element is not disambiguation; it is the most probable parse of the isolated word, a prior that has never laid eyes on your sentence — and it is what nearly every indexing script in production does.

Watch what that costs on two perfectly ordinary Russian words. Том parses at the top as a pronoun, while the noun — a volume of a book — sits far down the list, so the query «первый том» is quietly rewritten into something no reader asked for. Стали parses as the verb, and the steel vanishes from the index of a metals catalogue. Notice the shape of the damage: not noise but a systematic bias — the same word loses the same sense every single time, so an entire class of queries goes dark at once, and nowhere does an exception get raised.

Measure the gap honestly and it names itself. Compare the top parse against an oracle allowed to pick the best parse out of the very list the analyzer already returned: the difference comes to a few points of lemma accuracy. That difference is precisely what context buys, and precisely what a context-blind tool will not sell you at any speed. So: if your domain lives on homonyms — legal, medical, brand names — a pipeline that reads the sentence earns its price, and you already know from the previous beat what that price is. If it does not, keep the cheap tool, but stop saying that it resolves homonymy, and keep the untouched keyword field beside it: that field is the one place in your whole index where том is still том.

By hand: lemmatization inverts the ranking

Come to my desk. I lay out five short Russian documents and the captain's query, котята играют, and I score it the naive way first — BM25 (k1=1.5, b=0.75) over the raw surface forms. The gold answer котёнок играет с клубком shares no surface string with the query, so its score is exactly \(0.0\). Meanwhile an off-topic decoy, дети играют во дворе, repeats the exact form играют and takes rank 1 with \(1.3608\). The true answer sits buried at rank 2, not even a real candidate.

Now I fold both the query and the documents to lemmas — котята → котёнок, играют → играть — and run the identical BM25. The gold document now shares both query lemmas: its score climbs to \(1.3885\) and it steps into rank 1, while a shop document that has only котёнок trails at second and the surface-winner decoy drops behind. Same corpus, same query, same BM25 — normalization alone flips the order.

That one inversion is the whole second act, measured. Nothing clever happened: I only changed what counts as the same string. On a fusional language, that is the difference between finding the answer and walking past it.

One query, five documents, nothing scored yet. Watch what a Russian corpus does to a matcher that only knows surface forms.

Mode one: raw surface forms. The query word and the document word are the same lexeme — and not one character-level match anywhere. The postings list the query asks for is empty.

Surface scores. The gold document is NOT RETRIEVED — it is not «ranked second», it never enters the result set at all. A document about children in a schoolyard takes first place instead.

Mode two: stemming. The verb forms collapse cleanly onto one stem — but the noun does not: the query form, the document form and the shop's form each land on a DIFFERENT stem. One lexeme, three stems.

Stem scores. Stemming pulls the gold document out of the void — but only the verb matched, so it still sits behind a document about a puppy playing with a ball. Better recall, wrong order.

Mode three: lemmatisation. Every form of the lexeme — the query's, the document's, the shop's — lands on one and the same dictionary lemma. Nothing splits.

Lemma scores. The gold document takes first place and the result set grows again. Read the price row: surface forms cost nothing, stemming costs a rule set, lemmatisation costs a dictionary — and that price is what buys you the right order.

Stemming doesn’t fix it

I called stemming the fast default, so let me run it on my own corpus before you take my word for it. Snowball, applied symmetrically to the query and to the same five documents. The query folds to котят игра. And the stem котят occurs in no document at all: the gold line stems to котенок, the pet-shop line to кот. One lexeme, three stems — exactly the alternation the Snowball beat opened up. Only the verb matches; the noun contributes nothing.

The ranking that comes out is the honest picture, and it is a split decision. Recall improves a lot: the answer is back in the list, and with it one more document the surface pass never reached. The order does not improve at all — a shorter, off-topic line about a puppy and a ball sits above the gold document, because the only term the two share is the common verb, and length normalization favours the shorter text. Better recall, wrong order, and the reader who looks at the first result is no better off.

Read the three passes as one row and the trade is legible: the gold document goes from absent, to present but not first, to first — and only the lemma pass puts it on top. The cost row is the counterweight: nothing, then rules, then a dictionary held in memory. Stemming is not the wrong answer. It is the cheap answer, and this particular stem alternation is what defeats it.

Where normalization lives in the pipeline

In a real lexical pipeline the normalization sits inside the analyzer. At index time the chain runs tokenize → lowercase → lemma/stem → stop-word removal, so only normalized terms ever enter the index. At query time the identical analyzer runs again — otherwise the query котята never matches the indexed lemma котёнок, and you have quietly rebuilt the miss.

In Elasticsearch or OpenSearch this is one declaration: the built-in russian analyzer (a Snowball stemmer) or a hunspell/lemma plugin, attached once to the field so it governs both indexing and search. It needs no GPU, it is deterministic, and it carries the toy's ranking inversion straight into production.

That is the shape of the whole cure for lexical search: rule- or dictionary-based normalization, cheap and symmetric, applied with one code path to both arms of the system.

The negation trap

I have just handed you the analyzer chain without a warning label, and that is exactly how it ships in production. In Elasticsearch the russian analyzer is standard → lowercase → russian_stop → russian_keywords → russian_stemmer: the stop filter runs before the stemmer. Watch what that costs. On a two-query toy, карта не работает and карта работает both come out as the token list [карта, работает], because не sits in the standard Russian stop list. Two queries with opposite meanings become byte-identical before they ever reach the index.

The second consequence is quieter and stranger. Lucene's russian_stop.txt holds 159 entries and not one form with ё: все is in the list, всё is not; еще is there, ещё is not; его is there, её is not. So всё survives stop-word removal while все does not — one lexeme, two behaviours in the index, decided by an optional pair of dots over a letter. The stemmer would have folded ё→е and made the two agree; it just runs too late to help.

The cures are all one-liners, and you pick by what your users type. Put a char_filter that folds ё→е before the stop filter, so the list sees one spelling. Subtract не / ни / без / нет from the list, and pay for it in index size. Index negation bigrams (не_работает) with a shingle filter, so the pair survives as its own term. Or hand negation to the dense arm, which reads it as meaning rather than as a token to be dropped.

And now the honest part, because it changes how you argue for this fix. Savoy measured stop-word removal on Russian and found it moves MAP by roughly 2 % — statistically it is nearly nothing. The trap is not in average recall; it is in a class of queries. On everything without negation you lose two percent, and on everything with negation you return the exact opposite of what was asked, at full confidence, with no error in any log. That is the shape of nearly every real search bug worth catching.

When not to normalize

Normalization is not free, and the bill comes due in precision. Sometimes the form is the meaning: an exact quotation, a legal term, an article number, a part code, a personal name, and the minimal pair небо / нёбо, which a stemmer folds into one stem and quietly loses. The cure is not to abstain. It is a second field — analyzed for recall, keyword and untouched for precision — with the query routed to whichever one the intent demands. Dense retrieval routes around morphology differently, as the next act shows, but it does not replace an exact-match field either.

And watch which claim you are defending, because the two are not equally supported. That you must normalize is settled: on the CLEF collections a light stemmer very nearly doubles mean average precision over raw forms. Which normalizer wins is not settled at all — in the same measurements a light stemmer, Snowball and plain character 4-grams land within a hair of each other, and 4-grams know no Russian whatsoever. The measurement that would settle this outright does not exist in the literature — I return to it at the end of the chapter.

So a lemma looking right to a human is not evidence. The lemmatizer costs more, holds a dictionary in memory, stumbles on names, neologisms and typos where a rule simply chops a tail, and resolves no homonymy it was never shown a sentence for. Normalizing is the decision you make; which normalizer is a measurement you owe your own corpus.

Dense retrieval speaks Russian — if it saw Russian

A bi-encoder (from L10) scores by vector similarity, not string overlap, so it sidesteps the surface-form miss entirely — inflected forms of one word land close in the space on their own. Two mechanisms combine: sub-word tokenization means котёнок and котята share a stem piece (so the very fragmentation that was a tax in Act 1 becomes a soft match here), and contrastive training places different forms of one meaning near each other.

The catch is a prerequisite: the encoder must have seen Russian in training. The English default from L10 has an English-centric vocabulary and was tuned on English pairs, so its Russian is weak and zero-shot transfer sags. You need either a multilingual encoder — multilingual-E5, BGE-M3, LaBSE — or a ru-specific one.

So dense retrieval usually needs no explicit lemmatizer: the morphology dissolves into the vector. But that only holds if both the query and the corpus live in one Russian-aware space — the same symmetry lesson, one layer up.

The string intersection is empty; the sub-word one is not

Act one closed with a promise, and here is where it is paid. Write the two matching rules side by side on the same pair. BM25 sums over shared strings, so for our query and our gold document \(\bigl|q \cap d\bigr| = 0\) and the sum has no terms in it. The encoder does not read strings; it reads the piece sequence the tokenizer produced, and there \(\bigl|\mathrm{sub}(q) \cap \mathrm{sub}(d)\bigr| > 0\) — both forms begin with the same stem piece. The two statements are true of the same pair at the same time.

That is the hinge of the whole lecture, and it has a sign flip inside it. The finer the tokenizer breaks a word, the higher the bill in act one — and the likelier a shared piece in act three. One property, two signs. So dense retrieval needs no lemmatizer stops being a slogan: the segmentation is doing, statistically and for free, the folding a stemmer does by rule. Tokenosaurus's mouthful of crumbs was a soft match waiting to happen.

Keep the honest boundary, though, because a shared piece is necessary, not sufficient. Overlapping pieces only help if training taught the encoder to build a stable meaning on them — which is the prerequisite of the previous beat wearing a formal coat. And two unrelated words can share a piece just as easily, so the mechanism buys a soft opportunity to match, never a guarantee. That is why the exact-term field survives this act too: the lexical arm promises the string, and only the string.

A vocabulary is a ceiling, not just a price list

Act one sold you the vocabulary as an account: so many slots, so many tokens per word, so much money. Now read the same object as an alphabet. A vocabulary entry is a unit the model may attach a meaning to. If a language was granted almost no entries, its words arrive as bare characters — and a bare character is a unit whose meaning is entirely borrowed from its neighbours.

Follow the mechanism through. A useful sub-word is one that recurs: the same piece shows up across thousands of different sentences, so gradient after gradient can settle a stable direction onto it. Give a word no merges of its own and where it breaks starts depending on what surrounds it — the same stem arrives as one shape here and another shape there, and no single unit accumulates enough evidence. Attention then has to rebuild the word's meaning from crumbs, from scratch, on every occurrence. The tax and the quality gap are not two findings; they are one fact read from two ends.

And that hands you a diagnostic you can run before downloading a single checkpoint: open the vocabulary and count the entries that hold two letters of your script in a row. If essentially none do, the model has no units for your language, only its letters — and no amount of fine-tuning on top will conjure an alphabet the tokenizer refuses to spell. That, not the model card, is what "the encoder must have seen Russian" means when you make it mechanical.

Why training folds the forms — and when it does not

The dense arm was sold on two mechanisms and only one of them has been proved. The sub-word one is settled: two forms share a piece, so their inputs overlap where their strings did not. But a shared piece is an opportunity, not a result — the model still has to decide that two sequences with a piece in common mean the same thing. The second mechanism is what makes that decision, and it is worth opening, because opening it also shows where it is absent.

Contrastive training pulls a query and its relevant passage together and pushes everything else away. Now look at what a positive pair is made of: a question somebody typed and a passage somebody else wrote, about the same thing, in a language where each sentence put the shared word into whatever case it needed. The inflectional variation sits inside the positive pair. So the objective is not merely tolerant of morphology — it is paid to ignore it, on pair after pair, because ignoring it is the only way the loss goes down. That is the exact sense in which the encoder learns the lemmatizer you did not write.

And the same mechanism tells you when it is missing. If the pairs were produced by machine translation, both sides were inflected by one system, consistently, from one English source; the variation a human corpus supplies for free is simply not there, so the objective never has to pay to ignore it. That is the translationese warning again, now with a mechanism under it instead of a caution. So read a model's training mix and ask it one question: was there anything in here that forced this encoder to treat котёнок and котята as the same word? If the answer is no, the shared sub-word is a hope, not a fold.

The dense arm does not promise you negation

One of the cures offered for the negation trap was to hand negation to the dense arm, on the grounds that it reads meaning instead of dropping tokens. It is the most attractive of them and the least guaranteed, so let me argue against my own suggestion. Take the pair the trap was built from — карта не работает and карта работает. They share every content word; the whole difference is one short function word. Whatever the encoder does, it does to a sequence in which the negation is a tiny minority of the pieces.

Then think about what pooling does to a minority. A passage vector is built by collapsing the token axis into a single point, so a signal carried by one piece among many is averaged against everything else in the passage before it ever reaches the comparison. And the training objective rarely pushes back: contrastive pairs are queries and the passages that answer them, and a corpus of such pairs holds very few minimal pairs that differ by a negation alone and are labelled as opposites. Nothing in the recipe forces the model to spend capacity on the one word that reverses the sentence.

So keep the four cures in an honest order. The reliable ones are mechanical: take не, ни, без and нет out of the stop list so the word survives into the index at all, and index the negation bigram so that не_работает becomes a term in its own right. The dense arm is a useful additional voice, not the place to put the requirement. And if your product genuinely turns on negation, this stops being a retrieval question and becomes an evaluation question: you need a query set built out of minimal pairs, because a class of query that never appears in your measurements cannot fail visibly.

Multilingual or ru-specific?

The multilingual route is the safe default. multilingual-E5 carries the same query:/passage: prefixes as English E5 across roughly a hundred languages. BGE-M3 is the flagship: 100+ languages, up to 8192 tokens, and — usefully for Russian — three outputs from one forward pass, a dense vector (L10), a learned-sparse one (SPLADE-style, L12) and a multi-vector one (ColBERT-style, L12), so it hands you the L12 hybrid inside a single model. LaBSE covers 109 languages but is a bitext specialist, better at translation retrieval than ad-hoc search.

The ru-specific route — ruBERT/ruRoBERTa as bases, ru-en-RoSBERTa as a retrieval encoder — earns its place on a narrow Russian domain (legal, medical, officialese) where a multilingual model spreads its capacity thin across a hundred languages. Otherwise a strong multilingual encoder is frequently just as good. One warning belongs here, because this course used to give the opposite advice: the older sentence-similarity encoders trained on Russian, sbert_large_nlu_ru among them, are not retrievers. They were trained to tell whether two sentences mean the same thing, never to match a query against a passage, and on a retrieval benchmark they collapse. A Russian name on the model card is not a retrieval result.

The decision rule is the L10 choosing-an-embedder slide in a new coat: shortlist by language and domain, then evaluate the finalists on your own Russian data rather than a leaderboard average.

The languages that usually get nothing

Look at the language counts in the table again: 100, 100+, 109. Those numbers have barely moved in years, and not by accident. Data for a rare language is scarce, and every slot in the training mix is paid for by some other language's slot. The arithmetic has always pushed toward the same hundred.

mmBERT (Marone et al., 2025) breaks the pattern with a scheduling trick rather than a data miracle. It trains on 3T tokens across more than 1800 languages — but the 1700 low-resource ones enter only during the decay phase, at the very end, when the learning rate has already fallen. The early phases, with big corpora and big steps, build the general structure of language; the late phase no longer re-teaches the model, it seats new languages into a space that already exists.

That is why a small corpus suffices there and would not suffice earlier: late and gentle, it is enough to place a language and not enough to damage the others. The reported result is that classification quality on those languages approaches what much larger general models deliver — from data that, spent early, would have been drowned out.

For this course the point is personal rather than abstract. The book you are reading exists in three languages, and one of them — Tatar — is exactly the kind of language that the hundred-language models leave out. A method that lifts such languages from a short late phase is the difference between «no model covers us» and «a model covers us, now go and verify it on our own benchmark» — which is precisely what the next beats do with ruMTEB. Coverage is not quality; 1800 languages is a claim about the training mix, not a promise about yours.

A reranker for Russian

L10 left you a cascade with a top floor: a cross-encoder that re-scores the shortlist the retriever hands up. For Russian that floor is short of options and shorter of evidence. The multilingual candidates are bge-reranker-v2-m3, jina-reranker-v2 — read that licence before production, it forbids commercial use — and Qwen3-Reranker. The only Russian-only cross-encoder, DiTy/cross-encoder-russian-msmarco, was trained on a machine-translated corpus and ships with no published evaluation at all.

The multilingual ones publish no Russian number either. The single citable Russian result is RusBEIR's, and it argues loudly for the cascade: reranking lifts both ladders, the lexical one and BGE-M3; on the extended set the authors report an average gain of about nine nDCG@10 points. Notice the footnote inside that sentence, because it is the kind that gets dropped when the number is retold — the two ladders and the average were measured on different collections of the same benchmark, so you may not subtract them against each other.

Hence the rule, and it is an uncomfortable one: today a Russian reranker is picked on the prose of a model card. That makes an evaluation on your own Russian queries mandatory rather than advisable, and it makes the licence a design constraint rather than a legal footnote. Rerank depth and cost per pair behave exactly as in L10 — the cross-encoder pays, not the index.

The “ru” in a model name is an intention

Here is the belief that survives every embedder discussion in a Russian-speaking team: a model trained for Russian does not need comparing with a multilingual one — on a Russian corpus it wins by definition. It sounds like tautology, which is what makes it dangerous. It is not a tautology; it is a hypothesis, and the leaderboard tests it.

The board says otherwise, in both directions. On the Russian suite's retrieval column a general multilingual retriever sits above two encoders named for the language — so the prefix in a model name states what the authors aimed at, not what they reached. And take the honest converse too, because it is the half people drop: other Russian models sit above that same multilingual one on the same column. Neither branch wins by construction. What decides is the training objective — retrieval pairs versus sentence similarity — and the training data, not the language written on the card.

So the rule is the L10 one, unchanged and now with teeth: shortlist from both branches and evaluate on your own Russian queries. The tendencies worth carrying as priors, not verdicts — the ru-specific model tends to win on a narrow domain with its own lexicon, where a multilingual model has spread its capacity across a hundred languages; the multilingual one tends to win on a mixed corpus with Latin script living inside Russian sentences, which is most of the real web.

A hundred all the way, seventeen hundred at the end

Turn the headline number into arithmetic and it stops being marketing. Of mmBERT's 1800+ languages, 1700 enter only during the decay phase. Subtract: 100 languages ride the whole training run. That is not a coincidence — it is precisely the coverage the previous generation offers, the 100 of multilingual-E5, the 100+ of BGE-M3, the 109 of LaBSE from the table two beats ago.

So the honest description of mmBERT is not «a model that knows 1800 languages instead of 100». It is «the same hundred, plus seventeen hundred seated at the end» — and the share matters: \(1700/1800 = 94.4\,\%\) of its coverage was bought in the phase where the learning rate had already collapsed.

Which is exactly why it works, and why it could not have worked earlier. Early, with big steps, a tiny corpus for a rare language would be drowned by the languages with terabytes — or, worse, would drag the shared space toward itself and damage them. Late, with a small step, the space already exists: the new language finds a seat in it, and the old ones do not move.

Carry the shape, not the number. When someone offers you a model «supporting 1800 languages», the question to ask is not how many but when — a language present from the start and a language added in the final phase are two different promises, and only the benchmark on your own data tells you which one you got.

The one asymmetry you have to keep

This chapter has one catechism — the same transformation on both sides — and by now it is loud enough to be applied where it does damage. So here is the exception, said deliberately. The stage that retrieves and the stages that read must not be handed the same text. What the index stores is a folded alphabet: lemmas or stems, ё collapsed, case gone, stop words dropped. What a cross-encoder and a generator need is the sentence a human actually wrote.

The reason is the one-way map. Normalization is many-to-one and therefore has no inverse, so the terms in a postings list are a lossy record of the document. A reranker fed those terms is being asked to judge relevance from котят игра — to rebuild the language it was trained on out of a shape no writer ever produced — and it will do that badly. The same holds for the snippet a reader sees and the passage you paste into a prompt. The stored source is the single truth for everything downstream of the ranking; the analyzer's output is a search key and nothing else.

So state the rule together with its scope, because the sloppy version costs recall in one place and precision in another. Symmetry is a requirement between the two sides of one comparison, not between the stages of a cascade. Query and index must be folded the same way — that is one comparison. Query and passage handed to a cross-encoder must be raw the same way — that is a different comparison, with its own symmetry. Nothing ever breaks because a transformation was applied. Things break because it was applied to one half of a pair.

Benchmarks: native vs translationese

Three benchmarks measure Russian retrieval, with the metrics you already know from L5/L10 — nDCG@10, MRR@10, recall@k. MIRACL is the gold standard: open retrieval over 18 languages including Russian, human-annotated on native in-language corpora. mMARCO is MS MARCO machine-translated into 13 languages — convenient and large, but it carries translationese. ruMTEB is the Russian branch of MTEB, 23 tasks spanning retrieval, STS and classification.

Translationese is the quiet trap. Machine-translated Russian syntactically echoes its English source — word order, phrasing, lexical choices a native speaker would not use — and a model with strong English priors can exploit that calque. So a high mMARCO score can reflect the model's grip on translation, not your users' language, and it will not transfer.

It is the L5 Goodhart lesson in a new coat: a high score on the wrong distribution is not what you meant to measure. Select on MIRACL and ruMTEB; use mMARCO for scale and training while discounting the bias.

The length regime

There is one more number you need before you choose an arm, and it is not about Russian at all — it is about how long your documents are. RusBEIR took the same content and re-cut it into windows of different sizes, then ran the same systems over each cut. On single sentences (17.6 words on average) the dense arm walks away with it: BM25 scores 13.58 nDCG@10, multilingual-E5-large 16.70, BGE-M3 17.30, FRIDA 20.79. Seven points is not a nuance, it is a verdict.

Now widen the window. At six sentences (105.8 words) BM25 is at 31.54 and has already caught the multilingual encoders. On full articles (2059 words) the ranking has flipped completely: BM25 74.95 against multilingual-E5-large's 60.62 — fourteen points, the other way. Same corpus, same models, same metric; only the length of the unit changed.

So dense always wins is not a law, it is a statement about a chunk size somebody forgot to mention. Which arm wins is a function of how long your documents are, and that makes chunking (L15) and the retrieval unit you design in L18 into a retrieval-model decision, not a formatting one. It also explains, better than any argument about semantics, why the answer in production is a hybrid rather than a choice: you do not get to promise that every document in your corpus lives in the same length regime.

And here is the third bill of this chapter, finally itemized on Russian data. On MIRACL's Russian split BM25 gets 0.334 nDCG@10 and the dense retriever mDPR gets 0.407 — but their fusion gets 0.532, with Recall@100 going 0.661 → 0.797 → 0.874. That is the whole hybrid argument in one row. One honest footnote: this is score fusion, not RRF. RRF is what you will actually deploy after L12, and no published RRF number for Russian exists to quote you.

The prefix is not cosmetic

Act 2 said symmetry means one lemmatizer in both arms. Nothing is lemmatized in the dense arm, so it is easy to read that lesson as finished here. It is not — it only changes shape. The general form is the one this chapter keeps circling: the same transformation on the query and on the corpus, whatever that transformation happens to be.

The E5 family makes it concrete. The query: and passage: prefixes are part of the input the model was trained on, not decoration. Encode the corpus as passages, hand the encoder a bare query, and you have fed it a distribution it never saw. The loss is silent: no exception, no log line, nothing red on any dashboard — just a quieter ranking. The same class of bug covers a corpus encoded with one model version and a query with another, or vectors normalized on one side and not the other.

So ask what would have caught it, because that is the part worth carrying away. No unit test sees a prefix: both sides return vectors of the right shape, and the cosine comes out a perfectly plausible number. Only an end-to-end retrieval metric on held-out Russian queries catches it — the same answer this chapter gives to every question about which model to trust.

Hard negatives in Russian

If you ever fine-tune a retriever on Russian, Act 2 stops being a lexical-search topic and becomes your training data. Hard negatives (L11) are usually mined with BM25: take the top of the ranking for a query, drop the gold document, call the rest negatives. Which means the lexical arm silently authors the training set the dense arm learns from — and it authors it in whatever state you left it in.

In Russian that breaks twice. Un-normalized, the postings list for an inflected query form is empty — the very result this chapter opened with — so BM25 returns noise, the hard negatives turn out trivially easy, and the contrastive objective learns only to tell the gold document from an arbitrary one, which is nothing. Normalized, the conflations of Act 2 turn into false negatives: a stemmer folds стал, сталь and стали onto one stem, a context-blind lemmatizer reads стали as стать and loses the metal, and a genuinely relevant document lands among the negatives of its own query. Gradient descent then spends your GPU hours pushing the right answer away.

So the order of work is fixed, and it is not the order most teams follow. Fix the lexical arm first. Mine with it second. Fine-tune third. A morphology bug in the index is an annoyance you patch on a Friday; the same bug in your negatives is baked into the weights and comes back as a mystery in the evaluation.

A leaderboard row is a number plus three qualifiers

You are about to pick an encoder off a board, so learn to read one row properly. A benchmark score is never just a number: it is a number plus which task, plus which split, plus which version of the suite. Drop any of the three and you are quoting a different quantity than you think.

Start with the task, because a Russian suite is mostly not retrieval. Most of its tasks are classification, clustering, similarity and reranking, and the headline average is therefore dominated by columns you did not come for — the L5 lesson verbatim, and it will happily recommend a model that cannot retrieve. Then look inside the retrieval group, because the tasks there are not equally informative. One of them asks you to find an article given its own headline: one relevant document per query, an easy proxy, and models bunch up near the ceiling and stop separating at all. The column that separates candidates is the column worth reading.

Then the version, which is the qualifier people forget precisely because it is boring. When a suite replaces its retrieval tasks with harder-negative editions it issues a new version and marks the old one superseded — and numbers across the two are not comparable at all. A table from an old blog post and today's board are two different measurements wearing one name. So: name the task, name the split, name the version — and then go and measure on your own corpus anyway, because none of those three qualifiers is your traffic.

A thousand small cuts

Even with the right model and normalization, recall leaks through Russian orthographic edge cases — each trivial alone, together a real hole, each healed by one line of preprocessing. The ё/е collapse: ёлка is routinely written елка, one word split into two strings; normalize ё→е symmetrically. Case: lowercase both arms so москва matches Москва, but keep a case-preserving field where an acronym like МИР must not merge with мир.

Transliteration and code-switching: users type Moskva for Москва and drop English brand and tech terms into Russian sentences; expand queries with transliteration variants or lean on a multilingual embedder whose space already spans both scripts. And the uniquely Russian keyboard-layout typo: type привет on the English layout and out comes ghbdtn — same keys, Latin letters, meaningless to the index — fixed by a deterministic ЙЦУКЕН↔QWERTY key map plus a did you mean.

The unifying principle is the whole chapter's: fold the query and the corpus to one form, with one code path, in both arms. The small things are cheap alone and costly in aggregate.

A real user query. They forgot to switch the layout, so the fingers typed Russian and the machine received Latin. Not a single document matches — and the search log will record this as «no results», not as «a typo».

Switch the layout map on: key by key, the Latin string turns back into Russian. But look at the key marked in red — ё does not sit among the letters, it sits on the backtick, and the map preserves it when that key was pressed. This query uses е instead, a separate spelling choice.

The repaired query meets the corpus — and still finds nothing. The document is spelled with ё, the query with е. To a human they are the same word; to the index they are two different terms, different bytes, different postings lists.

Fold ё to е in BOTH arms — in the analyser and in the query pipeline — and the document is found. Recall starts climbing. Now press the toggle and fold it on the index only: recall drops straight back to zero. A one-armed normalisation is worse than none.

A different cut, same wound. This query looks exactly like the real word — but several of its letters are Latin twins of Cyrillic ones. Same pixels, different code points, and a score of exactly zero. This is how spam gets past filters and how deduplication breaks.

Confusables normalisation (Unicode UTS #39) maps the Latin twins back onto Cyrillic and the word is found. Read the ladder: each repair is worth a measured amount, and every one of them obeys the same rule — normalise in BOTH arms, or you make things worse than doing nothing.

Homoglyphs: Cyrillic under Latin

Here is a cut that survives every normalizer in this chapter, because nothing about it looks wrong. A handful of lowercase Cyrillic letters — а е о р с у х — are visually identical to their Latin twins, and in upper case the set is wider: А В Е К М Н О Р С Т У Х. Note the asymmetry, because it decides where you look first: В and Н have Latin doubles while lowercase в and н do not, so capitals are the dangerous ones. Whole Russian words can be spelled end to end in Latin homoglyphs — оса, роса, хор, сухо — and a сосна with four of its letters swapped for Latin twins — c, o, c and a, leaving only н Cyrillic — scores zero against the real one, with nothing visibly different on the screen.

The damage compounds across the whole pipeline at once, which is what earns this its own beat. Exact match is dodged, so the term vanishes from the postings list. The tokenizer's careful merges collapse, and a word that cost one token starts costing several. And the embedding lands somewhere it has no business being, because the sub-words it was built from are not the sub-words of the word you meant. One substitution, three mechanisms broken.

This is not a thought experiment. A domain registered in 2017 rendered on screen exactly as apple.com and slipped past mixed-script defences precisely because it was single-script — every letter Cyrillic. The cure is a confusables mapping in the preprocessing chain, applied in both arms, next to its neighbouring chore: normalize every incoming string to NFC, because both ё and й decompose, and macOS hands you the decomposed form straight off the file system.

Transliteration, and the order of the fixes

щука has more than one Latin spelling, and the standards disagree with each other long before the users get a chance to. ISO 9 renders it with a diacritic, ŝuka; GOST system B gives shhuka; BGN and the Russian passport rules give shchuka. The widely copied claim that GOST gives shch is simply wrong. And system B has a sting nobody expects: х becomes x, so хорошо transliterates to xorosho, which no naive matching rule will ever recover.

Users obey none of the three, and they code-switch inside the sentence besides — скачал update для iPhone. So the practical answer is not one map but a variant dictionary for the entities you actually care about, plus a dense arm whose space already spans both scripts. And the layout typo is only one class of error: dropped letters, neighbouring keys and phonetic spellings all pass straight through the ЙЦУКЕН↔QWERTY map and need a real corrector behind it.

One rule outranks all of the others: compute Damerau—Levenshtein distance over lemmas, not surface forms. Otherwise котятам and котята sit one edit apart, and the corrector helpfully fixes the case ending — which is to say it silently replaces the reader's query with a different one. And the fixes have an order, because each narrows the alphabet the next one works in: layout, then case and NFC, then ё→е, then confusables, then typos, then normalization — and the same chain runs again in the index arm.

A one-sided fix is worse than no fix

I have been repeating the same transformation on both arms like a catechism. Let me now put a ladder under it, because the interesting rung is not the one you expect. Take a small corpus where half the documents are written with ё and half without — the real web, in other words — and a user who types the query without it. Raw index, raw query: you retrieve whatever happened to be spelled the user's way, which is some of it. Now fold ё→е in the index only, and recall goes to nothing at all.

Sit with that rung, because it is the whole hour's lesson finally holding a knife. Asymmetry is not half a cure; it is an anti-cure — measurably below doing nothing. The reason is plain once you say it: the accidental matches were the only thing holding recall up, and normalizing one arm destroys exactly them. Every ё-form in the corpus has become an е-form while the query still carries its ё, so the strings that used to coincide now cannot. Fold both arms and recall returns; add the stemmer on top of both and it climbs again, because the fixes compose.

Then the trap that outlives the toy: we normalize is a claim about a field, not about a system. The Snowball stemmer folds ё in its own prelude, so a stemmed text field is cured for free — while the keyword field for entities, the phrase field, the autocomplete index and the facet values never pass through that stemmer and stay untouched. Each of those is a separate arm, and a fix that is true of one of them is the one-sided fix again, wearing a different mask. Audit per field, not per pipeline.

One word, two byte strings

Two Unicode facts about Cyrillic, and they pull in opposite directions. The good one first, because it lets you stop worrying about something: Cyrillic case mapping is one-to-one and locale-independent. There is no Cyrillic analogue of the Turkish dotless i, no letter whose uppercase depends on which country the server thinks it is in. Lowercase both arms and be done — the only real decision left is the one you already met, keeping a case-preserving field so an acronym does not merge with the common noun that spells the same.

Now the bad one. The letters ё and й are each a single code point that also decomposes into a base letter plus a combining mark, and neither composition is excluded, so both spellings are legal Unicode for the same word. Two strings identical on screen therefore have different lengths and never compare equal, and this is not exotic: macOS hands you the decomposed form straight out of the file system, so a corpus ingested from one laptop can disagree with a query typed on another over a word that looks the same in both.

Which fixes the order of your preprocessing, and the order is the whole point. Normalize to NFC on input, in both arms, before anything else touches the string — before lowercasing, before the ё→е mapping, before tokenization. Do it later and the ё→е filter silently misses the decomposed spelling, because what it is looking at is no longer one character; you will have shipped a normalizer that fixes precisely the strings it happened to be tested on, and reports success on all of them.

The third arm is yesterday’s index

A previous beat told you to audit per field rather than per pipeline. Take the same idea one dimension further, into time. An index does not store text; it stores the terms the analyzer produced at the moment each document was written into it. The query, by contrast, is analyzed now. So the two sides of your comparison are not query against index — they are today's analyzer against every version of the analyzer that has ever run over this corpus.

Which turns an innocuous edit into a migration. Add a filter folding ё→е, subtract не from the stop list, swap a stemmer for a lemmatizer: each of those changes what the query folds to immediately, and changes what the corpus folded to only for the documents indexed after the change. The older documents keep their old terms and quietly stop matching. It is the one-sided fix again — except that nothing in the configuration looks one-sided, because the file plainly says the analyzer is attached to the field for both indexing and search. The asymmetry is between the code and the data the code has already written.

The discipline is unglamorous and has to be a habit rather than a decision. An analyzer change is a reindex, in the same deployment, or the field is split so both spellings stay searchable until the rebuild finishes. And it buys you a test most search systems lack: take a document already sitting in the index, run today's analyzer over its stored source, and check that the terms you get are the terms the index holds. That is a cheap detector for the very class of failure this chapter is about — no error, no log line — arriving this time through the calendar rather than through the language.

Not every cure belongs in both arms

This act has hammered «both arms» hard enough that the reflex is now to drop every cure into the analyzer chain and call it done. Look at the roundup once more, at the column that says where to fix it, and the cures split cleanly in two. The ё→е fold, lowercasing, NFC and the confusables map go into both arms, because a document can carry them exactly as readily as a query. Layout restoration and transliteration expansion go into the query preprocessor only, and nowhere else.

What divides them is the question of who produced the string. A layout slip is the trace of a human typing into a box with the wrong keyboard mode: your corpus was not typed by your readers. Run the ЙЦУКЕН↔QWERTY map over your documents and you will fix nothing, because there was nothing to fix — instead you will mangle every legitimately Latin token in them: brand names, code identifiers, English quotations, the very language switching this act warned you about. Transliteration expansion breaks the same way from the other side: widening a query into variants is cheap and reversible, while rewriting documents into variants multiplies the index and destroys the very exactness a keyword field is kept for.

So state the rule in its sharper form, and notice that it is the same rule rather than an exception to it. A cure that removes a difference both sides can have lives in both arms. A cure that undoes damage only the query side can suffer lives before the query and nowhere else — and that is not asymmetry, it is symmetry stated precisely: you restore the query to the form the corpus is already in, and from there both sides walk one and the same chain. It pins the order down too: the restoration has to happen before anything else touches the string. Test every new cure with a single question: could a document have acquired this defect? Yes — both arms. No — the query preprocessor, first in the queue.

When to reach for what

Tie it together. For keyword search on exact terms, use BM25 with lemmatization — cheap, transparent, and it fixes the forms. For semantics, synonyms and paraphrase, use a multilingual dense encoder — meaning past morphology, no lemmatizer. For a narrow Russian domain, try a ru-specific model or fine-tune. For cross-lingual ru↔en, use a multilingual vector or LaBSE, one shared space for both languages.

And for production search, the best Russian baseline is neither alone but the hybrid: lemmatized BM25 fused with a multilingual dense retriever by RRF (from L12). The lexical arm keeps the exact terms a dense vector blurs — names, product codes, legal citations — while the dense arm carries meaning past the morphology. Two failure modes, two arms, one fused ranking.

None of this is a new architecture. It is the familiar pipeline — tokenizer, BM25, bi-encoder, hybrid — honestly tuned for a language that carries its grammar inside its words.

Fuse ranks, not scores

The word hybrid has been standing in for two different recipes, and for Russian the difference is not a matter of taste. Score fusion takes a weighted sum of the two arms after rescaling them into a common range, \(s_{\oplus}(d)=\alpha\,\hat s_{\mathrm{BM25}}(d)+(1-\alpha)\,\hat s_{\mathrm{dense}}(d)\) — so the rescaling is redone on every query, and the weight \(\alpha\) has to be fitted on labelled data. Rank fusion — the RRF you already met — reads nothing but positions, \(\mathrm{RRF}(d)=\sum_{a}1/(k+\mathrm{rank}_a(d))\): one smoothing constant and not another knob in sight.

Now recall what this chapter does to the lexical arm for a living. Lemmatization folds surface forms into one term, so document frequencies move, so idf moves, so the whole magnitude of a BM25 score moves — while the dense arm's cosine is left exactly as it was. A weight fitted before that swap is quietly wrong after it: nothing crashes, the ranking simply gets worse in a way no exception will ever tell you about. And you will change the normalizer again — a stop-list fix, an ё→е filter, a different stemmer are all edits of the same class.

Ranks do not care. A monotone rescoring of either arm leaves every position where it was, so rank fusion survives the analyzer edit that expires a tuned weight. Start there: it is the fusion you can defend before you own a single Russian evaluation set. Reach for score fusion only when you have your own Russian labels to fit \(\alpha\) on — and then treat that weight as part of the normalization stack, re-fitted whenever the stack changes, not as a constant you set once.

Ten things to check before you ship

So let me say it the way I would say it standing next to you on launch day. Before anything else, measure the fertility of your corpus with your tokenizer — the arc from ×5.74 to ×0.67 says the number is a property of a choice you are making, not of the Russian language, and you can only price a context window once you know it. Then check that the normalization runs in both arms and is literally the same code; that не, ни, без and нет have been subtracted from the stop list; and that a char_filter folding ё→е sits before that stop filter rather than after it, where the stemmer would find it too late.

Then the input side, where the damage is silent. Normalize every incoming string to NFC, because ё and й both decompose and the same word arrives from macOS as a different byte sequence. Keep one keyword field, untouched by any analyzer, for the things that must match exactly — article numbers, product codes, names — because the stemmer that saves your recall is the same stemmer that will merge МИР with мир. And restore the keyboard layout in the query preprocessor, before anything else touches the string, so ghbdtn becomes привет instead of an empty result page.

Then the dense arm, where the wrong number gets read off the wrong column. Choose your embedder by the Retrieval score on ruMTEB and not by the average over its 23 tasks — nine of those are classification, and the average will happily recommend a model that cannot retrieve. Add the query: and passage: prefixes wherever the model was trained with them, because a missing prefix is a silent, ordinary, expensive accuracy loss. And then evaluate on your own Russian data, because every published number you have just read was measured on somebody else's corpus.

Nine of those ten are just chores. The tenth is the rule that holds the other nine together, and it is the sentence this whole chapter has been repeating: the same transformation on both sides. For the lexical arm that transformation is the analyzer — one declaration, attached to the field, governing indexing and search alike. For the dense arm it is the same encoder with the same prefixes on the corpus and on the query. Every failure in this chapter, from the fragmented token to the vanished negation to the query encoded by a different model, is one asymmetry that somebody introduced and nobody measured.

Russian was the example, not the subject

Before the door closes, where the meter is actually running. Not byte-level architectures — those are old enough now to be history rather than frontier. What moves is the vocabulary budget sized per language: not a bigger vocabulary but how many of its slots each language is granted, which makes the token tax a designer's decision rather than a property of Cyrillic. Then byte-level fallback inside ordinary production tokenizers, so a rare word shatters instead of falling out of the vocabulary altogether. And instruction-tuned multilingual embedders, where the Russian gap closes with data — instructions, pairs, fine-tuning — and not with architecture. Read the training mix, not the block diagram.

Then say the gap out loud, because that is where you take the baton from the literature. A controlled comparison of lemma against stem against raw forms for Russian BM25 does not exist: one line of work measures stemming against no normalization at all, another asserts the lemma's superiority without an ablation. Published knowledge ends there. Past that line the measurement is yours, and it has to be true of exactly one corpus: yours.

And Russian was the example, not the subject. Arabic attaches clitics and leaves the vowels unwritten, so its normalization unit is clitic stripping rather than the lemma. Turkish and Tatar string suffix onto suffix until a word is a sentence — both the paradigm and the token bill run larger than Russian's, with cleaner seams to cut along. Finnish is the same disease at a bigger dose. Chinese breaks an earlier step entirely: no spaces, so the pipeline starts with segmentation before normalization is even a question. The checklist does not change — measure fertility on your corpus, choose the normalization unit your language actually needs, apply it in both arms, evaluate on your own data. The diagnosis is per language; the move against it is the same everywhere.

In RAG the tax is paid twice

Before the door closes, look forward at the pipeline this chapter feeds. Chunking, context retrieval and everything built on them measure their budget in tokens — and act one already told you what a token buys in Russian. The same window covers less text, so either the chunks get shorter and each one holds less of the argument, or the context bill grows. There is no third option, and choosing not to decide means the first one happens to you silently.

Then the bill arrives a second time in the same request. You pay the tax once encoding the query, and again on every passage you paste into the prompt — retrieval is the step that multiplies the tokenized text, so a language that costs more per word costs more per retrieved answer, not per query. And grounding by quotation breaks on exactly the mechanism this chapter spent an act repairing: search for a quotation as a literal string against a lemmatized index and it will not come back. The field you rank on and the field you verify a citation against cannot be the same field.

One thing more to carry, because it turns a slogan into a design decision. Re-cut the same corpus into different lengths and the two arms swap places — that was the length regime, and it means dense always wins is a claim about your chunk size, not about your models. So chunking is a retrieval-model decision, made together with the arm weights and not before them. Carry that forward: the same pipeline is about to move beyond text, and every one of these bills goes with it.

The crumbs that fold the forms are the crumbs that blur the names

The chapter closes on a hybrid, and the reason given for keeping the lexical arm is that it holds the exact terms a dense vector blurs — names, article numbers, product codes. That is usually stated as a fact about two technologies. It is better than that: it is the same mechanism as the rescue, read with the sign flipped.

Follow it through. A form meets a form in the vector space because the tokenizer breaks both into pieces the model has attached a stable meaning to. Now hand that same tokenizer a name it has never seen — a surname, an article number, a part code, a rare brand written in Cyrillic. It has no merges for that string either, so the string shatters; but this time the pieces are not shared with anything you meant, and each of them carries a meaning learned somewhere else entirely. The vector that comes out is assembled from crumbs belonging to other words. Two unrelated codes that happen to share a fragment end up near each other, and the name you asked for sits no closer to itself than the arithmetic happens to place it.

One property, two signs — for the second time in this chapter. Fine segmentation is what lets котёнок and котята meet, and it is what stops a surname like Мещеряков from reliably retrieving itself. So the exact-match field is not a legacy component kept for the nervous; it is the other half of one design. One arm promises the string and nothing but the string, the other promises meaning and cannot promise the string, and you fuse them precisely because no single representation does both. Ask each query which promise it needs, and the arm follows.

Morphology strikes back — and loses to the same move

Morphology struck back, and it lost to the same move three times. The token tax, the surface-form miss and the model mismatch are three faces of one fact — Russian carries its grammar inside its words — and one principle answers all three: fold the query and the corpus to one form, in both arms. Whether the form is a token, a string, or a vector, symmetry is the whole game.

On our toy the payoff was one number: the gold answer's BM25 went from \(0.0\) to \(1.3885\), rank 2 to rank 1, the moment the forms matched. In production it is a multilingual encoder plus lemmatized BM25, fused, with the small orthographic cuts dressed — Tokenosaurus fed, the answer surfaced.

Not a new model. The familiar pipeline, honestly tuned for Russian — and the same discipline waiting for any language that does not happen to be English. L20 takes that pipeline further still — multi-hop, multimodal, and answerable for what it does — and the question it opens there starts right here: whose language does your search speak?
