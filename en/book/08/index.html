The scroll I forgot the start of

Let me tell you how I used to read. I would take a long scroll and run my eye across it, left to right, thread by thread — and by the time I reached the far edge, I had already forgotten how the first thread began. Everything I had seen was crushed into a single knot in my hand, one context vector \(c\), and I was meant to weave a whole answer from that one overloaded strand.

This is the old recurrent way. Each word waits for the one before it; the meaning of the entire sentence gets squeezed into one thread that must carry it all. On a short scroll it holds. On a long one it frays — the beginning fades, the far-off word I needed slips out of reach, and the knot cannot hold what I stuffed into it.

So I stopped reading like that. In the last chapter you sat at the Council and watched the crossing itself: queries, keys, values, one encoder block assembled in front of you. Here we build the whole loom around that block — stack it into a machine, and see the three ways one machine can be threaded: reading the finished cloth (BERT), weaving forward one thread at a time (GPT), turning one cloth into another (encoder-decoder) — all the way out to RLHF, where a master weaver retunes the pattern to the wearer's taste. No single overloaded strand, no forgotten opening.

How far a signal has to walk

Before we lay the words on the table, let me measure the old way properly, because «the beginning fades» is a complaint, not a mechanism. Put a number under it: for two threads at positions \(i\) and \(j\), count the operations a signal — and, on the way back, its gradient — must cross to get from one to the other. In a recurrent loom the answer is \(\operatorname{path}(i,j)=|i-j|\): the signal walks every intermediate state in turn, because there is no other road. In attention the answer is \(\operatorname{path}(i,j)=1\): any pair is wired straight through one weight matrix.

Now you can see why length hurts, and there is no mysticism in it. Every hop is another multiplication on the way back; string enough of them together and the gradient either fades to nothing or blows up, so the far-off dependency is exactly the one the loom never learns. And this is why gluing on a second pass does not rescue the old way: run the scroll right-to-left as well and each pass still walks its own \(|i-j|\) states — you have bought a second long road, not a short one. There is a second complaint beside it, about time rather than gradient: step \(t\) cannot begin until step \(t-1\) has finished, so a recurrent loom cannot be run wide, while attention scores every pair in a single sweep.

So keep the trade in one line, because the rest of the chapter pays for it. Attention collapses the worst path from \(n\) hops to one — and buys that with a table of every pair against every pair, which grows with the square of the cloth's length. The bill arrives in the act on efficiency, and it is why anyone still looks back at recurrence: on a truly long scroll, walking is cheap in memory and crossing is not. Ask yourself where that boundary runs for the cloth you actually weave.

Lay every word on the table — and let each ask the rest "how much do you matter to me?"

For years I wove the old way, left to right, one pass — every word I read got crammed into a single carrying-thread, and by the end of a long sentence that one thread was frayed to breaking, trying to remember the beginning while I worked the end. The idea that frees the loom is almost embarrassingly simple: stop passing the sentence along a single thread. Lay every word on the table at once.

Now let each word turn to all the others and ask one honest question — "how much do you matter to me?" The pronoun it turns to every noun behind it; the verb turns to its subject. Give every word direct access to every other — that, and nothing more mystical, is what I mean by attention. No word has to shout down a corridor of time steps to be heard.

And the new value of a word is not itself alone but a weighted blend of all the others — much of the ones it leaned toward, a whisper of the rest. The threads no longer pass a message hand to hand and lose it; they cross, each with each, and every crossing carries its own weight.

Which stops the loom lights — and the one it only looks like

Before the first formula, put this hour on the map. The spine has four stops — Get Data → Measure → Rank → Generate — and a lecture claims a stop by what it hands the Ship, never by the words that happen to appear on its slides. That distinction is about to do real work, because this chapter will say generate a great many times and still not stand on Generate.

Two stops are lit, and they divide the hour cleanly. Get Data owns everything that turns text into vectors: the pieces the tokenizer cuts, the position added to each one, the block stacked into depth until a word's vector finally knows the sentence around it. That is the Archives — the machine room that produces the thing an index will later hold. Measure owns the comparison running inside that machine: every word asking every other how much it matters is a similarity score with a softmax laid over it, and the loom is built out of nothing else. Measure takes a second gift at the far end of the chapter, where the reward model of RLHF is a ruler learned for a quality nobody could write a formula for.

Now the two dark stops, and the second one is the trap. Rank stays dark because nothing in this hour is ever put in order: no result list, no candidate pool, no cascade, no latency budget — L10 takes this very encoder and builds all of that. Generate stays dark although half the chapter emits words: the GPT line, teacher forcing, temperature, top-p, the whole tail on alignment. On this course Generate means the Oracle — a grounded answer standing on retrieved evidence — and this hour hands the Ship neither evidence nor grounding, only a machine able to produce a next token. That is precisely why the tail here is marked beyond the border of search. Carry the rule out with you: to place an unfamiliar topic on the spine, ask what it hands the Ship, not which verbs it uses.

Query, Key, Value — and why I divide by √dₖ

Here is how a thread learns who to listen to. From each word I spin three small vectors on the loom. A Query — what am I looking for? A Key — what do I offer to others? And a Value — what I actually pass along if you attend to me. Three learned projections, \(Q=XW_Q,\ K=XW_K,\ V=XW_V\), nothing more.

To score thread \(i\) against thread \(j\), I take the dot product of its query with the other's key: \(E_{ij}=Q_i\cdot K_j\). High when they align, low when they don't. A row of softmax turns these scores into weights that sum to one — a soft lookup, not a hard pick — and the output is the blended values, \(Y_i=\sum_j A_{ij}V_j\). The thread listens a little to everyone, mostly to whoever fits.

One catch. In \(d_k\) dimensions the dot-product's variance grows with the dimension — big scores push softmax into a spike, almost one-hot, and the gradient goes numb. So I divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) first. Same alignment, gentler numbers: unscaled, \(\mathrm{softmax}(0,0,6)\to(0.002,0.002,0.995)\); scaled by \(\sqrt{4}=2\), \(\mathrm{softmax}(0,0,3)\to(0.045,0.045,0.909)\). Still decisive, but the cloth stays soft enough to keep learning.

The catch in the crossing: scores swell with the width

Look closely at one crossing before we stack the loom any higher. The score between two threads is a dot product in \(d_k\) dimensions — a sum of \(d_k\) small products. And sums grow. If each coordinate is an independent strand with unit variance, the variances simply add: \(\mathrm{Var}(q\cdot k)=d_k\). Widen the loom and the typical score swells like \(\sqrt{d_k}\) — not because the threads agree more, but merely because there are more terms in the sum.

Big scores are poison for the softmax. Push one score far above the rest and the row saturates: nearly all the weight lands on a single crossing, the others get almost none — and, worse, almost no gradient. A saturated row is a loom wound too tight: one thread pulled taut to ringing, the rest hanging slack, unteachable. So before the softmax I loosen the tension by exactly the factor the width added: divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), and the scores keep unit variance at any width. Remember the heart of the catch: the divisor is no tuning whim — it is the width of the crossing itself, undoing the swelling it caused.

Self-Attention: Threads That Read Each Other

Here is the quiet trick that makes the loom clever. I take one piece of cloth — one sentence — and from that same cloth I spin three questions of every thread: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer?), and a value (what do I actually carry?). \(Q = XW_Q,\ K = XW_K,\ V = XW_V\), all three born from the same input \(X\). This is self-attention: the threads do not look outside for meaning, they look at each other.

So every word forms its own query and reaches across to the keys of all the other words in its own sentence — direct access to the whole cloth in a single pass, no thread forced to carry the message down a long relay. And here is why it matters: the word bank is coloured by its neighbours. Beside river it leans toward mud and current; beside money it leans toward vaults and interest. Same thread, different weave, because context bled into it.

The old Lexical Gremlin never learned this. He only checks whether two words match — bank equals bank, and he shrugs at the rest. He cannot see that one bank is a shore and the other a safe. Self-attention outgrows him precisely here: it does not compare letters, it lets each thread take on the hue of the company it keeps.

One crossing, worked by hand

Let me work one crossing in front of you, symbol by symbol, on the smallest cloth that shows everything: three threads, \(x_1, x_2, x_3\). From each I spin the three tags with the same shared combs: \(q_i = x_i W_Q\), \(k_i = x_i W_K\), \(v_i = x_i W_V\). Nothing about thread one is special — the same three matrices touch every thread.

Now stand on thread one and put its question to the whole row. Three scores: \(e_{11} = \frac{q_1\cdot k_1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\), \(e_{12} = \frac{q_1\cdot k_2}{\sqrt{d_k}}\), \(e_{13} = \frac{q_1\cdot k_3}{\sqrt{d_k}}\) — thread one against itself, its neighbour, and the far one. A softmax over the row turns them into weights \(a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{13}\): all positive, summing to one — a budget of attention, spent across three crossings.

The new thread is the blend: \(y_1 = a_{11}v_1 + a_{12}v_2 + a_{13}v_3\) — mostly the value of whoever's key answered the question best, a whisper of the rest. Do the same standing on threads two and three, and you have done by hand exactly what the matrix form \(\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k}\right)V\) does in one stroke: every row of it is one thread's question, one budget, one blend.

Several small looms, one set of threads

One loom, one weaver, one obsession — that is how I began. But watch a single head at the loom: it locks onto one kind of crossing. Maybe it follows grammar, tying each verb back to its subject. Ask that same head to also track who she refers to three threads back, and it fumbles — one pair of hands cannot chase two patterns at once.

So I do the honest thing: I set up several small looms side by side, and I hand each the very same threads. These are the heads. One head weaves syntax, another weaves coreference, another just binds neighbours close — each is free to hunt a different relation, in its own little subspace. I split \(d_{\text{model}}\) across \(h\) heads, so each works in \(d_k = d_{\text{model}}/h\) coordinates — and the total labour never grows, I merely divide it.

When every loom has done its narrow, beautiful work, I lay their strips of cloth edge to edge — that is the concat — and pass them through one last comb, \(W_O\), which blends the separate patterns into a single fabric. Without that final comb the heads would stay strangers; with it, syntax and meaning are woven into one cloth.

The loom machine, one unit

Attention alone is not the loom — it is one shuttle pass. The real machine, the Transformer block, has two sub-layers stacked in order: first multi-head self-attention, letting every thread cross every other; then a feed-forward network that reworks each thread on its own, widening it \( d \to 4d \) and pulling it back to \( d \). Two moves: cross the threads, then dress each one.

But between and after each sub-layer sit the two quiet parts that make the whole thing hold. Residual — add & keep — means I never throw the original thread away: I lay the woven detail beside the thread that came in, \( x + \text{sublayer}(x) \), so nothing the earlier layers earned is lost. LayerNorm is the tension-comb: after each addition it re-tightens every thread to the same even pull, so no one thread goes slack or snaps as the cloth grows.

And here is the beautiful part: the block gives back cloth of the exact same shape it took in, \( (T, d_{\text{model}}) \) to \( (T, d_{\text{model}}) \). So I do not need a hundred different machines. I build one good loom and run the cloth through it, again and again — stack the same block \( N \) times, and depth does the rest.

Why the machine stacks

I called the shape preservation beautiful. It is better than beautiful — it is forced, and the force is the residual. Writing a sub-layer with its residual and its comb gives \( x' = \mathrm{LayerNorm}\big(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x)\big) \), and that addition is only legal between two tables of the same shape. So attention and the feed-forward net do not merely happen to hand back an \( n \times d \) cloth; they are not permitted to hand back anything else. The block is a map \( \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} \), and that is a shape-preserving machine by construction.

Three things the rest of this chapter treats as given fall straight out. First, a block's output is a legal input to the next block, so depth needs no adapters — «twelve blocks» and «twenty-four blocks» are the same machine run to different depths, not two machines. Second, \(n\) appears in no weight matrix at all: the same weights accept a phrase or a page, and the only ceiling is the table of position tags the threads arrive with, which is the very next beat. Third, the block's price is set by \(d\) — by how wide the cloth is — and not by how many heads split it, because splitting \(d\) across heads and reassembling costs the same either way.

So the family album stops being a list of tricks. Sharing one block's weights across all layers, dropping half the layers, or bolting a task head on top are all legal moves on a machine whose input and output are the same object. And here is the boundary, because shape is not price: the table of pairs that attention builds grows as \(n^2\), so a longer cloth stacks just as cleanly and costs quadratically more. The shape holds all the way; the bill arrives in the act on efficiency.

The loom that forgot the order

Here is a secret the loom won't confess on its own: attention has no sense of order. Shuffle the threads on the beam and the pattern comes out shuffled the very same way — nothing complains. It reads a bag of threads, not a line of them. So "cat sat on mat" and "mat sat on cat" arrive as the exact same heap, and the loom weaves them identically. That is a disaster for meaning.

So before I feed the threads in, I clip a small tag onto each one — a positional encoding — added right onto its vector: \( \tilde{x}_t = x_t + PE_t \). Now thread number three knows it is third, and "first" no longer looks like "last."

Two ways to stamp the tag. The original Transformer used sinusoidal marks — fixed \(\sin/\cos\) at many frequencies, no parameters, and they keep counting past any length seen in training. BERT instead learns a table of position tags, positions \(0 \dots 511\) — more flexible, but it runs out at 512. Fixed and clever, or learned and capped: I choose by how long the cloth must grow.

Three silhouettes of one machine

Step back from the single block and squint: every transformer you will meet in this chapter is one of three silhouettes built from the same part. Stack the blocks with their full, two-sided crossing and you get an encoder-only machine — a reader; BERT lives here. Stack them with a triangular mask, each thread seeing only what came before it, and you get a decoder-only machine — a writer, weaving forward; the GPT line lives here. Put a reading stack beside a writing stack and let the writer's threads also reach across into the reader's finished cloth — cross-attention — and you have the encoder-decoder, the translator's silhouette: T5 and BART.

And where does such a machine keep its weight? Every block carries the same furniture: four projection combs for attention (\(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O\)) and the two wide boards of the FFN (\(W_1, W_2\)) — and the boards outweigh the combs roughly two to one, so the lion's share of a block's weight sits in the modest feed-forward layer, not in the celebrated attention. At the entrance stands the embedding table, one row per vocabulary entry — a hefty piece in its own right. In their parts the silhouettes barely differ; they differ in the mask and the plumbing. Only the encoder-decoder pays extra: its writing blocks carry a third attention — the cross-crossing into the other stack.

Counting what one block weighs

I said the boards outweigh the combs; now count them, because the ratio is not a rule of thumb but arithmetic you can do in your head. Attention carries four square combs — \(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O\) — each mapping the width onto the width, so each holds \(d^2\) numbers: \(4d^2\) for the whole crossing. The feed-forward net carries two boards: one widens a thread from \(d\) to \(4d\), the other brings it back — \(4d^2\) apiece, \(8d^2\) together. One block, \(12d^2\), and two thirds of that sits in the modest feed-forward layer nobody names the machine after.

Read what does not appear in the count, because those absences are what the chapter has been leaning on. The number of heads is absent: splitting \(d\) across \(h\) heads and sewing the strips back together is the work of those same four combs, so heads redistribute the labour and never add to it. The length \(n\) is absent too — the same weights accept a phrase or a page, and the only ceiling is the table of position tags. And depth is absent from the block, which is the whole point: stacking multiplies \(12d^2\) by the number of storeys, so weight grows linearly with depth and with the square of the width. Widening is the expensive dial; stacking is the cheap one.

Two more pieces stand outside the blocks, and both are ruled by the vocabulary rather than by \(d\): the embedding table at the door, one row per entry, and the projection back into the vocabulary at the exit. In a small machine they are a serious share of the whole, which is why the shrinking tricks of the variants act go after the vocabulary table first. And keep one boundary bright: this count is parameters — what the machine weighs at rest. It is not the price of a pass in arithmetic and not the price of generation in memory; those have axes of their own, and reading the bill straight off the wiring is what the next chapter is for.

BERT: reading the whole cloth at once

So far I have woven forward: thread after thread, never letting the shuttle peek at what comes next. But when the goal is not to continue the cloth but to understand a finished piece, that rule only ties my hands. To judge a word I want to feel what sits on both sides of it. So I take the loom's reading half — the encoder alone, no forward-only decoder bolted on — and I let attention cross every thread with every other. This is BERT: Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers, a stack of \(N\) encoder blocks and nothing else.

The word bidirectional is the whole point. The old way stitched two separate looms — one reading left-to-right, one right-to-left — and glued their edges together. BERT weaves one cloth in one pass, where through self-attention each thread sees its left and its right neighbours at the same instant. Not two passes taped shut; deep, two-sided context baked into every fibre.

That two-sidedness is exactly what understanding wants — classifying a sentence, tagging each name (NER), or scoring how well a query matches a passage. And that last one is our own thread: BERT is the encoder behind dense retrieval. The vectors we searched by, back when we lit the hyperspace lanes, were woven on a loom just like this one.

Three stamps at the door: what a thread carries into BERT

Before the first block ever touches a thread, BERT stamps each one three times. The token embedding says which word-piece the thread is. The segment embedding says which of the two segments it belongs to — the same mark \(E_A\) or \(E_B\) runs down a whole segment, so the loom can tell the first sentence of a pair from the second. The position embedding says where in the line it stands. The three stamps are simply added, coordinate by coordinate: \(x_i = \mathrm{token}_i + \mathrm{segment}_i + \mathrm{position}_i\) — one vector per position, and that sum is what enters the encoder.

Two special threads frame every sheet. \([CLS]\) opens it — a thread with no word of its own, woven in precisely so that after all the crossings it can serve as the aggregate of the whole cloth: classification heads read their verdict off it. And it is exactly this kind of aggregate that Sir Cosine will be comparing by angle once Lecture 10 assembles the bi-encoder. \([SEP]\) closes each segment — the seam between the two pieces of a pair.

One detail is easy to walk past: BERT's position stamps are learned — a trained table of tags, not the fixed sinusoidal marks of the original Transformer. Learned tags are more flexible — and finite: the table ends, and the cloth acquires a hard ceiling on its length.

Tokenosaurus returns: WordPiece

Which threads does the loom accept at all? Not whole words. The jaws you met back in Lecture 2 are back — Tokenosaurus, now biting by BERT's own rulebook, the WordPiece vocabulary. Frequent words pass whole; a rare word gets bitten into pieces that are in the fixed vocabulary. A continuation piece wears the mark \(\#\#\): playing becomes play \(\#\#\)ing — the second bite openly admitting it is not the start of a word.

The payoff is the end of the OOV (out-of-vocabulary) problem: there is no word the loom must refuse, because any word — a typo, a fresh coinage, a chemical name — can be assembled from pieces, down to single characters. And the special threads are citizens of the same vocabulary: \([CLS]\), \([SEP]\), \([MASK]\) are entries in the word-piece list like any other, each with its own embedding row.

Mending the cloth from both edges

Here is how BERT actually learns to weave. I take a finished sheet of cloth, and before the model ever sees it I punch small holes: I hide about 15% of the threads at random and dare it to guess each missing one back. That is masked language modelling — a fill-in-the-blank, cloze task. To mend the hole in the cat \([MASK]\) on the mat, the model must read the pattern from both edges at once — «cat» on the left, «on the mat» on the right — and only then reach for sat. This two-sided reading is the whole point: seeing the entire cloth is what makes BERT bidirectional.

One honest wrinkle in the recipe. Of the hidden 15%, not all become a literal \([MASK]\): the split is 80% \([MASK]\), 10% a random thread, 10% left untouched. Why the theatre? Because at fine-tuning time no \([MASK]\) ever appears — so if the loom only ever mended obvious holes, it would grow lazy everywhere else. The two special threads that frame every sheet, \([CLS]\) at the start and \([SEP]\) between segments, ride along untouched.

The first BERT wove with a second objective too — next-sentence prediction: given segments A and B, does B truly follow A, or is it a random stranger? The verdict is read straight off the \([CLS]\) thread. I tell you this plainly because later weavers found NSP a weak signal — RoBERTa dropped it and improved. The instinct to model how sentences connect survived; this particular stitch did not.

Mending one hole, in slow motion

Let me mend one hole in front of you — no numbers, just the motions. Take the sheet the cat \([MASK]\) on the mat: the third thread pulled out, the gap left open. The whole sheet goes through the encoder as usual, and every crossing is allowed: the empty position reaches left to "cat" and right to "on the mat" at the same instant. By the top of the stack the hole is no longer empty — it holds a contextual vector woven entirely from both banks of its neighbours.

Now the MLM head leans in: one linear layer over that vector, projecting it onto the whole vocabulary — one logit per word-piece. A softmax turns the row of logits into a distribution, and among candidates like sat, ran, is the mass pools on the one that agrees with both banks: the cat sat on the mat, because "cat" stands on the left and "on the mat" stands on the right. Either bank alone could mislead; together they pin the verb down.

The lesson is charged only at the holes: the training loss is cross-entropy at the masked positions and nowhere else. Punch holes, weave the sheet, guess the missing threads, pay for the misses — and that, repeated over an ocean of text, is the whole of BERT's schooling.

Why not always [MASK]: the 80/10/10 theatre

There is a trap hidden inside the mending lesson, and the first weavers spotted it. Suppose every hidden thread literally became \([MASK]\). The loom would then learn a lazy rule: work hard only where the mask token stands; everywhere else, coast. But at fine-tuning time — the entire life the model is actually built for — the token \([MASK]\) never appears at all. The loom would have trained itself on a signal its real work never shows: a clean train/inference mismatch.

Hence the little theatre. Of the threads chosen for hiding, only 80% become a literal \([MASK]\); 10% are swapped for a random thread, and 10% are left untouched — yet still must be predicted. Now no position grants the right to coast: an innocent-looking thread may be an impostor, an untouched one may be the exam. The only strategy that survives is to build an honest representation of every thread on the sheet — which is exactly what we wanted the loom to learn all along.

The stitch that didn't survive: NSP

The original BERT wove with a second lesson beside the mending — next sentence prediction. Take segments A and B: half the pairs are genuine — B truly follows A in the source text — and half are strangers pulled from anywhere. The verdict — follows / does not follow — is read off the \([CLS]\) thread, and the segment stamps \(E_A/E_B\) exist precisely so the loom can tell the two pieces apart.

The debate came later, and it is a good lesson in how the field corrects itself. The task turned out to be too easy: a random stranger usually comes from a different document entirely and talks about something else — so the loom passes the exam by merely matching topics, never learning the fine coherence of how one sentence hands the thread to the next. A signal you can cheat teaches little. RoBERTa ran the experiment squarely: drop NSP, keep everything else — and the cloth came out better. ALBERT kept the instinct but hardened the stitch: predict the order of two adjacent sentences — same topic by construction, so topic-matching no longer helps. The idea of modelling how sentences connect survived; this particular stitch did not.

Two passes taped together are not one loom

Here is the myth that survives every lecture on BERT: «bidirectional means two passes — one left to right, one right to left, glued at the seam». As a description of the loom BERT replaced, that is fair. ELMo ran two separate recurrent passes and concatenated their outputs after the fact, so no thread was ever conditioned on both sides at once: each pass built its picture in ignorance of the other, and the gluing happened at the end. BERT has one encoder, and inside it a thread's self-attention reaches left and right in the same instant, layer after layer. There, two-sidedness is taped on; here, it is woven in.

Now the question nobody asks, and the whole mechanism lives in it: if a full mask is so plainly better, why not keep the ordinary «name the next thread» lesson and simply let the model look both ways while learning it? Because then the thread would see itself. Ask the loom to name the thread at position \(i\) while position \(i\) lies on the cloth in front of it, and the honest answer is to copy it out. The loss would fall beautifully and the machine would learn nothing: that is not a hard exercise done well, it is a broken exercise.

So masking is not decoration on top of a bidirectional encoder — it is the permission to be bidirectional. Hide the thread, and looking right becomes honest, because the answer is no longer on the cloth. The two decisions are locked together and neither survives alone: a full mask with a next-thread objective is a leak, and a hidden-thread objective under a curtain would be a loom forbidden the very context its objective demands. When someone tells you a model is «bidirectional», ask for the second half of the sentence: what was hidden while it looked?

Weave one great bolt, then cut many garments

Here is the recipe that quietly changed everything. First I weave once, and I weave slow: for weeks the loom runs on an ocean of raw, unlabelled text — no one hands it answers, the cloth teaches itself by mending the holes I punch in it (masked-language-modelling). This first pass is dear. It burns the compute, it fills the bolt. But it happens exactly once, and out of it comes a single enormous bolt of general language — the pretrained base \(\theta\).

Then the cheap part begins. When a real task arrives — is this review sour or sweet? where in this passage does the answer lie? — I do not weave a new bolt. I take the same cloth and tailor it: pin on a light head, a single linear layer, and stitch briefly on a small labelled batch. Minutes, not weeks. A tiny garment, cut from a bolt that already knows the language.

That is transfer learning in one breath: pay the great cost once, then amortise it across dozens of tasks. The old way trained a fresh model per problem, always from the bare thread. The new way weaves the bolt one time and lets a hundred tailors cut from it — and that is why the whole field could suddenly move so fast.

One tower, snap-on heads

Here is what the tailoring looks like up close. The pretrained tower stays one and the same: threads go in, and out comes the fully woven sheet \(H\) — one contextual vector per position. On top of that sheet you pin a head, and the head is deliberately tiny. For classification — is the review sour or sweet? does one sentence entail the other? — the head is a single linear layer over the \([CLS]\) aggregate, softmax over the labels, done. For extractive question answering the head is two span pointers over the context threads: one scores every position as a possible start of the answer, the other as a possible end.

The tower is one; the heads are interchangeable — a different light attachment per task, snapped onto the same loom. And in the usual recipe the tower is not frozen under the head: base and head are stitched together, end to end — the whole cloth shifts slightly toward the task while the head learns to read it.

One yardstick for every tailor: GLUE, SuperGLUE, SQuAD

Once a hundred workshops are all tailoring from the same kind of bolt, one question decides everything: whose garment is better? Tailors who each sew their own garment and grade it themselves cannot be compared. So the field agreed on a common yardstick. GLUE is a fixed suite of language-understanding tasks: some over a single sentence (is it grammatical — CoLA; is the review sour or sweet — SST-2), some over a pair (does one entail the other — MNLI and RTE; are two questions paraphrases — QQP; how similar are two sentences — STS-B), each with its own agreed metric. SuperGLUE is its harder successor, cut for the day the first yardstick grew too easy. SQuAD stands apart: extractive question answering, where the answer must be found as a span inside a passage.

One trap the yardstick sets itself: STS-B, inside GLUE, measures the similarity of sentences — a pair task over whole sheets of cloth. It is not the word-similarity yardstick from the word-embedding lectures; those measured single threads. Same word "similarity", entirely different garments — keep the two sticks on separate hooks.

Base against large: what grows — and what refuses to

BERT ships in two sizes, and the difference is worth naming in plain words. Large is twice as deep — twice the stack of blocks; its threads are wider — a heftier hidden vector per position; and it looks through more attention heads in every block. Pile these together and the whole loom comes out more than three times heavier in parameters, with the bill to match: more memory to hold it, more compute per pass, fine-tuning slower and touchier.

Now the catch: one thing does not grow — the length of the cloth. Both sizes read a sheet up to the same fixed number of threads, because the ceiling is set by the learned position table, not by depth or width. A heavier loom weaves a richer cloth, not a longer one. When someone tells you "take large, our documents are long" — that is the trap: size buys depth of reading, and not a single extra thread of length.

The mending lesson in one line: the MLM objective

Everything the mending lesson does fits in one line: \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MLM}} = -\sum_{i \in M} \log P_\theta\!\left(x_i \mid x_{\setminus M}\right)\). Read it slowly. \(M\) is the set of masked positions — the holes. \(x_{\setminus M}\) is the sheet with those positions hidden — everything the loom is allowed to see. \(P_\theta\) is the model's distribution over the vocabulary at a hole, and \(-\log P_\theta(x_i \mid \cdot)\) is the price of a guess: near zero when the true thread was called near-certain, enormous when it was called near-impossible. The sum runs over the holes only — unmasked positions contribute nothing.

Set the writer's objective beside it, and the two families of this chapter part ways in the conditioning bar alone. An autoregressive model pays \(-\log P_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})\): the next thread, seeing only the left bank. MLM pays for a hidden thread seeing both banks at once. In that single difference lives all of BERT's strength as a reader — every thread is shown the whole sheet — and all of its weakness as a writer: it never once practised continuing a cloth.

And the objective carries two boundary-stones of its own. The sum touches only a small share of positions per pass — each sheet teaches less than it would teach a writer, so the schooling costs more over the same ocean of text. And the mask token the training leans on never appears at tailoring time — the gap the replacement theatre from the lesson above patches, and the one ELECTRA later removes altogether by judging every thread instead.

Four looms, one machine

After BERT, the workshop filled with cousins — and I want to be honest about them: none rebuilt the loom. All four are still encoder-only machines weaving the whole cloth bidirectionally. What each one fixed was the recipe, the parts, or the lesson — never the weave itself.

RoBERTa just trained the same loom more carefully: more thread, more epochs, a mask that reshuffles every pass instead of freezing, bigger batches — and it threw out NSP as dead weight. ALBERT built the same cloth from fewer parts — it factors the vocabulary embedding and lets every layer share one set of weights (though a slim loom still runs the full pass, so it saves memory, not compute).

DistilBERT puts a small apprentice loom beside the master and has it copy the teacher's soft choices — about 40% smaller, 60% faster, and still ~97% of the quality. ELECTRA changes the lesson: a tiny helper swaps in plausible fakes, and ELECTRA judges every single thread — real or replaced? — so the signal comes from all positions, not just the 15% you masked. Four cousins, one machine.

RoBERTa: the same loom, schooled harder

When the workshop first asked whether BERT could be beaten, the humblest answer won: don't touch the loom — fix the schooling. RoBERTa is the same encoder-only machine down to the last gear; what changed is the recipe. More cloth to practise on and longer at the bench, larger batches — and above all dynamic masking: the holes I cut for the mending lesson move to fresh places on every pass, instead of being cut once and forever — otherwise the loom slowly memorises where they are.

And one thing was thrown out entirely: NSP, the “do these two swatches come from one bolt” quiz, was judged dead weight — the loom weaves better without it. Pin the takeaway above the bench: RoBERTa's whole gain is a training recipe, not a new architecture. The objective is still MLM, the machine still reads the cloth both ways. Before you build a new loom, ask whether the old one was ever taught properly.

Two ways to shrink a loom

ALBERT shrinks the loom by reusing parts. Two independent tricks. Factorized embeddings: instead of one giant vocabulary table of size \(V\!\times\!H\), the thread passes through a narrow waist — \(V\!\times\!E + E\!\times\!H\) with a small \(E\) — so the dictionary no longer has to be as wide as the loom's hidden state. And cross-layer parameter sharing: every storey of the machine turns the same set of gears. But be honest about what this buys: fewer parameters is not less compute. The thread still climbs through all \(L\) storeys — a slim loom saves memory, and the forward pass stays full-length.

DistilBERT shrinks the loom the other way — it raises a smaller one outright, with the master standing beside it. Knowledge distillation: the apprentice machine learns to reproduce the teacher's soft probabilities — not the bare “the missing word was ‘bought’”, but the whole shading of how nearly it could have been ‘took’ or ‘sold’. Soft labels carry far more of the master's judgement than hard answers do, so the apprentice comes out smaller and faster while keeping almost all of the craft. Two strategies — and they don't compete: one shares gears inside the old machine, the other trains a new, smaller machine at the master's side.

ELECTRA: don't guess the patch — find the forgery

MLM has a quiet inefficiency: however long the lesson runs, the loom learns only from the positions you masked — the rest of the cloth just lies there. ELECTRA rewrites the lesson. A small helper-generator fills the masked places with plausible fakes, and the model itself — a discriminator — inspects the finished cloth thread by thread, answering for every position: original or replaced? That is replaced-token detection: \( \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RTD}} = -\sum_i \log D_\theta\big(\mathbb{1}[x_i=\tilde{x}_i] \mid \tilde{x}\big) \), and the sum runs over all positions. The same bolt of text now yields training signal from every thread, not just the 15% you hid.

Two boundary stones before you shelve this. It is not a GAN: the helper learns from an ordinary MLM loss, and no adversarial gradient flows between the two. And the verdict “real or fake” is binary — the discriminator never learns a distribution over the vocabulary, so you cannot turn ELECTRA around and make it generate. It is a better reader's schooling, and only that. One bonus, though: the mask gap closes — at fine-tuning the model sees ordinary sentences, exactly the kind it was schooled on, with no [MASK] token it will never meet again.

Same skeleton, different parts

The variants above all date from a two-year window around 2019–2020, and it is fair to ask what happened next. The short answer: for five years, almost nothing — and then ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024) arrived and showed that the thing worth changing was never the architecture. The skeleton you have just learned — embeddings, a stack of attention blocks, a head — is intact. What changed are the parts inside it.

Four swaps, each already familiar from this chapter. Absolute position embeddings give way to RoPE, so position enters as a rotation rather than an added vector. The GeLU activation becomes GeGLU, a gated variant. Padding tokens disappear from training and inference entirely — the unpadding trick, which stops the model from spending arithmetic on placeholders. And attention alternates: ordinary layers see only a 128-token local window, while every third layer looks globally.

That last one is the load-bearing change, and it is worth pausing on. Full attention over 8192 tokens would cost 8192² pairs per layer; a 128-token window costs a fraction of that, and the periodic global layer restores the long-range wiring the window cuts. The result is a native context of 8192 tokens — sixteen times BERT's 512 — at 149 million parameters in the base model against BERT-base's 110. Not a far bigger model: sixteen times the input for a third more parameters.

For a search course this is not trivia. The input length is the chunking limit — the one that will constrain us in the chapter on chunking. An encoder that holds 8192 tokens can cut a document where the thought ends rather than where the window does.

The trap of “newer is better”

Faced with four cousins, the reflex is to sort them by date and take the last one. Resist it — the four answer different questions, and the year on the label answers none of them. Quality is what you need and compute is cheap? RoBERTa's recipe. Memory is tight but latency isn't? ALBERT — fewer parts, though remember its full-length pass: it will not be faster. Serving speed and size? DistilBERT. The scarce thing is your pretraining budget itself? ELECTRA's denser lesson buys the most learning per pass.

And keep the map straight: only ELECTRA changes the objective; none of the four changes the weave — every one is still an encoder-only loom reading the whole cloth. So the choice is a matter of budget and task, never of novelty. Pick the loom for the cloth you must weave, not for the date stamped on its frame.

The decoder writes forward

I've shown you BERT reading the finished cloth — seeing every thread on both sides to mend a hole in the middle. But there is another way to weave, and it is how the loom creates rather than mends. Instead of reading all at once, I write forward: one thread, then the next, each chosen only from what already lies behind my hand. This is the decoder, and its craft is called autoregression — I predict the next token from the past alone, \( p(x) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \).

To make the loom honest, I hang a curtain over every thread ahead — a causal mask. Before the softmax that turns scores into attention, I add \( -\infty \) to any position \( j \gt i \), so the future collapses to exactly zero weight. Position \( i \) may only ever look at \( x_{\le i} \). If it could peek ahead, predicting the next thread would be cheating — the answer would already be in the weave.

So this is the great contrast of the whole loom. BERT sees the whole cloth — bidirectional, mending. The decoder writes it into being — one thread at a time, never peeking ahead. Same machine, same crossings of thread over thread; only the curtain differs. Draw it, and the loom stops reading and begins to generate.

Teacher forcing: schooled on the truth

How do you train a forward-weaving loom without waiting for it to write one thread at a time? By a trick called teacher forcing. During training the whole true text is already known, so I lay the ground-truth threads on the loom and let every position learn at once: each one, looking left through the causal curtain at true threads only, predicts its own next token. One parallel pass, a lesson at every position — that is why training a writer is fast.

At inference the truth is gone. The model writes a token, feeds its own output back into the input, and writes the next — strictly one at a time, standing on its own choices. The asymmetry has a name and a price: exposure bias — in school the loom always stood on true threads, but at the fair it stands on its own guesses, a situation it never once saw in training, and an early slip can pull the following threads askew. Keep the two regimes apart in your head: parallel over the truth when learning, sequential over itself when weaving.

The curtain, worked by hand

First, the identity that makes forward weaving legitimate. The probability of any sequence factorises exactly — \( P(x) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \). That is not a property of the model; it is probability theory's chain rule. The model merely parameterises each factor. All the loom needs is a way to compute those factors honestly — never letting position \(t\) see anything to its right. That is the whole job of the causal mask: to the attention scores, before the softmax, add \(M_{ij}=0\) for \(j\le i\) and \(-\infty\) for \(j\gt i\).

Now by hand. Séréga curtains off the right half of the cloth and stands at thread \(t_2\). Unmasked, \(t_2\) would score all three of \(t_1, t_2, t_3\) — and the future thread \(t_3\), being most like what comes next, would grab the largest weight. That is a leak: the answer already woven in. With the curtain, the score of \(t_3\) becomes \(-\infty\); the softmax turns it into a weight of exactly zero — not small, zero, since \(e^{-\infty}=0\) — and the remaining weight redistributes over \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), still summing to one. The order is the crux: mask before softmax. One triangular curtain — and the very same loom stops reading and honestly writes.

The KV-cache: a shelf of finished rows

Generation is one token per step — and a naive loom would re-measure the entire woven prefix at every step, as if seeing it for the first time. But look at what actually changes: the keys \(K\) and values \(V\) of past positions don't. Once a thread is woven, its \(K\) and \(V\) rows are fixed for good. So keep them — that is the KV-cache. Each new step computes \(Q, K, V\) for the one new token only; its fresh query then reads all the old keys and values straight off the shelf. Note what is not cached — the queries: each \(q\) is used once, on its own step, and discarded.

The trade is compute bought with memory, and it is worth knowing which axis grows. The shelf holds a \(K\) row and a \(V\) row for every past position, in every layer, for every head — and it grows by one row with each token you emit, for as long as the generation runs. The step itself, meanwhile, is cheap: one query row against the stored keys, a single row of scores — nothing like the full square matrix the prefix once cost. And one scope note: the cache speeds up inference only. In training there is nothing to cache — teacher forcing already computes the whole prefix in one parallel pass.

A decoder is not a lesser encoder

Two myths ride together here, and both are worth breaking. First: “a decoder is just for generating text — a crippled encoder that lost half its sight”. No. The curtain is not a defect; it is a different reading contract. The encoder's contract: see the whole cloth, both sides of every thread — and so it reads. The decoder's contract: see only the left side, because its craft is laying the next thread — and so it writes. And a next-token LM is no narrow gadget: the same single objective carries classification, translation, code, step-by-step reasoning — anything you can phrase as “continue this text”.

Second myth: “more parameters = more understanding”. The objective is to predict a plausible next token — plausible, not true. Scale buys fluency and reach; by itself it buys neither truth nor intent. Size is not alignment — that gap is exactly what RLHF exists to close, at the end of this chapter. So choose by contract, not by prestige: need to read a finished text deeply — take the reader; need to write one forward — take the writer.

From the top of the stack to an actual word

Follow one thread up past the last block and see what still has to happen. The stack hands back a table of exactly the shape it took in — one vector per position, still written in the loom's own coordinates, and a coordinate is not a word. One last linear map turns each row into a score for every entry of the vocabulary, so the table becomes as wide as the dictionary is long, and a softmax across that row is literally \(p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\). Everything the decoder claims to be lives in that final row: the chain-rule factorisation only becomes real once a distribution over actual words comes out.

Now look at the two ends of the machine together, because they are one journey travelled twice. At the door the embedding table reads a word into the width; at the exit the projection reads a width back into words. Both maps run between the same two spaces, and the usual move is to make them literally the same matrix — weight tying. It halves the largest single piece of furniture in a small loom, and it forces the two directions to agree on one geometry: a word's row is both how it enters and how it is scored on the way out.

Two consequences worth carrying. The size of this piece is set by the vocabulary, not by the width: every extra entry is another row to store and another score to compute at every step, which is why a fat dictionary is felt during generation and not only on disk. And notice what a decoder-only block does not carry: there is no cross-attention in it, because there is no second cloth to consult — only masked self-attention over what has already been written. The third attention arrives together with a second stack, and that is the act on the two-loom machine.

Which axis is the expensive one depends on the phase

Generation is not one regime but two, and they are priced differently — which is where wrong capacity plans come from. Prefill: the whole prompt goes through the loom once, so the query side is a block of rows and attention really does build the full square of pairs. That is where the quadratic bill of the efficiency act is actually paid. Decode: exactly one new thread arrives per step, so exactly one query row, one key row and one value row are computed; the step's scores are a single row against the shelf — one query against every stored key. No square is built at all.

Now the reading rule, and it inverts what most people carry away. While you are decoding, the expensive axis is not attention — it is the shelf. The KV-cache holds a key row and a value row for every past position, in every layer, for every head, and it grows by one row with every token you emit, for as long as the generation runs; the step's own arithmetic meanwhile stays that single row. Carry the square-table intuition into decoding and you will budget memory for the cheap thing and run out of it on the dear one.

Two things fall out. The quadratic does come back — but only when you sum the whole generation: one row, then two, then three, and the steps add up to a triangle. And a whole family of «long-context» tricks becomes legible from one question: which phase, which axis? The ones that let several heads share a single set of keys and values, or that page the shelf in and out, are not touching attention's arithmetic at all — they are shrinking the cache. Ask that question of anything sold to you as faster inference, and one sentence will tell you whether it helps the phase you are actually paying for.

GPT: the forward loom, grown enormous

BERT wove seeing the whole cloth at once. GPT keeps to the other loom I showed you — the one that weaves forward, one thread at a time, never peeking ahead. It is a decoder-only language model: pretrain it to guess the next token, \( p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \), on a mountain of raw text, and it learns to lay down thread after thread. GPT-1 (2018) still needed a small fine-tune per task; GPT-2 (2019), scaled up to 1.5B threads on its beam, could take the task phrased inside the prompt and answer zero-shot.

Then GPT-3 (2020) grew the very same forward loom to 175B parameters — and something new fell out of the mere size of it. In-context learning: pin a few worked examples to the beam — sea → море, sky → небо, tree → дерево — hand it cloud → ?, and it weaves back облако. I clipped no new gears, turned no screws. The examples only changed the context the loom is conditioning on, never its weights.

This is the surprise of scale I keep at the front of the workshop. The forward loom did not become smarter — it became big enough that a handful of threads at the top of the cloth is enough to tell it which pattern to weave. Few-shot is that, and no more: not learning, not understanding — a giant machine reading the hint you pinned and finishing the weave in kind.

One recipe, growing scale, a new interface

The GPT line is one recipe repeated with more of everything: a decoder-only loom trained on a mountain of raw text with the plainest objective there is — \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} = -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \). The first GPT proved the recipe: pretrain the forward loom, then fine-tune it for each task with a light head on top — the gears still re-tuned per job. GPT-2, grown much larger, showed something stranger: phrase the task inside the prompt itself, and the loom solves it zero-shot — with no per-task tuning at all. GPT-3, larger again, added the last step: show a few examples in the prompt, and it picks the pattern up few-shot.

Hold on to what actually changed along that line — and what didn't. The weave never changed: the loom writes forward, one thread at a time, from the first GPT to the last. What changed is the interface: from “pretrain, then re-tune the gears for every job” to “pretrain once, and state the job in words”. Scale did not build a new machine; it made the old machine answer to language.

In-context learning: examples pinned to the beam

Look closely at what those pinned examples do. In-context learning: a handful of worked input→output pairs goes into the prompt, the query goes last, and the loom completes it by analogy. The pairs are not decoration — they specify the task. Pin translation pairs and the same loom translates; pin label pairs and it classifies; swap the pins and the job changes, with the machine untouched. And there is no magic in the mechanism: the examples merely change the context on which \( p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \) is computed. The weights stay frozen; no gradient flows.

What this replaces is an entire fine-tuning run. Where the reader needed a labelled set and a training loop to become a classifier, here you show a few examples and simply ask. The price is symmetrical: nothing is kept. The task lives only in the prompt — cut the cloth off, and the next prompt starts from the same frozen weights, needing the same examples pinned all over again.

The reader and the writer

Set the two paradigms side by side and the chapter folds into one picture. BERT — encoder + fine-tune: the reader. It sees the whole cloth both ways, and to point it at a task you change it — pretrain once, then fine-tune a light head and the weights on a labelled set. Its home ground is understanding — classification, QA — wherever labels exist. GPT — decoder + prompt: the writer. It weaves forward under the curtain, and to point it at a task you talk to it — state the job, pin a few examples if need be; the weights stay untouched.

So the choice is not “which is better” but “whose contract fits”. A fixed task and a labelled set → the fine-tuned reader is precise and hard to beat. No labels, or a task that changes by the hour → the writer takes the job straight from a prompt. One loom, the same crossings of thread over thread — and two ways of bending it to a task: one adapts its weights, the other adapts its context.

Few-shot is not learning on the fly

The phrase “in-context learning” sets the trap all by itself: it tempts you to think the model trains on the examples in the prompt. It does not. No gradient step happens; not one weight moves. Few-shot is conditioning: the examples change only the context on which \( p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) \) is computed — the loom before the prompt and the loom after it are the same machine, bolt for bolt.

The test is simple: if this were learning, something would stay. Nothing does — take the examples away, and the behaviour goes with them; every new prompt starts from the same frozen weights. And carry over the companion caution from the decoder-misconception catch: the LM objective is to predict a plausible token, so a convincing few-shot answer is evidence of pattern-matching over the pinned examples, not of “understanding” the task. When the weights genuinely must change — that is fine-tuning, the reader's road, not this one.

The law that kept everyone growing the loom

Three times now the story has gone «and then they made it bigger», and three times I have not said why anyone believed that would work. The reason is a measured regularity rather than a hope. Across many multiples of size, the loss of a next-thread model falls as a power law in three quantities at once — the parameters in the machine, the text it is trained on, and the compute spent — and the curve is remarkably straight when both axes are read logarithmically. A power law promises no miracle: it is diminishing returns with a knowable exponent, where every equal step of quality costs a multiple of the last one rather than the same increment again.

What earns it the name is that it predicts before the run. Fit the curve on small looms you can afford and read off what a loom you have never built will cost to reach a given loss — that turns «shall we scale?» from a bet into a budget line, and it is precisely how the GPT line was argued for. The law says a second thing too: the three quantities must grow together. Pour parameters onto an unchanged pile of text and the measured curve peels away from the prediction, because the machine runs out of things to learn long before it runs out of capacity.

Now the boundary, and it is the half of the story that gets dropped. The law predicts the loss — the average price of guessing the next thread — and a loss is not a list of abilities. Not a line of it said that a large enough loom would take its instructions from examples pinned to the prompt: in-context learning was found, not forecast. So read scaling laws as the reason people kept building, not as an account of what came out: a smooth, boring curve underneath, and an argued-over, lumpy story of behaviour on top. Hold the two apart and the next act, where taste beats size, will not surprise you.

Choosing the next thread

The loom has woven every crossing and now, at the very edge of the cloth, it hands me not a word but a distribution — softmax over the whole vocabulary, a probability hanging on every possible next thread. The model is done thinking. Decoding is the separate craft of turning that fan of probabilities into one actual thread, again and again, until the cloth is finished.

The safe hands pull the brightest thread every time. Greedy takes \(\arg\max_i p_i\) — the single likeliest token. Beam search hedges: it keeps the \(b\) best partial weaves running in parallel and finishes the one with the highest length-normalized probability. Both are careful, both are deterministic — and both fray into the same flaw: on open-ended cloth they loop, they repeat, they go safely dull. Beam is a search, remember — never sampling.

So I sample instead, and I get two knobs. Temperature \(T\) reshapes the fan before I draw: \(p_i \propto e^{z_i/T}\), so \(T\lt1\) sharpens toward the favourite (and \(T\to0\) collapses back to greedy) while \(T\gt1\) flattens it toward daring. Then I snip the frayed tail: top-k keeps a fixed \(k\) brightest threads; top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose mass reaches \(p\), so the cut adapts — narrow when the loom is confident, wide when it hesitates. That is the one dial between a boring weave and an unhinged one.

The brightest stitch is a trap

Look closer at the careful strategies from the last beat — their vision has a real flaw. Greedy decoding takes \(\arg\max_i p_i\) at every step: the locally brightest stitch, laid at once and never reconsidered. But the loom judges cloth, not stitches — the probability of a whole weave is the product of its steps, and the thread that shines brightest right now can lead the pattern into a corner where every continuation is poor. Greedy cannot back out: it commits thread by thread, so one myopic choice taxes the entire cloth.

Beam search widens the gaze: keep the \(b\) best unfinished weaves — prefix hypotheses — extend each of them in parallel, and at the end finish the one whose probability, normalized by length, is highest. The normalization is no ornament: every extra stitch multiplies the product by a factor below one, so raw probability always votes for the shortest cloth, and without the correction the beam would cut every weave short. Two boundaries to keep. Beam is still a deterministic search, never sampling. And in open-ended weaving both habits fray the same way — repeats, loops, the safely dull cloth. That failure has a name, degeneration, and it is the reason the chapter's next stitches turn toward chance.

Temperature, written out

Before any tail-cutting comes one clean knob, and it has earned its formula: \(p_i = \frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}\). Here \(z_i\) are the logits — the raw scores the loom assigns each candidate thread before softmax — and \(T\) is the temperature that divides them all ahead of the exponent. That division is the whole trick: it stretches or shrinks the gaps between scores. With \(T\lt1\) the gaps widen, the exponent amplifies the leader, and the fan sharpens toward the favourite; with \(T\gt1\) the gaps compress and the fan flattens toward daring.

The two ends of the dial are worth walking to. Send \(T\to0\) and the division blows every gap up toward infinity: all the mass lands on the single largest logit, and sampling collapses back into greedy \(\arg\max\). Send \(T\to\infty\) and every \(z_i/T\) slides toward zero: the fan levels out toward a uniform draw, any thread as likely as any other — pure noise. Notice what temperature never does: it never reorders the candidates, it only redistributes belief among them. The knob reshapes the fan before the draw — it is not itself a way of choosing.

Cut the tail by count, or by mass

Why cut at all? Because the vocabulary fan drags a monstrous tail: thousands upon thousands of threads, each individually absurd, each carrying a sliver of probability. No single one of them is likely — but some absurd thread getting drawn is quite likely, and one bad stitch can derail the entire weave, since every later thread is chosen in its light. Truncated sampling keeps the dice in play but removes the cliff: first cut the tail, then draw from what remains, renormalized.

Two cutting rules. top-k keeps a fixed count — the \(k\) brightest threads — renormalizes, and samples. Simple, but the width never adapts: the same \(k\) whether the loom is certain or torn. top-p, the nucleus, keeps the smallest set of threads whose cumulative mass reaches \(p\): when one thread dominates, the nucleus shrinks almost to a point; when many threads are plausible, it widens to hold them. Do not blur the two: \(k\) fixes a count, \(p\) fixes a mass — and it is the mass rule that bends with the loom's own confidence.

One decoding step, by hand

Now line the knobs up and turn each one once, symbolically. The loom has just produced a vector of logits \(z\) over the candidate threads \(t_1, t_2, \dots\) — that is where every strategy starts, and they all read the same vector. First, temperature: divide, \(z_i \mapsto z_i/T\), sharpening or flattening the fan before anything is cut. Then softmax: \(p_i \propto e^{z_i/T}\) — logits become a probability on every thread. Then the cut: sort the threads by falling probability and walk down the list, summing as you go, until the running mass first reaches \(p\); those threads are the nucleus, and the frayed tail below them is discarded. Renormalize the survivors so they sum to one — and draw.

The drawn thread is stitched to the cloth, the whole loom runs forward once more, and a fresh fan of logits opens for the next stitch — decoding is this little ritual on repeat, one thread per turn. Notice what the walk teaches. The order is fixed: temperature reshapes first, the cut trims second, the draw always comes last. And the deterministic strategies turn out to be degenerate settings of the same ritual — greedy is «skip the draw, take the maximum», top-k is the same walk down the list with a count in place of a mass. One distribution, many knives.

Three Families, One Loom

Step back from the single loom, and you see it comes in three temperaments. Same machine — attention, feed-forward, residual, LayerNorm — but threaded three different ways, because the mask decides which threads are allowed to meet. Encoder-only (BERT, RoBERTa) opens the whole weave: every thread crosses every other, both directions, so it understands the finished cloth — classification, QA, search. Decoder-only (GPT, LLaMA) clamps a causal mask on: each thread sees only what came before, so it weaves forward, one new thread at a time — pure generation.

Between them sits the encoder-decoder (T5, BART): one loom reads the source cloth whole, a second weaves the answer forward — and a new stitch, cross-attention, joins them. There the decoder's threads form the questions \(Q\), while the keys and values \(K,V\) come from the encoder's finished reading. It is the shuttle that carries meaning from the cloth you read into the cloth you write — the natural shape for translation and summarisation, where output is a transformed input.

So it is one family portrait, three jobs. T5 treats every task as «text → text» and learns by mending dropped spans; BART corrupts the input every which way and asks the decoder to reconstruct the original. Three masks, three objectives — but one loom underneath. Choose the threading to fit the cloth you mean to weave.

The shuttle's formula: cross-attention

The shuttle that joins the two looms deserves its own formula: \(\operatorname{CrossAttn}(y, z) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q(y)\,K(z)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V(z)\). Read the arguments before the algebra: \(y\) is the decoder's state — the cloth being written, everything laid down so far; \(z\) is the encoder's output — the cloth already read, in full. The queries \(Q(y)\) come from the writer's own threads; the keys and values \(K(z), V(z)\) come from the reader's. Self-attention draws all three projections from one place — cross-attention is the same crossing with the sources split, and that split is the entire definition: every thread being written asks its questions of everything that was read.

Boundaries, so the picture stays honest. Decoder-only looms have no cross-attention — GPT carries only causal self-attention; the shuttle exists only where there are two cloths. The bridge is priced by the product of the two lengths — the writer's threads times the reader's — so on long documents the shuttle itself becomes the bottleneck. And keep one echo for the later chapters: «lay the retrieved documents into the context» is this very formula with the bridge moved out of the architecture and into the prompt — cross-attention is RAG's direct ancestor.

T5 and BART: two ways to live with two stacks

T5 answers «what do we do with two stacks?» with radical bookkeeping: every task becomes «text → text». Translation, summarization, classification, similarity — all of them the same move: read one cloth, weave another; a short task prefix («translate…», «summarize…») tells the loom which craft is wanted today. Its pretraining matches the shape: span-corruption denoising — whole spans of threads are torn out of the cloth, and the model learns to re-weave the missing pieces at the output.

BART answers the same question by pedigree: take a bidirectional encoder — a reader of BERT's kind — and bolt on an autoregressive decoder — a writer of GPT's kind. Its pretraining is a denoising autoencoder taken literally: spoil the input cloth every way at once — mask threads, shuffle them, delete them outright — and demand that the decoder reconstruct the original, whole. That habit of mending ruined cloth is exactly why it shines at summarization and generation. Two philosophies, one architecture: T5 unifies the format of the tasks, BART unifies the damage to recover from — and both live on the cross-attention bridge you have just seen.

When two stacks are honestly needed

Now the honest question: each stack works alone — the encoder understands, the decoder generates — so what are you paying for both for? For a task that itself splits into two halves. Translation is the cleanest case: you must understand the whole source sentence at once — the encoder's craft, the full mask — yet you can only write the answer left to right — the decoder's craft, the causal mask. Neither half closes the task alone: the reader does not write, and the writer never sees the source whole. Summarization has the same anatomy. Wherever the output is a transformed input, the two-loom machine with its cross-attention bridge is the natural shape.

And when is one stack enough? Whenever the task is open continuation — and, ever more often, whenever you can simply lay the source into the prompt: a decoder-only loom then reads it through the same causal self-attention, the bridge moved from the architecture into the context. Keep the mask table as your compass: full mask — understanding; causal — generation; full + causal + cross — transformation. Choose the loom by the cloth, not by fashion.

When the cloth is too wide to cross every thread

Attention has one beautiful, ruinous habit: it crosses every thread with every other. On a short scarf that is nothing — 512 threads, and the crossing matrix is half a megabyte a head. But length enters quadratically, and the loom does not forget it. Take the cloth to \(32\text{k}\) threads and the score matrix alone swells to \(2.15\,\text{GB}\) per head — near \(26\,\text{GB}\) across twelve heads, for one layer's crossings. That \(O(n^2)\) is the wall I hit on any truly long document.

There are three ways past it, and I keep them straight in my hand. Sparse, windowed weaving — Longformer, BigBird — crosses each thread only with its neighbours plus a few global anchor-threads. Linear weaving — Linformer, Performer — approximates the full crossing at \(O(n)\). Both are honest bargains: they trade a little fidelity for a lot of room, so the cloth is no longer bit-for-bit the one you'd have woven the slow way.

And then the sly one — FlashAttention. It changes nothing in the pattern: same softmax, same result up to floating-point summation order. It just refuses to lay the whole \(n\times n\) out on the slow table (HBM). Instead it works block by block on the fast bench (SRAM), keeping a running edge — an online softmax of running max and sum — so memory falls \(O(n^2)\!\to\!O(n)\) with the weave identical. When the cloth is huge, you don't cross it all at once. You cross it in windows.

Every thread against every thread: the quadratic wall

Where exactly does the wall stand? In the score matrix. Attention computes \(E = QK^{\top}\): one score for every pair of threads, an \(n \times n\) sheet — per head, per layer. Double the length of the cloth and the crossings do not double, they quadruple; stretch it tenfold and the sheet grows a hundredfold. On a short scarf the sheet is a trifle; walk the length out to a real document — a contract, a book chapter — and the score sheets alone, head after head, layer after layer, swallow the whole bench. That is what \(O(n^2)\) means once it stops being notation and starts being memory.

And note what kind of cost this is. The arithmetic itself — the multiplications — is the smaller half of the pain; the killer is materializing the \(n \times n\) sheet in the loom's slow, distant memory and hauling it back and forth. That diagnosis writes the prescription twice over: you can keep the mathematics and change the order of the work, so the sheet never exists whole — or change the mathematics itself, so there is less sheet to make. The next two beats open those two doors in turn.

Tiles and a running edge: how Flash weaves

FlashAttention opens the first door: same mathematics, different order. The rule is blunt — the \(n \times n\) sheet is never written to the slow table (HBM). Instead, tiling: cut \(Q\), \(K\), \(V\) into blocks, carry one small pair of blocks at a time onto the fast bench (SRAM), compute that patch of scores where it lies, fold its contribution into the output — and let the patch vanish before the next one arrives.

The subtle part is that softmax seems to need the whole row — its maximum for stability, its sum for normalization — before any single weight is right. The online softmax dissolves the objection: keep a running maximum \(m\), a running sum \(\ell\), and a running output \(O\), and let each new block correct everything accumulated so far; when the last block has passed, the row equals exactly the softmax you would have computed whole. Memory falls from \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n)\) — and read the savings correctly: not fewer multiplications, but less memory traffic, fewer trips to the slow table. The weave itself — unchanged.

The second door: approximate the crossing

The second door changes the mathematics itself. Linear attention shrinks the crossing by algebra: Linformer projects \(K\) and \(V\) down to low rank — as if the reader's threads were retold in a few representative strands before anyone crosses them; Performer (FAVOR+) replaces the softmax kernel with random features, so the crossing factorizes. Sparse attention shrinks it by geography: Longformer and BigBird let each thread cross only a local window of neighbours, plus a few global anchor threads that everyone may consult. All of them come out at \(O(n)\).

Now the price tag, in one line: all of these approximate full softmax attention. The cloth is no longer the one the slow loom would have woven — usually close, never guaranteed identical; that fidelity is what you spend to buy the length. It is an honest bargain — but it is a bargain, which is exactly what the sly door of the previous beat never was, and that distinction is the next beat's whole point.

Flash is not an approximation

The two doors get blurred so often that the confusion deserves its own stitch. The myth: «FlashAttention is a linear or approximate attention — it changes the formula.» The truth: FlashAttention computes the very same softmax attention — not an approximation; the result matches the standard computation up to the order of floating-point operations, because tiling reorders the summation and touches nothing else. It is an IO trick: the win is in memory and traffic, \(O(n^2)\!\to\!O(n)\), not in the mathematics. The approximators are the other family — Linformer, Performer, Longformer, BigBird. A field test to carry away: if switching it on changed your model's quality, then whatever you switched on was not FlashAttention.

The writer pays for a square and uses half of it

Take the act's ruler back to the curtain, because the writer's bill has a wrinkle the reader's does not. The score sheet is \(n \times n\) — a row per query, a column per key. The causal mask kills the strictly upper triangle: every pair where the key stands to the right of the query goes to \(-\infty\), and that is about half the square, decided in advance and on principle, for every sheet the machine will ever weave.

Now the part that surprises people the first time. The mask changes what the softmax sees, not what the memory holds. The sheet is laid out at full size before the mask ever touches it, and \(-\infty\) takes exactly as many bytes as any other number, so a naive causal loom pays the reader's whole quadratic bill for a sheet half of which it has promised in advance never to use. The wall is not «the reader's problem»: it is the same wall, paid at twice the honest price.

Which is why the tiling door two beats back is not merely convenient for a writer — it is its natural fit. Work the sheet block by block and a block lying wholly inside the forbidden triangle need never be computed, never be held, never travel to the slow table at all; the running edge asks only for the tiles it is standing on. So read the efficiency act with the phase in mind and the picture closes: it is prefill that lays this square out, a decode step is a single row, and the trick that saves the reader memory saves the writer arithmetic it was buying for nothing.

Fitting the cloth to the wearer

A freshly pretrained loom is a marvel — and stubborn. It has learned only to weave the most plausible next thread, so ask it a question and it may answer with another question, wander off the pattern, or hand you something coarse. It is skilled, not obliging. Prompting alone won't tame it: a clever request is just how I hold the shuttle, not a change in the loom itself. To make it helpful, honest, harmless I must adjust the machine to the wearer's taste — and that adjustment has three passes.

First I sit it beside good work — demonstrations of instruction → answer — and let it imitate the format. This is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Then, because I cannot write a rule for "nicer," I let people be the judges: I lay two finished pieces side by side and they point to the better one, \(y_w \succ y_l\). From thousands of such verdicts a second little machine, the reward model, learns to guess the human's thumb — a single number for any cloth. Finally I let the loom weave freely and nudge every choice toward that reward with PPO, tied by a short leash \(-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\) back to the SFT weave so it never drifts into strange, gamed patterns.

And here is the quiet marvel: taste beats size. A small loom fitted this way — InstructGPT at 1.3B — is preferred over a base machine a hundred times larger. RLHF adds no new facts (those came from pretraining); it only aligns behavior. The simpler path, DPO, skips the reward model and the RL loop and optimizes the same preferences directly. But I stay watchful: chase the reward too hard and the loom starts flattering the judge — weaving what earns a thumbs-up rather than what is true. Goodhart's warning at the loom — when the measure becomes the target, it stops measuring.

The fitting in three passes: SFT → RM → PPO

Watch the master's hands: the fitting is three distinct passes, each with its own objective. Pass one — SFT, supervised fine-tuning: sit the loom beside demonstrations «instruction → answer» and maximize \(\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\big[\log \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big]\) — plain imitation, and what it teaches is the format of answering, the posture of an obliging weave.

Pass two — the reward model: from human verdicts \(y_w \succ y_l\), a separate small machine \(r_\phi(x,y)\) learns to score any cloth with one number — the customer's taste, cast into a scalar (the next beat opens it up). Pass three — PPO: now the loom weaves freely, and reinforcement learning nudges the policy toward the reward — \(\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{SFT}}\big)\). The second term is the short leash: the KL penalty holds the weave near its SFT manners, so the loom neither drifts into strangeness nor learns to play the reward itself. Base loom → SFT → RM → PPO — that is the whole fitting room, Séréga adjusting the pattern to the wearer pass by pass.

A proxy for taste

You cannot write a rule for «nicer» — so stop trying and collect verdicts instead. For one instruction \(x\), show people two finished cloths and let them point: \(y_w\) preferred over \(y_l\). The reward model trains on those comparisons with \(\mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\) — push the winner's score above the loser's — until it can hand any cloth a single number. Two facts to hold onto. The RM learns from comparisons, not from gold answers, because people point at the better piece far more reliably than they write a perfect one. And the RM is not the final model: its number is a training signal for PPO, and at inference the wearer talks to the policy loom — never to the judge.

And name what that number is: a proxy. The reward model guesses the human thumb; it is not the taste itself, only its stand-in — and a proxy is Goodhart's home ground. Press the loom hard enough toward the scalar and it will find weaves that score high without being better — flattering the judge instead of the wearer. That is reward hacking, the exact drift the KL leash exists to restrain: when the measure becomes the target, it stops measuring.

A small fitted loom beats a giant

Here is the InstructGPT result, stated with no scoreboard. Put two weaves in front of people — one from a small loom that went through the whole fitting, SFT and RLHF; the other from a base loom more than a hundred times its size — and by human preference the small fitted loom wins, decisively. Not on one lucky prompt — as the standing verdict of the comparison.

Read it as the act's payoff. The giant does not know less — knowledge lives in pretraining, and by that measure the big loom holds vastly more cloth-lore. It simply does not do what is asked. Alignment is about behavior, not size — and a modest budget of human preference, sewn in through the three passes, buys more usefulness than another leap of scale. Séréga would put it plainly: the wearer never asks how large your loom is — the wearer asks whether the cloth fits.

DPO: the same taste, without the RL loop

The three-pass fitting works, but it is heavy machinery: a separate judge to train, an RL loop to keep stable. DPO — direct preference optimization — asks the sharp question: if everything begins with pairs \(y_w \succ y_l\), why not learn from the pairs directly? One classification loss on the policy itself: \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\Big)\). Read it aloud: raise the preferred weave's probability relative to the reference loom, push the rejected one down — with \(\beta\) as the same short leash that KL played in PPO. No explicit reward model, no RL loop.

Simpler and more stable than the full RLHF pipeline — but do not mistake the family: DPO is still preference optimization on pairs, the same goal reached with fewer moving parts. The customer's taste still enters as comparisons; only the middleman is gone.

Two myths about the fitting

Myth one: «train a big enough loom on enough text, and it will come out obedient — or a clever prompt will make it so.» No. A base loom has learned exactly one skill — lay the plausible next thread — and plausibility is not obedience: ask it a question and it may weave a question back, wander off the pattern, or hand you something coarse. «Helpful, honest, harmless» is a separate objective that pretraining never optimized, and prompting is not alignment: a prompt is conditioning at inference — how you hold the shuttle — while the fitting changes the loom's own weights.

Myth two: «RLHF is just fine-tuning on the correct answers, and the model knows more afterwards.» Wrong twice. The preference passes learn from comparisons — which of two cloths is better — not from a book of gold answers; and they add no knowledge at all: facts come from pretraining, the fitting only aligns behavior. The act's risks live in the same place — reward hacking and sycophancy, the loom placating its judge. And keep the last boundary crisp: few-shot examples in the prompt are not RLHF — one conditions the loom for a single weave, the other refits the machine itself.

Why a search course walks past its own border

Let me name the border honestly before we leave it behind. Nothing in the fitting room brings a query and a passage closer together: the three passes change behaviour, not the geometry a retriever searches in. So half of this chapter — the writer, the decoding knobs, the reward model, the short leash — lies past the border of search proper, and no exam question stands on it.

It stays in the book because the search systems of the later chapters are made of exactly these parts, and made of them without saying so. The machine that turns retrieved passages into an answer is a fitted decoder read with the knobs of the decoding act — temperature, the cut, the width of the beam — and every one of those knobs decides how factual the answer will sound. The machine you then point at your own system, to score whether the answer was any good, is that same writer once more, asked to prefer one cloth to another. Read that twice: the moment a fitted loom judges your search, the reward model's own disease comes with it — the judge is a proxy for the taste you actually wanted, and a proxy is Goodhart's home ground.

So carry the map, not the trivia. The reader is your retriever and your reranker: full mask, understanding, a light head. The writer is your generator and your judge: causal mask, continuation, knobs at the exit. The two-stack machine is the ancestor of «lay the retrieved documents into the context», with the bridge moved out of the architecture and into the prompt. When a later chapter says «and an LLM does the rest», ask the three questions this act has handed you: which family, under which mask, with which knobs? Past the border it is ordinary engineering, and it stops being magic exactly when you can name the parts.

The whole loom, in one glance

Step back from the loom with me and look at the finished cloth. We began with the villain — the RNN bottleneck, one overloaded thread forced to carry the whole sentence and fraying on long inputs. Attention cut it loose: let every thread cross every other, weigh what matters, and read meaning where the old Lexical Gremlin only checked whether words matched. Stack that into a block — attention, feed-forward, residual, LayerNorm, position — and repeat it, and you hold the whole machine.

One block, three ways of weaving. Read the whole cloth under a full mask: that is BERT (encoder, MLM) — understanding and classification. Weave forward, one thread at a time, never peeking ahead behind a causal mask: that is GPT (decoder, next-token LM) — generation. Turn one cloth into another through cross-attention: that is the encoder-decoder (T5, BART) — translation, summarization. Then choose each next thread — greedy or beam when you want facts, top-p with temperature when you want open weaving; drop to windowed or sparse attention when the cloth outgrows what \(O(n^2)\) can afford; and let a master weaver retune the whole pattern to the wearer's taste with RLHF.

This is the machinery humming under everything we build. Dense and contextual retrieval — the encoders that turn a query and a document into vectors close enough to find each other — are Transformer blocks, woven exactly here. So carry one shape out of this chapter: every thread meets every other, and the cloth remembers the whole. One thing the loom does not tell you is what all that crossing costs — and that bill is exactly where the next chapter starts: The Wiring Diagram redraws this same machine as a circuit, where a wire is an axis, and the price of every crossing can be read straight off the picture. Thank you for standing at the loom with me.

Two scripts, one skeleton

Step off the metaphor for a moment and onto the keyboard: the whole chapter compresses into two short scripts, and they share one skeleton — tokenizer → model → head. The BERT script loads a tokenizer and a sequence-classification model, feeds the text in, and reads the answer off the classification head sitting on the \([\text{CLS}]\) thread: a softmax over the logits, done. The details are the chapter in miniature: the cutter is WordPiece, the context window is finite so long cloth gets truncated, and mean-pooling over the token vectors stands as the alternative to \([\text{CLS}]\).

The GPT script swaps every organ but keeps the skeleton: a causal LM instead of a classifier, byte-level BPE instead of WordPiece — a different cutter for the same trade — and instead of one softmax read-out, a generate call whose arguments you can now read like sheet music: do_sample switches the dice on (off, and you are back to greedy or beam), temperature reshapes the fan, top_k and top_p cut the tail by count or by mass, num_beams widens the deterministic search. The KV-cache hums underneath by default, and FlashAttention is one argument away.

One skeleton, three swappable organs — the mask, the cutter, the head. That is the practical residue of the whole loom: choose those three to fit your cloth, and the rest is a handful of lines.
