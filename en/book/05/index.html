The Proving Grounds

I'm Serega, and I've built two search systems. Both look fine. I ran a few queries through each, the results looked reasonable, I nodded sagely. One of them is better than the other. Which one? I genuinely cannot tell you, and "looks good to me" is precisely how you ship garbage with total confidence.

Here's the trap. Every chapter from here on is going to make a claim — neural beats BM25, reranking helps, this loss is better than that one — and every one of those claims is hot air until I can put a number on it. Before we touch a single neural network, we need an honest scoreboard: a way to turn "this ranking feels better" into a measurement I'd bet money on. Welcome to the Proving Grounds, where every Ship gets tested and opinions don't get a vote.

You can eyeball ten results. You can squint at a hundred. You cannot eyeball ten thousand queries, twice a day, on every single code change — and that's the real workload, because search teams ship constantly and each ship can quietly break something. Eyeballing doesn't scale, and worse, it doesn't catch the small regressions: the change that makes results 2% worse on average is invisible to a human and catastrophic to a business.

So we need a metric — a number per ranking. But a metric is useless without something to compare against: you can't grade an exam without an answer key. That answer key is called ground truth, and for search it means knowing, for some set of queries, which documents are actually relevant. Get that, and a vague feeling becomes arithmetic. The whole rest of this chapter is two questions: where does the answer key come from, and how do we turn it into a fair score?

Ground truth

The answer key has a name in IR: relevance judgments, or qrels for short. At bottom a qrel is the simplest possible thing — a label on a query-document pair: this document is relevant to this query, or it isn't. Pile up enough of those labels and you can grade any ranking any system produces, because you finally know what "right" looks like.

Real test collections get these labels from human annotators, slowly and expensively, which is a whole problem we'll dig into next. For our worked example we'll borrow a clean shortcut we set up earlier: our 20-Newsgroups documents already carry category labels from when they were posted. So for the query intent "space", we'll simply call a document relevant exactly when it comes from the space category. It's a stand-in, not a real human judgment — but it's consistent and reproducible, and it lets every ranking we built in the last chapter finally get a grade.

Where the answer key comes from

Borrowing category labels is a teaching trick. Building a real answer key is one of the hardest, least glamorous jobs in the field, and it's worth understanding because every number downstream inherits its sins. First decision: how fine-grained are the labels? The simplest is binary — relevant or not. But that throws away an obvious truth: some relevant documents are more relevant. So serious collections use graded relevance, often a 0-to-4 scale from "off-topic" through "marginally useful" up to "perfect answer." Binary is easy to collect and easy to compute; graded captures what users actually feel. We'll use both.

Second problem, and it's a brutal one: you cannot judge every document. A real collection has millions of them and a query has, what, a few dozen genuinely relevant? Paying humans to label millions of obvious non-matches per query is impossible. The test-collection paradigm itself goes back to the Cranfield experiments of the 1960s (which judged near-exhaustively); the trick the field landed on for large collections is pooling, introduced as a sampling technique in the 1970s and industrialised by TREC: run many different systems, take the top-k results from each, pool them together, and only have humans judge that pool. The bet is that between all those systems, almost every relevant document surfaces somewhere in the top-k, so the pool catches what matters without judging the ocean. (Sanderson (2010) is the survey of this whole offline-evaluation machinery, and Voorhees (2000, Information Processing & Management) showed the reassuring part: even when human assessors disagree, the rankings test collections produce stay remarkably stable.)

It's a good bet, but it leaves a sharp edge. What about a relevant document that no system ranked highly, so it never entered the pool? It's unjudged — and here's the quiet bias that trips up newcomers: most metrics treat "unjudged" as "not relevant." Which means a genuinely new system that surfaces good documents the old systems missed can get punished by an old pool, because its discoveries score as zeros. Metrics like bpref were invented specifically to soften this. The lesson: a test collection isn't neutral ground. It quietly encodes whatever the systems that built its pool already believed.

The guideline is the instrument

Before asking whether two assessors agree, ask a blunter question: agree about what? A query is a couple of words. A document is a page. Somewhere between them, somebody had to decide what the query means — and that decision does not live in the assessor's head. It lives in a written annotation guideline that pins the intent down: a query about space means spaceflight and astronomy, not free room on a disk. The guideline is not documentation of the measurement. It is the measuring instrument.

Which makes it the first place a scoreboard can go crooked, and the crookedness is invisible everywhere downstream. Loosen the definition of relevant and every system's score rises together — nothing improved, you moved the ruler. Write a guideline that quietly resolves an ambiguous query one way, and you have decided in advance which of your systems is right: a system answering the other reading now earns zeros on documents that are excellent answers to a question the guideline chose not to ask. The number that comes out the far end is exact, reproducible, and exact about a question a stranger wrote down.

Now put that next to the cost wall, and pooling stops looking like a money-saving trick. The pairs to judge are documents multiplied by queries; for any realistic collection that product is astronomical, while a query's genuinely relevant documents number in the dozens. Exhaustive judging is not expensive, it is impossible — so every real test collection judges a sample, and the sampling rule becomes part of the instrument alongside the guideline. Two written decisions, then, stand between the world and every number in this chapter: what counts as relevant, and what gets looked at. Neither is measured. Both are chosen.

Do the annotators even agree?

Now the question nobody wants to ask out loud: are the labels even right? Hand the same query-document pair to two trained annotators and they will sometimes disagree — relevance is genuinely a judgment, not a fact, and "is this article about space useful for someone searching space" has fuzzy edges. If your two judges flip a coin's worth of the time, your gold standard isn't gold; it's noise wearing a crown.

So we measure how much they agree, and we don't measure it naively. Two annotators who both say "relevant" 90% of the time will agree most of the time purely by chance, which tells you nothing. The fix is Cohen's kappa (and Fleiss' kappa for more than two judges): it measures agreement above what chance alone would produce, so a high kappa means the judges are really tracking the same signal, not just both leaning the same way. When kappa is low, you don't ship a sharper model — you go fix your annotation guidelines, because no metric computed on noisy labels can be trusted, no matter how clean the arithmetic looks. Your scoreboard is only ever as honest as the people who built its answer key.

What kappa subtracts — and what it cannot

Kappa is a single fraction, and it is worth writing out before you trust it: \(\kappa=\dfrac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}\). The numerator takes the agreement you actually counted, \(p_o\), and subtracts the agreement two judges with those same labelling habits would have stumbled into by accident, \(p_e\). The denominator is what was left above accident to be earned. So kappa never answers how often did they agree. It answers what share of the earnable agreement did they earn — a ratio, and its denominator moves.

Now watch that denominator on a real pool, because this is exactly where a competent annotation team scores badly. Pooled judging hands assessors a heap in which nearly everything is off-topic, so both judges say not relevant nearly always — and two habits that lopsided collide by chance nearly always too. \(p_e\) climbs toward the top of its range, \(1-p_e\) collapses, and an honest numerator ends up divided by almost no room at all. That is the prevalence paradox: raw agreement close to perfect, kappa close to the floor, and nothing whatever wrong with the judges. Quote the raw agreement and the label rates beside kappa, or the number cannot be read.

Then the consequence that reaches every other figure in this chapter. Label noise is a floor on resolution: two systems whose true difference is smaller than the disagreement between your judges cannot be told apart by any metric computed on those judgments, however many decimal places it prints. Agreement is therefore not a hygiene step you clear before the interesting part — it sets the smallest difference the Proving Grounds is able to see at all. And when it comes back low, the repair is upstream, in the guideline that is the instrument, never downstream in a sharper test.

The metrics, by the numbers

Let me pin those metrics to actual arithmetic, because every one of them is just careful counting and you should be able to redo it by hand. Take the real BM25 ranking we just graded — eight results, and using our qrels the relevant/not pattern down the list is [0,1,0,1,0,1,0,1]: rank 1 is a miss, rank 2 a hit, alternating to the bottom. Four of the eight are relevant, and all four relevant documents are in the list, so 4 is also the total relevant count.

Recall@k asks: of all the relevant documents, how many have we found by rank k? At k=1 we've found none → 0. By k=3 we've caught one of four → 0.25. By k=5, two of four → 0.5. By k=8, all four → 1.0. It climbs 0 → 0.25 → 0.5 → 1.0, and it can only ever go up as k grows. Precision@k asks the complementary question: of the k results we've shown, what fraction are relevant? At k=1, zero of one → 0. At k=3, one of three → 0.333. At k=5, two of five → 0.4. At k=8, four of eight → 0.5. So precision runs 0 → 0.333 → 0.4 → 0.5. Recall rewards finding everything; precision rewards not wasting the user's slots — and on this ranking they pull in different directions exactly as the theory promises.

MRR — mean reciprocal rank, \(\mathrm{MRR}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_i \frac{1}{\text{rank}_i}\) — cares only about the first hit. The first relevant document sits at rank 2, so the reciprocal rank is \(\frac{1}{2}=\mathbf{0.5}\); if the very first result had been relevant it would be \(\frac{1}{1}=1.0\), and a first hit at rank 10 would score only 0.1. That's the known-item metric: get the one right answer to the top, or pay for every rank it sits below.

Two of these only show their teeth across multiple queries, so add a second query whose pattern is [1,0,1,1,0,0,1,0] — a strong one that hits at rank 1. Its reciprocal rank is \(\frac{1}{1}=1.0\), so averaging the two queries gives \(\mathrm{MRR}=\frac{0.5+1.0}{2}=\mathbf{0.75}\) — a number equal to neither query alone, which is the whole point of a mean. MAP goes one level deeper: average precision (AP) averages the precision at each rank where a relevant document appears. For the second query the hits land at ranks 1, 3, 4, 7, with precisions 1.0, 0.667, 0.75, 0.571, so \(\mathrm{AP}=\frac{1.0+0.667+0.75+0.571}{4}=\mathbf{0.747}\); the first query's AP works out to 0.5. Average the two and \(\mathrm{MAP}=\frac{0.5+0.747}{2}=\mathbf{0.6235}\).

Now the headline metric, \(\mathrm{nDCG}\) (the cumulated-gain metric of Järvelin & Kekäläinen (2002)), the only one of the five that cares where on the list each hit lands. It rests on one idea: a relevant document at rank 1 is worth more than the same document at rank 8, so each gain gets discounted by how far down it sits. The discount is \(\frac{1}{\log_2(\text{rank}+1)}\), which at rank 1 is \(\frac{1}{\log_2 2}=1.0\) (full credit), at rank 2 is \(\frac{1}{\log_2 3}=0.6309\), at rank 4 is 0.4307, and keeps shrinking — 0.3562 at rank 6, 0.3155 at rank 8. DCG is just the sum of (gain × discount) down the list, \(\mathrm{DCG}=\sum_i \frac{g_i}{\log_2(i+1)}\). With binary gains, only our four hits at ranks 2, 4, 6, 8 contribute, so \(\mathrm{DCG}=0.6309+0.4307+0.3562+0.3155=\mathbf{1.7333}\). But a raw DCG isn't comparable across queries — a query with more relevant documents can pile up more gain — so we normalise by the ideal DCG: the score the perfect ranking would get if it sorted every relevant document to the very top. Here the ideal puts all four hits at ranks 1–4, so \(\mathrm{IDCG}=1.0+0.6309+0.5+0.4307=\mathbf{2.5616}\). Divide: $$\mathrm{nDCG}=\frac{DCG}{IDCG}=\frac{1.7333}{2.5616}=0.6766$$ That's our BM25 ranking's score — one comparable number in \([0,1]\), where 1.0 means "already perfect."

Binary gains throw away the obvious truth that some hits are better hits, so real nDCG uses graded gains. Re-grade the same four space documents — two strong ones (a clear, on-topic match) get gain 3, two marginal ones get gain 1 — and the arithmetic re-runs with those gains in place of 1s. The actual ranking now scores \(\mathrm{DCG}=3\cdot 0.6309 + 3\cdot 0.4307 + 1\cdot 0.3562 + 1\cdot 0.3155 = 3.8565\), against an ideal \(\mathrm{IDCG}=5.8235\) that front-loads the grades 3, 3, 1, 1, giving graded nDCG = 0.6622. (Many systems instead weight gains as \(2^{g}-1\), which punishes burying a great result even harder; that variant lands at 0.6563 here.) Notice graded and binary nDCG disagree slightly — 0.66 vs 0.68 — because they reward different things, which is exactly why you state which one you used. Five metrics, one pile of counting — and now you can reproduce every one of them without trusting a widget.

One query can lie. So bring two. Query 1 hits at ranks 2, 4, 6, 8; Query 2 hits at ranks 1, 3, 4, 7. MRR and MAP only show their teeth across a query *set*, so we score each query, then average.

Reciprocal rank cares only about the *first* hit. Query 1's first relevant result sits at rank 2 → RR = 1/2 = 0.5. Query 2 strikes at rank 1 → RR = 1/1 = 1.0. Two queries, two very different reciprocal ranks.

Average precision averages the precision at *each* rank where a relevant doc appears. Query 1 works out to AP = 0.5. Query 2's hits at ranks 1, 3, 4, 7 give precisions 1.0, 0.667, 0.75, 0.571, so AP = (1.0 + 0.667 + 0.75 + 0.571) / 4 = 0.747.

First average the reciprocal ranks across the set: MRR = (0.5 + 1.0) / 2 = 0.75. The mean of the two first-hit scores — Query 1 struck at rank 2, Query 2 at rank 1, and 0.75 sits between them.

Now average the average precisions: MAP = (0.5 + 0.747) / 2 = 0.6235. Notice: each final number equals *neither* query on its own. That is exactly why you report the mean over a query set, never a single lucky (or unlucky) query.

The same eight-result ranking, but now graded, not binary. Each hit carries a relevance grade: two strong space docs at ranks 2 and 4 score grade 3, two marginal ones at ranks 6 and 8 score grade 1, the four off-topic results score 0. nDCG still discounts each gain by 1/log₂(rank+1).

Binary gain first: flatten every grade to 1-if-relevant. Only the discounts matter, so DCG = 0.6309 + 0.4307 + 0.3562 + 0.3155 = 1.7333; the ideal puts all four hits up top → IDCG = 2.5616. nDCG = 1.7333 / 2.5616 = 0.6766. Binary can't tell a grade-3 from a grade-1.

Linear graded gain uses the grade itself: gain = g. The two grade-3 hits now weigh three times a grade-1. DCG = 3·0.6309 + 3·0.4307 + 1·0.3562 + 1·0.3155 = 3.8565; the ideal front-loads grades 3,3,1,1 → IDCG = 5.8235. nDCG = 0.6622 — slightly below binary's 0.6766, because the strong hits sit at rank 2 and 4, not 1 and 2.

Exponential gain, 2^g − 1, punishes burying a great result even harder: a grade-3 jumps to 7 while a grade-1 stays 1. DCG = 7·0.6309 + 7·0.4307 + 1·0.3562 + 1·0.3155 = 8.1029; IDCG = 12.3472. nDCG = 8.1029 / 12.3472 = 0.6563 — the lowest of the three. Same ranking, three scores (0.6766 / 0.6622 / 0.6563). They disagree because they reward different things — so always state which gain you used.

Where precision and recall come from

Precision and recall are not retrieval inventions. They are the confusion matrix read through a retrieval lens. Cross two yes/no questions — did I return this document, and is it actually relevant — and four boxes appear: returned-and-relevant (TP), returned-and-junk (FP), relevant-but-missed (FN), and correctly-left-alone (TN). Precision reads one row: of everything I returned, what share was right. Recall reads one column: of everything that was right, what share did I return. Same four counts, two different denominators, two different questions about the same system.

Now notice the box neither of them touches. TN — the millions of documents you correctly ignored — appears in neither formula, and that omission is the entire reason retrieval does not use accuracy. Accuracy counts TN, and in a real collection TN dwarfs everything else, so a system that returns nothing at all scores almost perfectly and a system that returns one brilliant document scores about the same. Precision and recall were built for exactly this shape of problem: a tiny positive class adrift in an ocean of negatives, where being right about the ocean is worth nothing.

One thing has to be smuggled in before any of this applies to a ranking, though. The confusion matrix needs a set — "what I returned" — and a ranking does not have one; it has an order. So we invent the set by declaring a cutoff: the top \(k\) counts as returned, everything below counts as not returned. That single move is what turns two static numbers into the pair of curves you will spend the rest of the act reading, and it smuggles an assumption in with it: that the user sees exactly \(k\) results and treats all \(k\) as equally good. Every metric ahead is an argument about how to soften that lie.

Somebody binarised your labels

The counting family — precision, recall, \(F_1\), MRR, MAP — accepts exactly one kind of label: relevant, or not. A serious collection ships a graded scale. Put those two facts side by side and something has to give, and what gives is silent: before any of those metrics can run on graded judgments, a binarisation threshold has to be picked — a line drawn across the grades, everything above it counted as a hit and everything below as a miss. Nobody announces this. It usually lives in one line of an evaluation script, written once and never mentioned again.

It is a decision with teeth, because the marginal grade is where most of the mass sits. Draw the line low and barely useful counts as a win: recall rises for everybody at once, and the system that piles up half-relevant results looks generous. Draw it high and only the excellent grades count: precision falls everywhere, and the system that hunts for one perfect answer suddenly wins the comparison it was losing. Not a single ranking changed between those two runs. You moved the same ruler again, one floor below the guideline.

Two habits, then. State the threshold in the same breath as the metric — MAP, counting anything above marginal as relevant is a measurement; MAP on its own is a rumour, and two teams quoting it are not comparing systems. And when you have graded labels, prefer a metric that can eat them: nDCG needs no line drawn at all, which is one plain reason to reach for it over MAP on a graded collection. The rule running through this whole act holds here too — every number in this chapter stands on a written choice somebody made before the arithmetic began.

Precision vs recall, and one number for both

Precision and recall aren't just two metrics; they're two ends of a tug-of-war, and watching them fight is half the job. Show fewer results and the ones you show are likelier to be good — precision up, recall down. Show more and you eventually catch everything relevant — recall up, but you've padded the list with junk, so precision falls. Every choice of cutoff \(k\) is one truce in that war, one operating point. Plot all of them — recall on the x-axis, precision on the y-axis — and you get the precision–recall curve, the single picture that shows the whole trade at once. On our running ranking the four operating points are \(@1\,(0,0)\), \(@3\,(0.25,0.333)\), \(@5\,(0.5,0.4)\), \(@8\,(1.0,0.5)\). (Textbook PR curves usually slope down as recall grows; ours happens to rise because this ranking clusters its hits at the even ranks — a reminder that a curve is a property of one ranking, not a law.)

A curve is honest but hard to put in a spreadsheet, so we want one number that summarises it without letting a system cheat. The naive move — average precision and recall — is a trap: a system that returns everything scores recall 1.0, and \((1.0 + \text{tiny})/2\) still looks respectable. The fix is the harmonic mean, which collapses toward the smaller of the two and refuses to be bought off by one inflated half. That's the F₁ score: $$F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}$$ At our \(k=5\) operating point (\(P=0.4,\ R=0.5\)) it gives \(F_1=\frac{2\cdot0.4\cdot0.5}{0.9}=\mathbf{0.4444}\); at \(k=8\) (\(P=0.5,\ R=1.0\)) it gives \(\frac{2\cdot0.5\cdot1.0}{1.5}=\mathbf{0.6667}\). When missing a relevant document hurts more than showing a bad one, tilt the balance with \(F_\beta\) (\(\beta \gt 1\) favours recall); and to average PR curves across queries, TREC historically used 11-point interpolated precision. F₁ is the workhorse summary whenever there's no graded scale and no ranking discount to worry about.

One more cutoff worth a name, because a fixed \(k\) is quietly unfair across queries: P@10 caps a three-relevant query at 0.3 forever while judging a fifty-relevant query through a tiny window. R-precision removes the unfairness by setting the cutoff to \(R\), the number of relevant documents for that query — precision at \(k=R\). A neat identity falls out: at \(k=R\) the denominators of precision and recall are the same, so R-precision = recall@R, a single self-scaling number. Our query has \(R=4\) relevant documents, so R-precision is \(\mathrm{P}@4\); the top four hold relevant docs at ranks 2 and 4, giving \(2/4=\mathbf{0.5}\). It correlates strongly with MAP in practice and adapts the window to each query for free.

The denominator you cannot see

Precision is a number you can verify yourself. Everything it needs — the documents you returned and their labels — is lying in front of you. Recall is not. Its denominator is every relevant document in the collection, and for a real collection nobody has ever seen that set. What you actually divide by is the count of relevant documents that the pool happened to surface and that somebody happened to judge. Recall is therefore not measured; it is estimated against a sample, and that sample was assembled by the systems that existed when the collection was built.

Which makes recall the metric pooling damages most, and it damages it in one direction rather than at random. A genuinely new system that surfaces good documents no old pool ever reached gets those finds scored as zeros, so it loses recall it actually earned — while the denominator, assembled from the old systems' habits, is too small, which flatters every one of them. That is not noise you can average away over enough queries. It is a bias pointing squarely at the incumbent, and it is the unjudged-document problem from Part 2 arriving with a precise address in the arithmetic.

So the craft is to be explicit about what your recall is recall of. Recall at a generous cutoff is the honest metric for a first-stage retriever, whose whole job is to lose nothing before the reranker gets to look — and it should be read aloud as "of the known relevant set". Precision at a small cutoff is the metric for whatever ranks last, because that is the part a user actually sees. And any absolute recall figure quoted without its collection and its pool is not a measurement of a system at all; it is a measurement of a collection, wearing a system's name.

Two ways to score what nobody judged

Unjudged counts as irrelevant is a convention, not a law, and there are two standard ways to refuse it. The first is to condense the list: walk down the ranking, delete every document that carries no label at all, close the gaps, and score the remainder as though it had been the ranking. The second is bpref, which drops positions entirely and counts something blunter — for each judged relevant document, how many judged non-relevant documents were ranked above it. Different arithmetic, one shared principle: score only on evidence a human actually produced.

What that buys is precisely the bias of the previous beat. A retriever that surfaces good documents the pool never reached is no longer punished for them — its discoveries are skipped rather than counted as zeros — so the arithmetic stops mistaking novelty for error. And bpref's bluntness about rank is deliberate, not a compromise: a measure that leans hard on exact positions is leaning hard on positions it has no labels for, so it degrades fastest exactly when the judgments are sparsest.

Now be honest about what they do not buy. Deleting the unjudged documents also deletes their cost: a system that stuffs its top with unlabelled junk has those slots quietly removed and comes out looking tidier than any user would find it. And neither measure invents a judgment — a relevant document nobody ever looked at is still absent from the denominator of recall, so the ceiling is as unknown as before. Read the family for what it is: robust measures let you reuse an old collection on a new kind of system with less injustice. They are never a substitute for judging the documents your system actually returns.

Two metrics with a user baked in

nDCG buys its discount off a shelf: \(1/\log_2(\text{rank}+1)\), a fixed curve that assumes nothing in particular about why attention fades down the list. Two well-known metrics do something more honest — they bake an explicit user model into the discount, so the metric matches a behaviour you can name. They're worth knowing by sight even if you never memorise the algebra.

The first is ERR (expected reciprocal rank), from Chapelle et al. (2009). Its user reads top-down and stops the instant a result satisfies them — a cascade. So one great hit at rank 1 makes everything beneath it nearly worthless, because the modelled user never gets there. That makes ERR essentially a graded MRR: it pays out for satisfying the user early, and it's the metric to reach for in single-answer or known-item search where people leave the moment they're happy. The second is RBP (rank-biased precision), from Moffat & Zobel (2008). Its user steps to the next rank with a fixed probability \(p\) and stops otherwise, so attention decays geometrically: \(\mathrm{RBP}=(1-p)\sum_r g_r\,p^{\,r-1}\). The persistence \(p\) is a knob — push it toward 1 for a patient user who scrolls deep, toward 0 for an impatient one who barely looks past the top — where nDCG's log discount is fixed for everyone. The lesson isn't the formulas; it's that a metric is a theory of your user, and when you can name how your users actually behave — cascade, persistent, known-item — you can pick a metric whose discount tells that story instead of a generic one.

The gain function is a claim

nDCG has two halves, and the last beat opened only one of them. The discount says how attention fades with position; the gain function says how much a grade is worth in the first place. The obvious choice is to let the grade be its own reward — a linear gain, where the reward is the label. The industry default is not that. Web search uses the exponential form \(g = 2^{\mathrm{rel}} - 1\), and the difference between the two is not cosmetic bookkeeping.

Read what each of them asserts about the world. Linear gain says the grades are an evenly spaced ruler: the step from barely-useful to good is worth exactly as much as the step from good to perfect. Exponential gain says the opposite — the top grade is worth many of the marginal ones, so one excellent result outweighs a whole handful of merely acceptable ones stacked underneath it. Web search chose the exponential form on purpose, because that is what its users are like: one right answer beats five adequate ones, and a metric that traded them evenly would cheerfully approve a ranking that never finds the right answer at all.

So the choice is a statement about your product, and it has two consequences worth saying out loud. First, it changes which system wins: exponential gain rewards finding the gem, linear gain rewards broad coverage, and two systems can trade places when you switch between them. Second, it makes the number incomparable across dashboards and papers — the same ranking, the same labels and the same discount produce different nDCG under different gains. Report which one you used, in the same breath as the cutoff. An unlabelled nDCG is a number without units.

How to read an nDCG

nDCG is a ratio, and a ratio is only as meaningful as its denominator. The denominator here is the ideal DCG: the score a perfect ranker would earn on this query with these labels — every judged document sorted by grade, best first, so each one pays the smallest discount it possibly can. That is the ceiling, and it is a different ceiling for every query. A query with two marginal documents has a low ceiling; a query with several excellent ones has a high one. Dividing by it is exactly what makes two queries' scores addable at all.

So the sentence to hold in your head is "the share of the achievable gain I collected" — not "the share of relevant documents I found", and certainly not "the probability that the ranking is right". A middling score means the relevant documents are in the list but sitting lower than they had to, each one paying a discount the ideal ladder avoided; the gap between your ladder and the ideal one is gain lost to placement, and nothing else. Note also that the discount curve flattens as you go down, so a score can sit close to the top while the bottom of the list is still shuffled: moving a good document from the last slot to the second-to-last barely registers.

Two readings people get wrong constantly. The top of the scale does not mean "nothing was missed" — it means that, among the documents someone judged, you ordered them as well as they can be ordered; anything nobody labelled is a separate conversation. And because the ceiling is computed per query, an average over a query set is an average of ratios with different denominators. That is perfectly legitimate, and it is also a decision: it makes an easy query and a brutal one count exactly the same, which is what you usually want and never what the metric tells you it did.

nDCG's fine print

First, the cutoff. nDCG is almost always reported at a cutoff, and the cutoff has to be applied to both halves of the ratio. Truncate your ranking at \(k\) but compute the ideal over the whole judged set, and the denominator becomes a ceiling nobody could have reached at that depth, so every score is quietly deflated. Truncate the ideal ladder too — sort the judged documents by grade and keep as many as the cutoff allows. Getting this backwards is the single most common nDCG bug, and it announces itself either as suspiciously low numbers or, when the mistake runs the other way, as a score above the top of the scale, which should be impossible.

Second, the ties. Two documents with the same score have no defined order, so the tie-break falls to whatever the sort happened to be stable about — insertion order, document id, whatever the index returned first. That is a number-changing decision made by an implementation detail: swap the two documents and the discounts they pay swap with them. If ties are common in your system, randomise the order and average over several draws, or you are reporting your sort's habits. And the neighbouring trap: a query with very few relevant documents has a tiny ceiling, so a one-position move swings its score wildly, and averaging over a query set lets those jumpy queries dominate the spread.

Third, the unjudged. A document nobody labelled is scored as worth nothing, and it still occupies its slot — so it absorbs a discount in your DCG while contributing nothing to the ideal, deflating the ratio for the very system that found it. That is the pooling bias from Part 2, arriving in the arithmetic. All three items point the same way: an nDCG is comparable only to another nDCG computed under an identical protocol — same cutoff, same gain function, same label set. Two systems evaluated under different fine print have not been compared at all, however tidy the table looks.

Pick the metric that matches the user

You just watched five metrics fall out of one ranking, and the natural reaction is "so which one do I use?" The honest answer is: it depends entirely on what your user is trying to do, and choosing the wrong metric is its own quiet way of lying. Each metric encodes an assumption about behaviour, so the trick is to match the metric to the task.

Looking up one specific thing — a known-item search, a question with a single right answer? Then only the rank of the first good result matters, and that's MRR: it rewards getting the answer to the very top and ignores everything below. Browsing a set, like shopping or research, where you'll skim several results? Then precision@k and recall@k speak your language — how much of page one is good, and how much of the good stuff you found. Care about ordering across many relevant documents with different degrees of usefulness, the way real web search works? Then you want graded nDCG, which alone among these knows that a great result at rank 1 beats a great result at rank 8. Same ranking, five numbers, and they can disagree about which system wins — so the discipline is to decide what your users actually do before you read the scoreboard, not after.

Two systems, one number each

Back to the very first problem: two systems, both look fine, which is better. Now we have a metric, so let's do the obvious thing — run both over a set of queries, average their nDCG, and compare. System B scores about 0.64, System A about 0.60. B wins by roughly four hundredths of a point. Ship B, right?

Not so fast, and this is the beat that separates careful people from confident ones. That single average hides a riot of per-query variation. Look underneath and B beats A on most queries — but on several queries A actually wins, sometimes by more than the average gap. The mean is a smooth lie told over a bumpy reality. Four hundredths could be a real, durable improvement, or it could be the luck of which fifteen queries happened to land in your test set. If you'd drawn a different fifteen, would B still win? That is exactly the question the next figure forces us to answer honestly — and "the average is higher" is not an answer.

A sample of queries, not a sample of users

Before testing whether B's lead is real, be precise about what real would even mean here. The average you computed runs over a query set, and a query set is a sample. So the claim on trial is exactly this: draw another query set from the same place, and would B still be ahead? That fixes what an offline verdict speaks about — queries like these, drawn the way these were drawn — and nothing else. It is not yet a claim about users, sessions or traffic; those come later, and they need a different experiment.

Which makes how the set was drawn part of the instrument, alongside the guideline and the pool. Sample the head of a traffic log and your average describes the queries people ask most, where every system is already decent and the differences are small and hard-won. Sample the tail and you are measuring the queries systems actually fail on, where the identical change looks enormous. Hand-pick a set of interesting-looking topics and you have measured your own taste, faithfully and reproducibly. None of those is wrong. They are different questions, and no significance test can tell you which one you asked.

One more thing hides inside the averaging. Every query counts the same, however often anybody types it — so a query nobody has issued in a month and a query that carries a large slice of your traffic contribute equally to the number you are about to ship on. If you want the traffic-weighted statement, you have to weight the average deliberately and then say that you did. The discipline is a sentence you should be able to write before the test runs: these queries, drawn this way, weighted like this. Only then does "the difference is real" have a subject.

Is the difference real?

A p-value is the probability, under a specified null model and its assumptions, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that the null is true or that the result was caused by luck. For paired retrieval evaluation, first define the population of queries and the per-query difference. Then choose a test whose assumptions fit that design.

The paired t-test tests a zero population mean; exact small-sample calibration assumes independent normal differences. Here \(t\approx2.27\), \(p\approx0.039\). Wilcoxon signed-rank uses signed ranks and assumes symmetric differences for its usual calibration; here \(p\approx0.048\). Exact sign-flip permutation requires exchangeability of A/B within independent pairs, or an appropriate symmetric-difference null. Conditional on magnitudes, signs then act like fair coins. Enumerating flips gives \(p\approx0.040\). Equality of means alone does not justify this procedure. Smucker, Allan & Carterette (2007) compare these tests empirically for IR.

All three p-values are below \(0.05\) in this example under their respective assumptions. They reuse the same data, so agreement is not three independent confirmations. Report an effect size and confidence interval too. The interval barely clears zero; that warrants more representative queries and attention to practical value, not certainty that the effect is real. Sample size improves power only within a suitable sampling design.

Why the test is paired

All three of those tests carried the same word in their description — paired — and it is doing more work than it looks. Queries differ enormously in difficulty. Some are easy for every system on earth, some are brutal for every system on earth, and that spread is far larger than the gap between two decent rankers. If you compared the two systems' pools of scores as if they were two independent samples, all of that query-difficulty spread would land in the noise term and drown a real difference under it.

Pairing deletes it by construction. Both systems answer the same query, so when you subtract their per-query scores the difficulty of that query cancels out of the difference — whatever made it hard hit both sides alike. What survives the subtraction is precisely the quantity you wanted: the effect of the system, one query at a time. The test is then no longer about two clouds of scores at all; it is about a single list of differences, and the only question left is whether that list sits away from zero.

Two consequences worth acting on. First, this is why an evaluation is built around a fixed query set that every system answers: let the systems be scored on different queries and you have thrown away the design that made the comparison sensitive in the first place. Second, it is why the per-query differences deserve to be read directly and not only fed to a test. A mean difference near zero can hide a system that wins hugely on one kind of query and loses on another, and that pattern is a product decision rather than a statistical one. The test tells you whether the average moved; only the list tells you what actually happened.

The spread is not the error

The picture that ought to frighten you is the one where the per-query differences scatter wider than their own average. Read naively, that says the effect is drowned. It isn't, and untangling why gives you the most useful pair of words in this chapter. The scatter of individual differences is the standard deviation — a fact about your queries, and it does not shrink when you test more of them. The uncertainty of their average is the standard error, \(\mathrm{SE}=\mathrm{sd}/\sqrt{n}\) — a fact about your experiment, and it shrinks with every query you add.

So a system can be reliably better on average while losing badly on plenty of individual queries. Those two statements live on different floors, and every test in this chapter is about the upper one only. That also explains the shape of an evaluation budget: the square root in the denominator is stingy, so the error falls far more slowly than the query count rises, and each additional decimal place of confidence costs disproportionately more than the last. The spread you cannot change without changing the collection. The error you buy down with sample size, and with nothing else.

Two things to actually do. Report the spread beside the mean, because it answers a question the mean cannot: is this a broad small lift, or a violent trade that wins on one kind of query and loses on another? Those are different launches at the identical average, and the choice between them is a product decision that no p-value is entitled to make. And never read a wide scatter as a verdict — read it as a price list. It tells you how many queries this comparison is going to cost, which is a question you can answer before running anything at all.

Two rankers, 15 queries each. Subtract per query: B − A. Eleven bars point up (B wins), four point down (A wins). The average gap is +0.0397 nDCG — small, and four queries actively disagree. Real, or the luck of which 15 queries you drew?

Make the null concrete: if B ≈ A, the sign on each query's difference is a coin flip. So flip every sign every possible way — all 2^15 = 32768 arrangements — recompute the mean each time, and count how often pure luck fakes a gap ≥ 0.0397. It happens 0.03979 of the time: p ≈ 0.040. No formula, just counting.

The paired t-test is the fast approximation: divide the mean gap by its standard error. t = 0.0397 / (0.0676 / √15) = 2.275 on df = 14, which the t-table turns into p ≈ 0.039. Wilcoxon, which uses only the ranks of the differences, lands at p ≈ 0.048 — just under the line.

Three roads, one destination: permutation p ≈ 0.040, t-test p ≈ 0.039, Wilcoxon p ≈ 0.048 — all below 0.05. The 95% CI for the true gain is [0.0023, 0.0772]: it excludes zero, so the win is real. But it grazes zero — the effect is small and fragile. The honest fix is more queries.

How many queries do you need?

Significance guards one door: it stops you announcing a win that is not there. Power guards the opposite one — the chance of noticing a win that is. A test with too few queries fails silently. It reports no significant difference for a change that genuinely helps, and that report is word-for-word identical to the report for a change that does nothing at all. Both errors are expensive; only one of them announces itself, which is why the quiet one keeps getting made.

Four knobs set the number of queries you need, and they trade against each other. The minimum detectable difference — the smallest gain you would actually act on. The per-query spread of your metric on your collection. The false-positive rate you are willing to live with. And power itself: how often you want to catch a real effect when there is one. The shape of the relationship is what to remember: the required query count grows with the spread, and with the square of the inverse of the difference you insist on detecting. Halve the difference you care about and you need four times the queries. That single fact explains why chasing ever-smaller improvements gets expensive so fast, and why a modest query set can settle a large gap while being helpless against a small one.

So the discipline is to size the test before running it, which means deciding up front what improvement would actually be worth shipping. A non-significant result on a small query set is not evidence that the change did nothing; it is evidence that your instrument could not see at that scale, and an honest write-up says so in those words. And when the interval comes back wide and hugging zero, the answer is never a cleverer test. It is more queries, or a bigger effect to go looking for.

The interval speaks in the metric's own units

A \(p\)-value is unitless. It answers one yes-or-no question — could luck alone have done this — and then it is spent. A confidence interval answers the question you actually had: how much better, and how sure are we. It is assembled from the same parts as the test — the mean difference, the standard error, and a table value setting how much slack a sample this size deserves — but it arrives in points of the metric, which is a language a shipping decision can be made in.

So read its two ends separately, because they say different things. The near end is the pessimistic case the data still permits; when it sits a hair above nothing, significant and worth shipping have come apart, and the honest one-line summary is "probably a real improvement, possibly a negligible one." The far end is the optimistic case, and it is the one people quietly plan around — a wide interval means you have not yet earned the right to plan around anything. The width itself is the previous beat read from the other side: it is the standard error wearing a confidence level.

And notice which failures the interval catches that the star never will. A very large sample can make a difference far too small to matter statistically certain: the interval sits clear of nothing and also lies entirely inside the range you would call irrelevant, which is a launch to refuse rather than celebrate. A very small sample can produce an interval so wide it straddles a serious win and a serious loss at once, and "not significant" is a terrible one-word summary of that state. So report the interval first and the verdict second. The interval contains the verdict; the verdict does not contain the interval.

The multiple-comparisons trap

There's a hole in the significance story big enough to drive a quarter through, and it's the single most common statistical sin in applied ranking. Read the \(p\)-value honestly: \(p \lt 0.05\) means that if the systems were truly equal, pure luck would fake a gap this big only about one time in twenty. Fine — for one test. But nobody runs one test. You try twenty tweaks, or score the same change on twenty metrics, or slice it across twenty query segments, and you keep the comparison that lit up green. At \(\alpha=0.05\), one in twenty random tests is "significant" by chance alone — so run twenty and you should expect a false positive even when nothing is genuinely better. Cherry-picking the winning \(p\)-value out of a pile is how a team ships pure noise wearing a result's clothes, completely convinced.

The fix is to pay for every look you take. Bonferroni is the blunt version: if you run \(m\) tests, demand \(p \lt \alpha/m\) instead of \(\alpha\), so twenty tests each face a \(0.0025\) bar — safe but conservative, and it can bury a real small effect. Benjamini–Hochberg is gentler: rather than guard against any false positive, it controls the false-discovery rate — the expected fraction of your "wins" that are bogus — which keeps more power when you're screening many candidates. But the cleanest defence isn't a correction at all: pre-register the one hypothesis you actually care about before you look, or confirm the apparent winner on a fresh query set it has never touched — better yet, in an online A/B test. The same trap has an online twin: peeking at a running experiment and stopping the moment it looks good is multiple comparisons in time, and it inflates false positives exactly the same way. The discipline is identical everywhere: decide what you're testing first, then look once.

The real world votes with clicks

Everything so far happened in a lab on frozen labels — that's offline evaluation, and it's indispensable, but it's a proxy. The labels came from annotators guessing what users want, not from users. The corpus is a snapshot, not the live, drifting, seasonal stream of real queries. Offline tells you a change might help. Only the online world, real users in real time, tells you it did.

The workhorse of online evaluation is the A/B test: split live traffic, send half to the old system and half to the new, and watch what people actually do — clicks, conversions, how long they stay. In our run the new system lifts click-through from 12% to 13.2%, a 10% relative gain, and with twenty thousand users behind it that clears significance comfortably (\(p \approx 0.01\)). But the same statistical discipline from a moment ago applies with teeth: you need enough users for power, you have to fight novelty effects (anything new gets clicked just for being new) and seasonality, and you must not peek — checking the test every hour and stopping the moment it looks good is a fantastic way to declare victory on pure noise. The cardinal sin of online eval is calling a winner early. (Kohavi, Tang & Xu (2020) is the practitioner's bible for getting all of this right.)

Split live traffic in two: 10,000 users see the old ranker (A), 10,000 see the new one (B). Then just watch what they click.

B wins on the surface: click-through rises 12% → 13.2%. That's +1.2 points absolute — a +10% relative lift. Ship B?

Not yet — could it be luck? If A and B were truly equal, how surprising is this gap? The two-proportion z-test pools the rate (p̄ = 0.126) and measures the gap in standard errors: z = 0.012 / 0.00469 ≈ 2.557.

z ≈ 2.557 maps to p ≈ 0.011 — far below 0.05. Pure luck would fake a gap this big only ~1% of the time. With 20,000 users behind it, the lift is real. Ship B.

Guardrails, and who gets randomised

An A/B test answers one question extremely well — did behaviour change — and almost everyone reads it as answering a different one: did the product get better. The gap between those two is where guardrail metrics live. You choose a target metric and watch it move; a guardrail is everything you would be horrified to lose while it moves. Clicks up and latency up. Clicks up and revenue down. Clicks up because the headlines got louder while the results got worse — which is why a click metric should never travel without a satisfaction signal beside it: dwell time, conversion, coming back tomorrow. Something that costs the user effort to fake.

Then the design detail nobody notices until it burns them: what exactly is being randomised? Split by session and one enthusiastic user contributes a pile of correlated observations, so the sample looks larger than it is, the spread comes out too small, and the verdict ends up confidently wrong — in the direction you were hoping for, which is the worst possible direction. Randomise by user, keep each user on one side for the whole experiment, and account for that clustering when you build the interval. The same discipline covers the sanity check worth running before anything else: if the traffic did not actually split the way you asked it to, nothing downstream is interpretable, however pretty the chart.

And the three procedural traps, which are one trap in three costumes. Peeking — checking the result repeatedly and stopping the moment it looks good — is multiple comparisons stretched over time; fix the sample size in advance, or use a test designed for continuous monitoring. Novelty — people click anything new for a few days — means an early reading measures curiosity rather than quality. Seasonality — weekday traffic is not weekend traffic — means you run whole cycles, not a convenient afternoon. Every one of them is a way of letting the data choose when to stop looking, and the fix in every case is to decide before you look.

Clicks lie too

Now the nasty part, and it should feel familiar — because it's a cousin of the position-bias monster from the very first chapter. Clicks are not a clean signal of relevance. People click the top result far more than the bottom one regardless of how good it is, simply because they look there first; in our model the chance of even examining a result decays geometrically from 100% at rank 1 down to about 57% by rank 10 (the very first chapter used a power-law fit of the same bias — different curve shape, same culprit). So a result at the top gets clicks it didn't earn, and a great result buried at rank 9 gets ignored it didn't deserve. If you just count clicks, you'll "learn" that being on top is what makes a result good — which is circular and false. (Craswell et al. (2008) compared click models for exactly this — the examination (position-based) model, whose decay we use here, against the cascade model.)

Two ideas rescue online eval from its own clicks. The first is interleaving: instead of showing one user system A and another user system B, blend both systems' results into a single list and see which system's contributions get clicked more. Because every user sees both, position bias hits both equally and cancels out — which is why interleaving can detect a winner with a fraction of the traffic an A/B test needs (Chapelle et al. (2012) validated that sensitivity gain at large scale). In our run it cleanly prefers system B. The second idea is inverse propensity scoring (Joachims, Swaminathan & Schnabel (2017)): if you know a result at rank 9 only had, say, a 60% chance of being examined, you up-weight its clicks to correct for the seat it was given. That's the seed of counterfactual, unbiased evaluation — estimating how a ranking would have done if its results had been seen fairly.

And one humbling truth ties the offline and online halves of this chapter together: they don't always agree. A change can lift nDCG in the lab and move nothing online — or even hurt. When that happens, your offline metric isn't predicting the thing you actually care about, and the grown-up response isn't to trust the lab harder; it's to ask why your proxy lied and fix the proxy. The whole point of a scoreboard is to stop fooling yourself, and the surest way to keep fooling yourself is to fall in love with one number.

How interleaving actually works

Interleaving got one sentence a moment ago, and the mechanism deserves the beat, because the way it cancels position bias is genuinely clever. The usual scheme is team-draft interleaving, named after picking teams in a schoolyard. Both rankers stand ready with their own ordered lists. Each round a coin decides who picks first; the winner takes its highest-ranked document that is not already placed, then the other one does the same. Every slot in the list the user finally sees carries an invisible tag naming the ranker that contributed it.

Now count. A click on a slot credits the ranker whose tag it carries, and the ranker holding the larger share of the credit wins that query. The bias cancels because of where the comparison happens: both systems are being judged inside one list, seen by one user, in one session. Whatever makes the top of a list attract clicks applies to both teams' slots alike, and the coin toss stops either side from systematically owning the good positions. Compare that with an A/B test, where the two systems are shown to different people at different moments, so every difference between those two crowds lands in the noise and has to be paid for with traffic.

That is where the sensitivity comes from: the same verdict on a fraction of the traffic, because a within-list comparison throws away the variance a between-group comparison has to absorb. Two cautions travel with it. Credit share is not a win rate — a system can take the larger share of the clicks while winning fewer queries, and those two summaries answer different questions, so report the one you actually mean. And interleaving is built to compare two rankings: it tells you which list users preferred, not how far any absolute metric moved. It settles a duel and leaves the dashboard to the A/B test.

Dividing by a small number

Write the target policy explicitly: \(\hat V(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_i \frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}r_i\). Here \(x_i\) is context, \(a_i\) the logged action, \(\mu\) the logging policy and \(\pi\) the target. With correct propensities, support and an unchanged conditional reward mechanism, \(\mu\) cancels in expectation. Changing \(\pi\) changes the estimate. Dividing by \(\mu\) alone cannot compare policies. Position-based click debiasing instead weights the document's contribution under the evaluated ranker by examination propensity.

The first catch is variance. A target action rarely chosen by the logger can receive an enormous probability ratio. One observation then swings the estimate. Clipping weights or dividing the weighted reward sum by the sum of weights (SNIPS) can improve stability, but generally introduces finite-sample bias. Unbiasedness alone does not make an estimate useful.

The second is support: \(\mu(a\mid x)>0\) wherever \(\pi(a\mid x)>0\). If the target chooses actions never chosen by the logger, more logs from that same policy cannot reveal their rewards. Without extra modelling assumptions target value is unidentified. Collect exploration data or run an experiment. Display probability and examination probability remain different quantities.

A thought experiment: make all 10 results EQUALLY relevant, then count clicks. If clicks meant relevance, the bars would be flat.

They're not. Rank 1 alone takes ~32.3% of clicks — purely because it sits on top. That's position bias.

The top 3 soak up ~60.6%. People click what they see first, not what's best.

Overlay the honest signal: if all 10 are equally relevant, clicks SHOULD be flat at 10% each. The gap between this flat truth and the tall bars IS the position bias.

So if you train on raw clicks — “more clicks = better” — you reward position, not relevance. Goodhart's law: the moment CTR becomes the target, it stops being a good measure.

The loop that feeds itself

The curve you just watched is worth one equation, because the equation says something the curve cannot. A click needs two things to happen at once: the user has to look at that rank, and the document has to deserve the click. Write that down and it factors — this is the examination hypothesis — into \( P(c{=}1\mid d,k)=P(e{=}1\mid k)\cdot P(r{=}1\mid d) \), where \(c\) is a click, \(e\) is that rank \(k\) was examined at all, and \(r\) is the relevance of document \(d\). The left factor falls down the page and knows nothing about the document. The right factor is what you actually wanted to measure.

Now take the estimate every logs-driven pipeline computes: clicks over impressions. It does not estimate relevance. It estimates relevance multiplied by the examination probability of whatever rank the old ranker chose, \( \widehat{r}(d)\propto P(e{=}1\mid k)\,P(r{=}1\mid d) \). Read that as machinery rather than as a warning: the ranker's own past decision sits as a factor inside its own next training label. Whatever it put on top gets a higher estimate, and a higher estimate puts it on top again, and the next batch of logs is more skewed than the last. Nothing adversarial is needed — no spammer, no clickbait. The system games itself.

Which is why the two defences you already met are not two ideas but one fix stated twice. Divide the estimate by the propensity, \( \widehat{r}\,/\,P(e{=}1\mid k) \), and the rank factor cancels algebraically; ask a human to judge the document apart from any ranking, and the factor was never in the label to begin with. It also explains the fine print on the first cure: the division is only as good as the propensity you divide by, because that number is the thing being removed. And it gives Goodhart's law an exact shape here — the quantity on your scoreboard carries a factor the ranker itself controls, so optimising the scoreboard is partly just optimising your own past.

The evidence, by the numbers

We've told the significance story twice now in words; here it is once in pure numbers, so you can check every claim against a figure. Over fifteen queries, system B's mean nDCG@10 beats A's by a flat \(\mathbf{0.0397}\) — the whole edge, four hundredths of a point. Under their stated assumptions, the tests assess how extreme the gap is under their null models, and all three land in the same neighbourhood:

The paired t-test gives \(t \approx 2.27\) on 14 d.f. \(\rightarrow p \approx 0.039\). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test (ranks only, magnitudes discarded) gives \(p \approx 0.048\). The permutation test — flip each sign all \(2^{15}=32768\) ways and count the gaps \(\geq 0.0397\) — gives \(p \approx 0.040\).

All three p-values are below 0.05, but they reuse the same data. The 95% confidence interval, \([0.0023,\ 0.0772]\), barely excludes zero under its assumptions and permits quite different practical effect sizes. This is evidence against the null, not a posterior probability of a real improvement. Report the effect and uncertainty, evaluate practical value, and collect more representative independent queries when precision is insufficient.

Online, the evidence comes from behaviour, not labels. The A/B test runs 10,000 users per arm; CTR rises 12% → 13.2% (a +10% relative lift), and on 20,000 users the two-proportion z-test gives \(p \approx 0.01\) — comfortably significant precisely because the sample is large. Interleaving is even cheaper: blending both systems into one list so position bias cancels, it credits system B 16 to A's 9 across five queries — a clean win on a sliver of the traffic. Offline said "probably," online said "yes" — but they can disagree, and when they do you fix the proxy, not force the verdict.

Offline is a filter, not a verdict

The two halves of this chapter meet in one empirical question: across many launches, does an offline gain predict an online gain? The answer is usually, and "usually" turns out to be exactly the right amount to build a process on. If offline predicted online perfectly, experiments would be a waste of traffic. If it predicted nothing, the entire inner loop of development — score every change against a frozen collection — would be theatre. It correlates, and that makes offline evaluation a filter: cheap enough to run on everything, accurate enough to throw out most of what deserves throwing out, and authoritative about nothing it lets through.

The interesting cases sit off the trend line, and they are not symmetric. A change that looks flat offline and helps online is a missed opportunity — annoying, recoverable, and you will probably stumble into it again. A change that looks excellent offline and hurts users is the dangerous one, because it sails through every gate you built and arrives carrying a chart in its favour. That combination almost always means the change exploited something about the labels rather than something about relevance: the pool's blind spots, the unjudged-as-irrelevant convention, the quirks of one guideline. It did not improve retrieval. It improved the measurement of retrieval.

So the process this correlation licenses is a ladder, not a courtroom. Iterate offline because it is fast, and treat a green offline result as permission to spend traffic rather than as a decision. Confirm on live users — interleaving first when you only need to know which ranking they prefer, an A/B test when you need the size of the effect on the metrics the business actually keeps. And when the two disagree, resist the reflex to believe the lab because the lab is tidier. A proxy that has stopped predicting is a proxy that needs repair, and the disagreement is the most informative signal you will get all quarter.

Goodhart the Trickster returns

And now the danger that's been circling this whole chapter, made flesh. The moment a metric becomes a target — the moment someone's bonus, or a training loop's gradient, points at the number instead of the thing the number was supposed to stand for — somebody will optimise the metric and quietly abandon the goal. That's Goodhart's Law — sharpened by Strathern (1997) into its crispest form, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Goodhart the Trickster is back, grinning, because we just handed him a scoreboard to game.

Watch him do it. Optimise for raw clicks, and since clicks flock to the top regardless of merit, you'll "learn" to reward whatever already sits on top — exactly the trap from The Lost Record. Take a ranking tuned to chase popularity instead of relevance: it shoves the busiest documents up front and pushes our four real space hits down to ranks 5, 6, 7, 8. Run the very same nDCG arithmetic on it — same four hits, same \(\mathrm{IDCG}\) of 2.5616, just worse seats — and its DCG collapses to \(\mathrm{DCG}=0.3869+0.3562+0.3333+0.3155=1.3919\), so \(\mathrm{nDCG}=\frac{1.3919}{2.5616}=\mathbf{0.5434}\): lower than honest BM25's 0.6766, even though it looks busy, confident and full of things people clicked. The gamed ranking optimised the proxy for relevance and lost the relevance. A number you optimise directly stops being a measurement and becomes a costume. This is why everything earlier in the chapter matters: significance keeps you from chasing noise, online eval keeps you from chasing lab artifacts, and a clear head keeps you from chasing a metric off a cliff.

One signal the optimiser cannot touch

In the abstract Goodhart is a maxim; inside a running search engine he has an address, and there are three. SEO spam games the lexical signals — stuff the terms in, and term-based relevance rises without a single word of the page becoming more useful to anybody. Clickbait games the click itself: the headline promises, the click happens, the target metric moves, and dwell time and trust quietly fall behind it. And the third needs no adversary at all — the feedback loop, where a ranker trained on its own biased logs learns to reinforce the positions it already chose. Two of these are attacks. The third is just the system left alone with itself.

The defences are this whole chapter, read as a list. Keep a set of human judgments the optimiser never sees — held out not only from the training data but from the tuning loop and from the dashboard people are rewarded on, because a metric anybody is paid against is a metric under pressure. Watch guardrails and satisfaction, not only the target, so that a change which rises by making the product worse has somewhere to show up. Debias the clicks before you learn from them, so the propensity the loop feeds on is divided out instead of amplified. And confirm an offline win on live users, because the two failure modes rarely fool both instruments at once.

Underneath all four sits one principle, and it is the one to carry out of the Proving Grounds: keep at least one honest signal that nothing in the system is allowed to optimise against. Every number in this chapter is a proxy, and a proxy stays useful for exactly as long as nobody is pushing on it. The moment a metric becomes a target — a bonus, a loss function, a launch criterion — assume it is being optimised, and go and find out what it stopped measuring. That is not cynicism about metrics. It is the only way to keep them worth reading.

An honest scoreboard

So now I can argue with numbers instead of opinions — and, just as importantly, I know which numbers lie and how. nDCG, MAP, MRR, recall@k: pick the one that matches what your users actually do. Test collections built from pooled, agreed-upon judgments. A significance test so you don't ship noise, an A/B test so you don't ship a lab fantasy, interleaving and propensity weighting so clicks don't con you, and a healthy fear of Goodhart so you never optimise a number into meaninglessness. That's the Proving Grounds. Keep this scoreboard close; every chapter from here on makes a claim only these tools can settle.

And the very first claim is already waiting at the gate. Our fast classical catalog from last chapter is brilliant and still synonym-blind — the Gremlin is right there behind his wall. Can a machine that understands meaning actually beat BM25 on this scoreboard, fair and square, significance and all? To even attempt it, words need coordinates. Next chapter: The Map of Meaning.
