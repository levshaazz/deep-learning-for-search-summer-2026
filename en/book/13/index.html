Hyperspace Lanes

You can't fly the whole galaxy star by star. Last lecture the Alliance united sparse and dense retrieval — but the Scouts still swept linearly over every vector in the corpus. At a thousand documents that is fine; at a billion it is death. A single query that compares itself to every vector is \(O(N)\) work, and \(N\) is now the size of the web.

This lecture opens the lanes: data structures that reach the nearest neighbours of a query in milliseconds instead of scanning everything. Approximate nearest-neighbour search (HNSW, IVF), vector compression (PQ), and the production serving that wraps them. Séréga takes the helm of the Ship's engine room — the part of the Bridge that delivers an order at galaxy scale, forever.

Why exact search dies

Exact \(k\)-nearest-neighbour search is doomed twice over. First the cost: comparing a query against \(N\) vectors of dimension \(d\) is \(O(N \cdot d)\) — linear in the corpus, every single query. Second the geometry: the Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith returns from Lecture 2. In high dimensions distances concentrate — the nearest and farthest points sit at almost the same distance, so the very notion of "the nearest" becomes fragile and the brute-force scan you paid so much for buys you less than you think.

So we make a bargain. We give up a sliver of recall we can't even feel — occasionally missing a true neighbour — in exchange for a mountain of speed. That bargain is approximate nearest-neighbour search, and the rest of this lecture is three ways to strike it.

The Wraith, quantified

The previous beat named the Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith; this one measures him. The thing that decays is not distance itself but relative contrast — the gap between the farthest and the nearest point as a fraction of the nearest. Write it as \(\dfrac{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}{d_{\min}}\). In two dimensions this ratio is large: the nearest neighbour is obviously nearer than the farthest, and "closest" is a crisp, meaningful word. As the dimension \(d\) grows, the ratio shrinks toward zero — and that is the whole curse in one line.

The classic result (Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan & Shaft, 1999) is that for a broad class of distributions \(\dfrac{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}{d_{\min}} \to 0\) as \(d \to \infty\). Intuition by hand: each coordinate adds its own little fluctuation to a squared distance, so the squared distance to any point becomes a sum of \(d\) roughly-equal random terms. By the law of large numbers that sum concentrates tightly around its mean — its spread grows like \(\sqrt{d}\) while its mean grows like \(d\), so the relative spread shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{d}\). At \(d = 2\) the contrast is wide and "nearest" is sharp; by \(d = 128\) the contrast has narrowed by an order of magnitude and the nearest and farthest neighbours sit at almost the same distance.

Read that consequence carefully, because it is subtle and it is the justification for this entire lecture. It does not mean nearest-neighbour search is pointless in high dimensions — real embedding spaces are not uniform noise; they have structure, clusters, and a low intrinsic dimension hiding inside the nominal 768. What it means is that the margin you are searching within is thin. The true neighbour beats the impostors by a hair, not a mile.

And a thin margin is exactly the regime where approximation is almost free. If the 1st and the 50th neighbour are nearly equidistant, then returning the 3rd instead of the 1st costs you almost nothing in real similarity — yet it can save you 99% of the work. The Wraith does not kill nearest-neighbour search; he kills the value of paying full price for exactness. He is, read correctly, the reason ANN works: the geometry that makes brute force fragile is the same geometry that makes the bargain a good one.

This is why the lecture spends no time apologising for the lost recall. The recall you give up lives in a region where the metric can barely tell the candidates apart in the first place. We are not throwing away good answers; we are declining to pay exact-search prices for a distinction the high-dimensional geometry has already half-erased.

The lane, not the straight line

A straight line to every star is exact and ruinous. A lane — a pre-built structure that lets you jump close to the destination and only look around there — is approximate and cheap. The three lanes are: a navigable graph you hop across (HNSW), a partition of space into cells you probe selectively (IVF), and a compression that folds each star-chart small enough to keep them all in memory (PQ).

And the one who rides them is Victor the Vector — the magnitude-and-direction arrow from Lectures 6–7 — now leaping gate to gate along the lanes.

Which ruler measures "near"?

Before we open any lane we have to settle a question the brute-force scan quietly assumed away: what does "nearest" even mean? Victor the Vector has both a direction and a length, and three different rulers each weigh those two properties differently. Choose the wrong ruler and the lane will faithfully fly you to the wrong star.

There are three rulers in play. Euclidean (L2) distance measures the straight-line gap between the two arrow-tips — it cares about displacement, and a longer vector is automatically "farther" even if it points the same way. Cosine similarity measures only the angle between the arrows — it normalises away length entirely, so two vectors that point the same direction score \(1.0\) regardless of magnitude. Inner product (dot) is the angle scaled by the magnitudes — it rewards vectors that are both well-aligned and long, which is why maximum-inner-product search (MIPS) behaves differently from both of the others.

These are not interchangeable, and the difference is not academic. A document embedding that happens to have a large norm can win an inner-product ranking on magnitude alone, while cosine would rank it by alignment and L2 by raw position. The same three candidates, ranked by the same query, can produce three different winners depending only on which ruler you picked — we prove exactly that in the next beat.

There is one identity worth memorising, because it lets you switch rulers for free. If you L2-normalise every vector to unit length first, then Euclidean distance and cosine similarity become two faces of the same coin: \(\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos(a, b)\). Minimising L2 over unit vectors is identical to maximising cosine. This is why so many vector indexes quietly normalise on ingest and then store only L2 machinery — they get cosine semantics from a Euclidean engine, with no special code.

So the practical rule: pick cosine when only meaning/direction matters (the usual choice for text embeddings), inner product when magnitude is a signal you want to keep (some recommender and late-interaction models), and L2 when you genuinely live in a metric space. Then build the lane for that ruler — because, as the worked example shows, the lane optimises whatever metric it was told to, and will not save you from choosing the wrong one.

Three rulers, one pair — by hand

Take two frozen integer vectors, \(a = (1, 2, 2, 0)\) and \(b = (2, 0, 1, 1)\), and run all three rulers over the same pair so the disagreement is impossible to wave away.

Inner product (dot): \(a \cdot b = 1{\cdot}2 + 2{\cdot}0 + 2{\cdot}1 + 0{\cdot}1 = 2 + 0 + 2 + 0 = 4\). Norms: \(\lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4 + 0} = \sqrt{9} = 3.0\); \(\lVert b \rVert = \sqrt{4 + 0 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{6} = 2.4495\). L2 distance: \(\lVert a - b \rVert = \sqrt{(1-2)^2 + (2-0)^2 + (2-1)^2 + (0-1)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{7} = 2.6458\). Cosine: \(\dfrac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert} = \dfrac{4}{3.0 \times 2.4495} = 0.5443\).

Now the identity from the previous beat, made concrete. The normalised dot — the inner product of the two vectors after dividing each by its own norm — comes out to exactly the cosine, \(0.5443\). That is not a coincidence: cosine is the inner product of the unit-normalised vectors, which is why an L2 index over normalised vectors silently delivers cosine ranking. Two rulers, one number, once you normalise.

And the flip — same vectors, different top-1. Query \(q = (2, 2, 0, 0)\) against three candidates. Candidate \(d_1 = (2,1,0,0)\): L2 \(= 1.0\), dot \(= 6\), cosine \(= 0.9487\). Candidate \(d_2 = (3,3,0,0)\): L2 \(= 1.4142\), dot \(= 12\), cosine \(= 1.0\). Candidate \(d_3 = (7,4,0,0)\): L2 \(= 5.3852\), dot \(= 22\), cosine \(= 0.9648\). The winners: L2 picks \(d_1\) (nearest in displacement), cosine picks \(d_2\) (perfectly aligned with \(q\)), inner product picks \(d_3\) (largest magnitude-times-alignment). Three rulers, three different #1 results, from one query and one candidate set.

Stare at that table until it stops being surprising, because it is the most common silent bug in a vector-search stack. A team trains an encoder with a cosine objective, then drops the vectors into an index configured for inner product, and quietly ships a system whose ranking rewards long vectors over relevant ones. The numbers above are the proof that the metric is not a detail you can defer — it is a modelling decision baked into both the encoder's training loss and the index's distance function, and the two must agree.

This is also why the lane diagrams that follow are agnostic to the ruler: HNSW will happily greedily-descend on L2, on cosine, or on inner product; IVF will cluster under whichever metric you hand its k-means; PQ will build its codebooks to minimise distortion in whatever distance you train it for. The structures accelerate a metric. Choosing which metric is upstream of all of them, and it is on you.

Where HNSW comes from

HNSW did not appear from nowhere; it is the third step of a clean lineage, and seeing the lineage makes the structure inevitable rather than magical. The chain is skip list → navigable small world (NSW) → hierarchical NSW (HNSW). Each step fixes a concrete failure of the one before it.

Start with the skip list (Pugh, 1990) — a one-dimensional idea you may already know. A sorted linked list lets you find an element only by walking it one node at a time: \(O(N)\). A skip list stacks express lanes above the base list: the bottom layer holds every element, the next holds roughly half of them, the next half of those, and so on. To search, you ride the top express lane until you would overshoot, drop down a level, ride again, and repeat. With geometrically thinning layers the search is \(O(\log N)\). The lesson HNSW steals: a hierarchy of progressively sparser layers turns a linear walk into a logarithmic one.

Next, navigable small world graphs (Malkov et al., 2014). Skip lists only sort one dimension; a vector lives in hundreds. NSW generalises the walk idea to a graph: connect each inserted point to its nearest neighbours, and — crucially — keep some of the long-range links formed early in construction. Those long links are the "small-world" shortcuts (the six-degrees-of-separation effect): they let a greedy walk cross the whole graph in a few hops instead of crawling neighbour-to-neighbour. But flat NSW has a flaw: with only one layer, the greedy walk can still take many hops in the worst case, and it can get trapped.

HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2016) is the marriage: take NSW's small-world graph and stack it into skip-list-style layers. Each node is assigned a maximum layer drawn from an exponentially-decaying distribution, so the top layers are extremely sparse (a few hub nodes spanning the whole space) and the bottom layer is dense (every node, richly connected). Search enters at the top, greedily walks the sparse hub layer to get near the target cheaply, then descends layer by layer, refining the neighbourhood at each level, until the dense base layer delivers the actual nearest neighbours.

That is the entire idea, and every knob now has a meaning you can reason about. M is how many neighbours each node keeps — the graph's connectivity; higher M means more shortcuts (better recall) but more memory and a slower build. efConstruction is how hard the builder searches for good neighbours when inserting; efSearch is how wide a frontier the query-time walk maintains. The next beats make the layer descent and the ef frontier concrete — but you now know why the structure has layers at all: it inherited them from a fifty-year-old trick for sorted lists.

Six vectors become six nodes in 2-D, wired into a navigable small-world graph (M = 2 — the link budget each node spends on insertion; a node also collects back-links, so actual degrees here run 2–3). The query ◆ sits to the right. Greedy search starts at the entry node n0 and will hop toward the query, one neighbour at a time.

From n0, compare its neighbours: n1 is 2.55 away, n3 is 4.30. The closest one wins — step to n1. Greedy always walks to the neighbour nearest the query.

From n1, the closest neighbour is n2 at 0.7071 — step there. Now look around: every neighbour of n2 is farther than 0.7071. No move improves things, so greedy stops. n2 is a local minimum.

And n2 is exactly the brute-force nearest neighbour (0.7071): recall@1 = 1.0. On six nodes this only shows the mechanism — greedy descent — not a speedup; the O(log N) win appears at scale. The ef knob keeps a wider candidate list so a single local-min trap (ef = 1 → recall 0.0) is escaped (ef = 3 → 1.0).

Greedy descent, by hand

HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) wires the vectors into a graph and walks to the query: from the current node, step to whichever neighbour is closest to the query, and repeat until no neighbour is closer. Take six nodes and a query \(q\).

Starting at the entry \(n_0\) (distance \(4.53\) to \(q\)), its neighbours are \(n_1\) at \(2.55\) and \(n_3\) at \(4.30\) — step to the closer, \(n_1\). From \(n_1\), neighbour \(n_2\) is at \(0.7071\) — step there. From \(n_2\), every neighbour (\(n_4\) at \(2.92\), \(n_5\) at \(1.58\)) is farther than \(0.7071\): a local minimum, so we stop. The path \(n_0 \to n_1 \to n_2\) took 2 hops, and \(n_2\) is exactly the brute-force nearest neighbour — recall@1 = 1.0.

On six nodes this shows only the mechanism — greedy descent — not a speed-up; here greedy still evaluates most nodes' neighbours. The real win is asymptotic: on a well-built graph the walk reaches the neighbourhood in \(O(\log N)\) hops instead of scanning \(O(N)\). Two knobs trade recall for cost: M (edges per node — connectivity) and ef (the candidate-list width during search). A single greedy walk can get stuck in a local minimum (recall \(0.0\)); widening ef keeps a frontier of candidates and escapes it (recall \(1.0\)).

Why the descent is O(log N)

The worked example walked the graph by hand and admitted it showed only the mechanism, not a speed-up — six nodes are too few. This beat earns the claim that on a real graph the walk is logarithmic, by counting the work layer by layer. It is a short, honest argument, and it is the reason HNSW is fast.

Set up the layer structure. On insertion, each node's maximum layer \(\ell\) is drawn from a geometric distribution: \(P(\ell) \propto e^{-\ell/m_L}\), where \(m_L\) is a normalisation constant (FAISS uses \(m_L = 1/\ln M\)). The practical effect is that each layer up holds roughly a factor \(1/M\) as many nodes as the layer below. So if the base layer (layer 0) holds all \(N\) nodes, layer 1 holds about \(N/M\), layer 2 about \(N/M^2\), and the top layer holds a handful.

Number of layers. The topmost non-empty layer \(L\) is where \(N/M^L \approx 1\), i.e. \(M^L \approx N\), i.e. \(L \approx \log_M N = \dfrac{\ln N}{\ln M}\). That is \(O(\log N)\) layers. Work per layer. This is the part people skip: on each layer the greedy walk does not wander far. Because the layer above already delivered the entry point into the right region, the walk on the current layer only needs a constant number of hops — bounded by the local degree \(M\) — to reach that layer's local optimum before descending. So the work per layer is \(O(M)\), a constant in \(N\). Total. \(O(\log N)\) layers \(\times\) \(O(M)\) hops per layer \(= O(M \log N) = O(\log N)\) for fixed \(M\).

Compare that to the geometric series hiding inside the descent. The nodes the search actually evaluates form the sum \(M + M + \dots + M\) over \(L \approx \log_M N\) layers — a sum that is logarithmic in \(N\), not linear. The brute-force scan evaluates all \(N\); HNSW evaluates \(O(\log N)\). On a billion vectors, \(\log_2(10^9) \approx 30\): the walk touches on the order of dozens of nodes, not a billion. That ratio — billions down to dozens — is the whole reason the latency budget three beats from now can quote 12 milliseconds for the search hop instead of half a second.

Two honest caveats, because a good depth beat states its assumptions. First, this is an expected-case argument under the small-world assumption that the long-range links genuinely let a greedy walk make progress at every layer; adversarial or badly-built graphs can degrade it. Second, the \(O(\log N)\) hides the constant \(M\) and the per-hop distance computations, so the real wall-clock cost is \(O(M \cdot d \cdot \log N)\) once you count the \(d\)-dimensional distance at each visited node — which is precisely why we still want PQ to shrink that per-distance cost. The structure gives you the \(\log N\); compression gives you the small constant.

Why the layers matter, by hand

The formal beat argued the layers give \(O(\log N)\); this beat shows what happens without them on a concrete graph — because the cleanest way to see why a structure exists is to watch the failure it prevents. Take twelve nodes, \(b_0\) through \(b_{11}\), split into two clusters far apart on the plane: a left clump around the origin and a right clump near \(x \approx 15\). The query \(q = (16, 6)\) sits inside the right clump, and the true nearest neighbour is \(b_9\) at distance \(1.4142\).

Base layer only — the trap. Start the greedy walk at \(b_0 = (0,0)\), distance \(17.088\) to \(q\), and walk only on the dense base layer. \(b_0 \to b_1\) (\(14.3178\)) \(\to b_2\) (\(13.0\)) \(\to b_4\) (\(12.0\)). At \(b_4\), every base-layer neighbour is farther than \(12.0\) — and no base edge crosses the gap to the right clump. The walk is trapped at \(b_4\) in the wrong cluster: 3 hops, and the true NN \(b_9\) is never reached. recall@1 = 0.0.

With the upper hub layer — the escape. Now enter at the layer-1 hub \(b_2\) (distance \(13.0\)). Layer 1 holds only the two hubs \(b_2\) and \(b_7\), connected by one long edge. One hub hop: \(b_2 \to b_7\), and \(b_7\) is at distance \(2.0\) — the hub layer has flung the search across the gap into the right clump in a single move. Drop to the base layer at \(b_7\) and refine: \(b_7\)'s neighbours include \(b_9\) at \(1.4142\) — step there; from \(b_9\) no neighbour is closer, stop. Total: 2 hops (one on layer 1, one on layer 0), \(4\) nodes visited, landing exactly on \(b_9\). recall@1 = 1.0.

Put the two runs side by side and the moral is stark. The same base graph, the same query, the same greedy rule — and the only difference is the entry point. Base-only enters at a node in the wrong cluster and the dense local edges keep it local, marching it straight into a local minimum it cannot leave. The hierarchy's job is precisely to pick a good entry point: the sparse upper layer's long hub edge teleports the search into the right region before the fine-grained base walk begins, so the base walk only ever has to do local refinement, never long-haul travel.

This is the single most important intuition about HNSW, and it is why "hierarchical" is the first word in the name. The base layer alone is just NSW, and NSW can get trapped when the long-range links happen not to bridge the particular gap your query needs. The upper layers are a guaranteed set of long-range shortcuts, organised coarse-to-fine, so the search always begins near the answer. Layers are not an optimisation on top of the graph; they are the thing that makes the greedy walk reliable.

The ef sweep, by hand

Layers fix where the search starts; the ef knob fixes how forgiving the search is once it is walking. A pure greedy walk keeps exactly one current node and always steps to its single closest neighbour. That is fast but brittle: one wrong turn into a local minimum and it stops, having missed the true neighbour. The fix is to keep not one candidate but a frontier of the \(ef\) best-so-far candidates, expanding all of them, so the search can back out of a dead end. \(ef\) (the search-time "size of the dynamic candidate list") is the width of that frontier.

Take a 30-node graph in the plane and deliberately start the search at the node farthest from the query — node 2 — which is the worst case, the one most likely to trap a greedy walk. The brute-force nearest neighbour is node 5, at distance \(5.831\). Now sweep \(ef\) and watch both the recall and the number of candidates the search evaluates.

\(ef = 1\) — pure greedy, one current node: the walk evaluates \(5\) candidates, marches into a local minimum, and stops short of node 5. recall@1 = 0.0. \(ef = 2\) — a frontier of two: now \(15\) candidates evaluated, the frontier holds an alternative when the greedy step would trap, the search escapes and reaches node 5. recall@1 = 1.0. \(ef = 4\): \(15\) candidates, recall \(1.0\). \(ef = 8\): \(15\) candidates, recall \(1.0\). \(ef = 16\): \(26\) candidates, recall \(1.0\).

Read the sweep as a curve and the whole engineering story is in it. Recall jumps from \(0.0\) to \(1.0\) the instant the frontier widens past one (\(ef = 1 \to 2\)) — a single extra candidate slot is enough to escape this trap. (That jump is a step, not a smooth ramp, because this is a single worst-case query: recall@1 can only be \(0\) or \(1\). The familiar smooth recall-vs-\(ef\) knee appears only once you average over a whole query set; on one query the genuinely smooth curve is the cost one.) After that, recall has already saturated at \(1.0\) and more \(ef\) buys nothing in correctness — yet the candidate count keeps climbing (\(15\) at \(ef{=}2\), up to \(26\) at \(ef{=}16\)). That is the cost side: every extra candidate is extra distance computations, extra latency. Past the knee of the cost curve you are paying more and getting the same answer.

So the operational recipe for \(ef\) is: sweep it on a held-out query set, plot recall against latency, and stop at the knee — the smallest \(ef\) that hits your recall target. Larger is not safer; it is just slower. Unlike \(M\) and \(efConstruction\), which are baked in at build time and cost a full rebuild to change, \(ef\) (i.e. \(efSearch\)) is a per-query dial you can turn at serve time, even per-request — high \(ef\) for a precision-critical query, low \(ef\) when latency is tight.

And the division of labour between the two build-time knobs is worth fixing in your head. M controls the graph's connectivity — more edges per node means more escape routes and higher attainable recall, at the price of memory (the edges are stored) and a slower build. efConstruction controls how hard the builder searches for each new node's neighbours — higher means a better-quality graph (so a lower \(efSearch\) suffices later) at the price of a slower one-time build. The pattern across all three: you are always buying recall with either memory, build time, or query latency, and the art is spending in whichever currency is cheapest for your deployment.

When HNSW is the wrong lane

HNSW is the default for a reason — it gives the best recall-for-latency of any in-memory ANN index, and for low-latency serving on a corpus that fits in RAM it is almost always the right first choice. But "almost always" is not "always", and a good engineer knows the edges of a tool before reaching for it. HNSW has three real boundaries.

It is memory-hungry, and the memory is the graph, not the vectors. Every node stores up to \(M\) edges per layer, and those edges are pure overhead on top of the vectors themselves. A common rule of thumb puts the graph at a meaningful fraction of the raw vector storage. So on a corpus that barely fits in RAM as raw float32, plain HNSW may not fit at all — which is exactly why the production answer is often HNSW-PQ: HNSW for the graph navigation, PQ to compress the vectors the graph points at, so the whole thing stays in memory at billion scale.

Inserts are cheap, deletes are not. Adding a vector to an HNSW graph is an incremental local operation. Removing one is genuinely awkward: the deleted node was a routing waypoint for other nodes' walks, and most implementations only tombstone it (mark it dead and skip it) rather than truly splicing it out. Tombstones accumulate, the graph slowly degrades, and the real fix is a periodic full rebuild — or the segment merge that quietly performs one for you. On a high-churn corpus — documents constantly added and deleted — that rebuild cost is a standing tax, and an IVF index (where you just reassign points to cells) can be the calmer choice.

The graph does not shard cleanly. HNSW's whole power is its long-range edges; cut the graph across machines and you cut the very shortcuts that make it fast, because a single greedy walk now wants to hop across a network boundary. Partitioned-by-design indexes like IVF distribute far more naturally — each cell can live on its own shard. At a scale where the index cannot fit one machine, HNSW's in-memory elegance becomes a distributed-systems headache.

None of this dethrones HNSW; it places it. The decision rule that falls out: reach for HNSW when the corpus fits in the RAM of one machine and you want the best recall-per-millisecond, accept its rebuild cost on a low-churn corpus, and reach for IVF (or IVF-PQ) when you are RAM-bound, sharded, or churning hard. The next lanes — partition and compression — are not competitors to HNSW so much as the answers to the exact regimes where HNSW strains.

Carve the galaxy into sectors

A graph walks; a partition sorts. The inverted file (IVF) first runs \(k\)-means over the corpus to pick a few hundred or thousand centroids, carving the space into Voronoi cells. Every vector is filed under its nearest centroid — built once, offline. At query time you don't scan the corpus; you find the cells nearest the query and probe only those.

The coarse quantizer

The widget showed the cells lighting up; this beat names the machine that draws them. The heart of IVF is a coarse quantizer: a function that maps any vector to which cell it belongs to, and nothing finer. It is "coarse" precisely because it throws away everything except the cell id — it is a deliberately low-resolution map of the space, and that low resolution is what makes it fast.

Building it is one offline step — a standard clustering step (k-means): run k-means over a sample of the corpus to find \(nlist\) centroids \(c_1, \dots, c_{nlist}\). Those centroids induce a Voronoi partition — every point in space belongs to the cell of its nearest centroid, and the cell boundaries are the perpendicular bisectors between neighbouring centroids. The corpus is then filed: each vector is assigned to its nearest centroid's inverted list (hence "inverted file" — the name and the structure are borrowed straight from the term→postings inverted index of Lecture 3, except the "term" is now a centroid and the "postings" are the vectors in that cell).

At query time the coarse quantizer runs twice over, conceptually. First it quantizes the query: compute the distance from \(q\) to all \(nlist\) centroids and rank the cells. This is cheap — \(nlist\) is a few hundred or thousand, not \(N\). Then it probes: take the \(nprobe\) nearest cells and do an exact scan only inside those lists. Everything outside the probed cells is never touched. The search cost drops from \(O(N \cdot d)\) to roughly \(O(nlist \cdot d)\) for the centroid ranking plus \(O(nprobe \cdot \tfrac{N}{nlist} \cdot d)\) for scanning the probed lists — and with \(nprobe \ll nlist\) that second term is a small fraction of \(N\).

The one number you choose at build time is \(nlist\), and it has a sweet spot. Too few cells and each inverted list is huge, so probing even one cell is nearly a full scan — no speed-up. Too many cells and each list is tiny, but now the centroids are so dense that the true neighbours of a query scatter across many cells, so you must raise \(nprobe\) to recover them — and the centroid-ranking cost \(O(nlist \cdot d)\) itself grows. A common heuristic is \(nlist \approx \sqrt{N}\), balancing the two costs; production systems tune it empirically.

Notice what IVF is not: it is not a compression scheme. The vectors inside each list are still full float32 — IVF makes search fast by skipping most of the corpus, but every vector it does look at, it looks at exactly. That is why IVF is so often paired with PQ into IVF-PQ: IVF decides which vectors to look at (skip the cells), PQ decides how cheaply to look at each one (compressed ADC distances). The two lanes compose, and the next beats quantify each half before the comparison beat puts them on one axis.

An inverted file (IVF) first carves the space into nlist = 3 cells: every vector is assigned to its nearest centroid (◇). Search will no longer scan all 9 points — only the points in the cells we choose to probe.

The query (★) is matched to the nearest centroid: it lands in cell c0. Its three true nearest neighbours are ringed — two sit inside c0, but the third lies just across the border in c1.

nprobe = 1 probes only the query's own cell, c0. It finds the two true neighbours inside c0 but misses the third (stranded in c1): 2 of 3 → recall@3 = 0.6667. Probing one cell is fast but leaves a neighbour behind.

nprobe = 2 also probes the second-nearest cell, c1 — exactly where the stranded neighbour sits. Now all three true neighbours are found: 3 of 3 → recall@3 = 1.0. More probes buy recall at the cost of more work; nprobe is the dial.

Recall vs nprobe, by hand

The knob is nprobe — how many of the nearest cells to probe. Take nine points in three cells, a query \(q\) that lands in cell \(c_0\), and its three true nearest neighbours: two inside \(c_0\), one just across the border in \(c_1\).

With nprobe = 1 we probe only \(c_0\). We find the two neighbours inside it but miss the third (stranded in \(c_1\)): \(2/3\) → recall@3 = 0.6667. With nprobe = 2 we also probe \(c_1\) — the second-nearest cell, exactly where the third neighbour sits — so \(3/3\) → recall@3 = 1.0.

That is the whole IVF tradeoff: probing more cells raises recall but costs more work. The danger is the boundary neighbour — a true neighbour that sits just outside the query's own cell. One cell alone always risks missing it; a couple of probes recover it. nprobe is the dial between speed and recall, and it is the reason IVF is paired with a re-ranking pass over the candidates it returns.

The full nprobe sweep, by hand

The worked example proved the climb on a tiny 9-point graph from \(0.6667\) to \(1.0\) in one step. To see the shape of the recall-vs-nprobe curve — and the work it costs — take a slightly larger toy: 20 points in 5 cells (\(nlist = 5\)), with a query \(q = (5,5)\) landing in cell \(c_0\), looking for its \(k = 5\) true nearest neighbours. The neighbours are not all in one cell: 3 of them sit in \(c_0\), one in \(c_4\), one in \(c_1\). The cells, ranked by centroid distance to \(q\), come out in the order \(c_0, c_4, c_1, c_2, c_3\) — so increasing \(nprobe\) peels them off in exactly that order.

Sweep \(nprobe\), tracking both recall@5 and the number of points actually scanned. \(nprobe = 1\) — probe \(\{c_0\}\), scan \(4\) points, find \(3\) of the \(5\) true NN → recall = 0.6. \(nprobe = 2\) — probe \(\{c_0, c_4\}\), scan \(8\) points, find \(4\) → recall = 0.8. \(nprobe = 3\) — probe \(\{c_0, c_4, c_1\}\), scan \(13\) points, find all \(5\) → recall = 1.0. \(nprobe = 4\) — probe four cells, scan \(17\) points, recall \(1.0\). \(nprobe = 5\) — probe all five cells, scan all \(20\) points (this is now a full exact scan), recall \(1.0\).

Read the two columns together and the IVF tradeoff is laid bare as a pair of curves. Recall climbs \(0.6 \to 0.8 \to 1.0\) and then plateaus — once \(nprobe = 3\) has captured every true neighbour, probing more cells finds nothing new. Meanwhile the work climbs monotonically: \(4 \to 8 \to 13 \to 17 \to 20\) points scanned, until at \(nprobe = nlist\) you are scanning the entire corpus and have thrown away the whole point of the index. The optimal \(nprobe\) is the one at the recall plateau's start — here \(3\) — the fewest cells that capture your recall target.

This is the same knee-of-the-curve story as HNSW's \(ef\), and the parallel is worth making explicit because it generalises: every ANN parameter is a recall-vs-work dial, and the right setting is the knee where recall saturates. \(ef\) and \(nprobe\) are the same idea wearing two costumes — widen the search effort until recall stops improving, then stop. The structures differ (a graph frontier vs a set of cells) but the tuning discipline is identical.

One last thing the numbers make vivid: the true neighbours scattered across \(c_0\), \(c_4\), and \(c_1\) are the boundary-neighbour problem from the worked example, generalised. The more cells your true neighbours spill into, the higher the \(nprobe\) you need — and how much they spill depends on \(nlist\) and on how cleanly your data clusters. This is the deep reason IVF results are almost always re-ranked: IVF returns an approximate candidate set cheaply, then an exact distance pass (or a cross-encoder, as in the latency budget) re-sorts the shortlist. IVF's job is recall of the candidate set, not final precision; precision is bought back downstream.

When IVF strains

IVF earns its place by being cheap to build and cheap to shard — but those same design choices give it failure modes that HNSW does not have, and naming them keeps you from blaming the structure for a problem you handed it.

The hard edge is the cell boundary. A query that lands near the wall between two cells has its true neighbours split across both, and probing only its own cell silently drops half of them — exactly the boundary-neighbour we watched cost recall in the worked examples. The cure is raising \(nprobe\), but that is a blunt cure: you pay to scan whole extra cells just to catch a few stragglers near one edge. In high dimensions, where (per the concentration beat) every point is near many boundaries, this gets worse, and IVF's recall-per-unit-work falls behind a good graph's. IVF trades a little recall fragility for its cheap build; HNSW trades a costly build for fewer boundary surprises.

Stale centroids are a silent recall leak. The centroids are trained once on a snapshot of the data. As the corpus drifts — new topics, new vocabulary, a shifting distribution — the Voronoi cells no longer match where the data actually lives. Lists become lopsided (some cells overflow while others empty), the partition stops reflecting true neighbourhoods, and recall quietly erodes without any error ever being thrown. The fix is periodic re-training of the coarse quantizer and re-assignment of the corpus — an operational cost that, like HNSW's rebuilds, belongs below the waterline of the Iceberg.

Imbalanced cells wreck the latency tail. k-means does not promise equal-sized cells. If the data is clustered, a few centroids capture huge lists while others capture almost nothing. Now \(nprobe\) is misleading: probing "3 cells" might mean scanning 3 tiny lists for one query and 3 enormous ones for another, so the latency varies wildly query-to-query even at fixed \(nprobe\). Since SLAs are written against the p99 tail (the latency budget beat), this variance is the real enemy — and it is why production IVF often uses balanced-clustering variants or caps list sizes.

The decision rule, completing the pair with the HNSW bounds beat: choose IVF / IVF-PQ when you are RAM-constrained, need cheap incremental updates, or must shard across machines — and accept that you will re-train centroids on drift and watch your p99 for cell imbalance. Choose HNSW when the index fits one box and you want the best recall-per-millisecond with no \(nprobe\) boundary fragility. Neither is universally better; each is the answer to a different operational question, which is exactly why the next two beats lay all the families on one comparison axis.

Fold the star-charts

Graphs and cells make search fast, but the vectors themselves still have to fit in memory — and at a billion vectors of a few hundred dimensions each, that is the wall. The third lane is compression: don't store the full float vector, store a handful of codebook indices that approximate it. The star-chart folds; the lane stays open.

Quantize the parts, not the whole

The compress turn said "store codebook indices instead of floats"; this beat explains the one idea that makes that practical — and it is a decomposition idea, the same flavour as Lecture 6's distributional intuition: the whole is too big to quantize directly, so quantize its parts and recombine.

Start with the naive version and watch it fail. Scalar quantization rounds each of the \(D\) coordinates to one of a few levels — say 256 levels, one byte each. That is simple but weak: it ignores how coordinates co-vary, and to represent a \(D\)-dimensional vector well you would need a single codebook covering the whole \(D\)-dimensional space. To get \(L\) levels per dimension over \(D\) dimensions you would need \(L^D\) codewords — astronomically many. Vector quantization (one codebook for the whole vector) has exactly this curse: the codebook you would need is exponential in \(D\).

Product quantization (Jégou, Douze & Schmid, 2011) sidesteps the curse with a Cartesian-product trick. Split the \(D\)-dimensional vector into \(m\) contiguous subvectors of \(d^* = D/m\) dimensions each. Quantize each subspace independently with its own small codebook of \(k\) centroids (the canonical choice is \(k = 256\), so each code is exactly one byte). A full vector is then represented by \(m\) bytes — one codebook index per subspace.

The decomposition is what beats the curse, and the arithmetic is the whole point. \(m\) subspaces each with \(k\) centroids can represent \(k^m\) distinct reconstructed vectors — an enormous effective codebook — while storing only \(m \times k\) actual centroids and only \(m\) bytes per vector. With \(m = 8\) and \(k = 256\), that is \(256^8 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}\) representable points from \(8 \times 256 = 2048\) stored centroids. You get the expressiveness of a giant codebook for the storage of a tiny one — because the giant codebook is the Cartesian product of the small per-subspace ones, never materialised.

There is a real assumption hiding in "split into contiguous subvectors": PQ implicitly hopes the subspaces are roughly independent and equally important. When the coordinates are correlated or have wildly different scales, slicing them blindly wastes codebook capacity — which is why production PQ is preceded by an OPQ rotation (a learned orthonormal rotation that decorrelates and balances the subspaces before splitting), and why TurboQuant's random rotation is doing a related job. But the core idea stands on its own: quantize the parts, multiply the codebooks, store the bytes. The next three beats make each step concrete — training one codebook, computing a distance through the codes, and counting the memory.

A raw embedding is a list of floats. Here D = 8 dimensions in float32 — 4 bytes each — so the vector costs 8 × 4 = 32 bytes. Multiply that by a billion vectors and memory, not compute, becomes the wall.

Product quantization splits the vector into m = 4 subvectors of d* = 2 dimensions each. Each subvector lives in its own little subspace and will be quantized independently — that is the “product” in product quantization.

Each subvector is replaced by the nearest of k = 256 learned centroids, so it stores just a centroid index. With 256 centroids the index fits in one byte. Four subvectors → four bytes.

32 bytes became 4 — compression 8×. At real sizes the win is bigger: a 768-d vector → 96 B (32×), a 128-d vector → 8 B (64×). Distances are then approximate via ADC — an m×k table lookup — and a representative recall@1 for PQ-m4 is ≈ 0.70. Memory shrinks; a little recall is the price (the memory↔recall dial).

Training one codebook, by hand

Before PQ can encode anything it must learn its codebooks, and a codebook is just the output of k-means run on one subspace. The widget shows the bytes; this beat opens the box and runs Lloyd's algorithm by hand on a single subspace, so you can see the inertia fall — the concrete meaning of "the codebook converged".

Take six 2-D subvectors that obviously form two clusters: \((1,1), (2,1), (1,2)\) near the origin and \((8,8), (9,8), (8,9)\) far away. We want \(k = 2\) centroids. Initialise them at the first two points, \(c_0 = (1,1)\) and \(c_1 = (2,1)\) — a deliberately bad start with both centroids in the same cluster, so the descent has real work to do. Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps: assign each point to its nearest centroid, then recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points. The quantity that must fall is the inertia \(\sum_i \lVert x_i - c_{a(i)} \rVert^2\) — the total squared distance from points to their assigned centroids, exactly the distortion PQ is trying to minimise.

Iteration 0 — assign to the initial centroids \((1,1),(2,1)\). Points \((1,1)\) and \((1,2)\) go to \(c_0\); the remaining four — \((2,1)\) and the three far points — are nearer \(c_1=(2,1)\), giving assignment \([0,1,0,1,1,1]\). Inertia of that bad split: \(\mathbf{284.0}\) — dominated by the three far points yoked to a centroid at \((2,1)\). Iteration 1 — recompute: the means move to \(c_0=(1.0,1.5)\), \(c_1=(6.75,6.5)\). Re-assign: now \([0,0,0,1,1,1]\) — the three near points to \(c_0\), the three far to \(c_1\). Inertia drops to \(\mathbf{20.6875}\). Final — recompute once more: \(c_0=(1.3333,1.3333)\), \(c_1=(8.3333,8.3333)\), the true cluster means. Inertia settles at \(\mathbf{2.6667}\).

The inertia sequence \(284.0 \to 20.6875 \to 2.6667\) is the whole story of k-means in three numbers, and it has two properties worth stating. First, it is monotone non-increasing — every Lloyd step either lowers the inertia or leaves it unchanged, which is why the algorithm provably converges (each step minimises the objective with the other variable held fixed; this is coordinate descent). Second, the size of the drop tells you the start was bad: the first recompute alone took us from \(284\) to \(20.7\), because a single good reassignment rescued the three far points from their wrong centroid.

Now scale this up in your head to what PQ actually does. This was one subspace; a real PQ index runs this same k-means \(m\) times in parallel, once per subspace, each producing a \(k=256\)-entry codebook. The encode step we saw in the widget — "map each subvector to its nearest centroid" — is just the assignment step of these trained codebooks, run on a new vector. And the converged inertia is precisely the quantization error: the lower it is, the closer the reconstruction, the higher the eventual recall. "Train the codebooks" is exactly "run \(m\) k-means until the inertias stop falling", and its cost is the production pain that the next frontier note (TurboQuant) is trying to erase.

The memory math

Product quantization (PQ) splits a vector into \(m\) subvectors and replaces each with the index of its nearest of \(k = 256\) learned centroids. With \(256\) centroids an index is one byte.

A \(D = 8\) float32 vector costs \(8 \times 4 = 32\) bytes. Split into \(m = 4\) subvectors → \(4\) one-byte indices → \(4\) bytes. Compression \(= 32 / 4 = 8\times\). At real sizes the win grows: a \(768\)-d vector is \(3072\) bytes → \(96\) bytes (\(32\times\)); a \(128\)-d vector is \(512\) bytes → \(8\) bytes (\(64\times\)).

Distances are then computed approximately by ADC (asymmetric distance computation): split the query into the same \(m\) subvectors, precompute an \(m \times k\) table of subvector-to-centroid distances, and sum table lookups — no decompression. The price is recall: a representative recall@1 for 4-subvector PQ against exact search is ≈ 0.70. Memory shrinks by \(8\times\); a little recall is what you pay — the memory↔recall dial.

Frontier — TurboQuant. The biggest production pain of PQ is training the codebook. TurboQuant (Zandieh et al., 2025) removes it: it is data-oblivious and online — randomly rotate the vector (which concentrates its coordinates into a Beta distribution), then apply a per-coordinate optimal scalar quantizer, with a two-stage MSE + 1-bit QJL residual giving an unbiased inner-product estimate. It lands within a small (\(\approx 2.7\times\)) constant of the information-theoretic distortion bound and reports better recall with near-zero indexing time, outperforming existing PQ techniques. We keep FAISS PQ as the taught baseline; TurboQuant is where the field is going.

The codebook you never train

Everything above assumed the codebook is learned: k-means on your vectors, and the centroids that fall out are the alphabet the codes are written in. That assumption has a cost you cannot see from inside your own benchmark. PQ carries no theoretical error bound, so the honest statement about its accuracy is «it measured well on the data we tried» — which says nothing about the corpus you have not tried. On MSong, a public benchmark, the fast SIMD implementation of PQ passes 50 % average relative error on estimated distances and cannot reach 60 % recall even after re-ranking (Gao & Long, 2024).

RaBitQ buys the missing guarantee by giving up the learned codebook entirely. Normalise every vector onto the unit sphere. Take the \(2^{D}\) vertices of a hypercube with coordinates \(\pm 1/\sqrt{D}\) — they are already spread evenly over that sphere, and nobody had to fit them to anything. Rotate them once by a random orthogonal matrix, a Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform. A vector's code is then the nearest vertex: a string of \(D\) bits, one per dimension. The distance estimate read off that code is unbiased, and its deviation is bounded on the order of \(1/\sqrt{D-1}\).

Read the trade honestly, because it does not run one way. At \(D = 768\), one bit per dimension is 96 bytes; PQ with \(m = 8\) is 8 bytes. PQ compresses twelve times harder, and at a billion vectors that difference decides architectures. What RaBitQ offers in exchange is no training step, no codebook in memory, and a bound that improves as dimension grows — 0.378 at \(D = 8\), 0.036 at \(D = 768\). For embeddings, where \(D\) runs into the hundreds, the slope points the right way.

So the decision rule is not «RaBitQ is better». It is: when the corpus drifts and nobody re-trains the codebook, or when you must state a worst case before rollout, take the guarantee. When memory is the binding constraint and the corpus is stable, PQ's twelvefold edge wins. And when IVF-PQ already runs and its metrics hold, swap nothing — replacing a quantizer is not an upgrade, it is a fresh set of risks.

Where the guarantee already runs

A method with a proof is still only a paper until someone ships it. RaBitQ shipped: it is integrated into NVIDIA's cuVS library as IVF-RaBitQ, the same pairing you already know — a coarse quantizer to pick the cells, a fine one to score inside them, only with bits instead of a learned codebook.

The numbers from that integration are worth reading as a shape rather than as a scoreboard. At recall around 0.95, IVF-RaBitQ serves three times the queries per second of CAGRA, a graph method, and builds its indexes 14.7× faster; against IVF-PQ it is better than four times the throughput. The build-time gap is the one to notice, because it is the direct consequence of the design: there is no codebook to train, so index construction is mostly arithmetic on vectors you already have.

And the throughput gap has a cause worth naming too. IVF-PQ typically re-ranks its shortlist against the raw vectors, which means touching full-precision data on the hot path; the bit codes carry enough of the distance that this step can be skipped. What looked like a memory disadvantage on the previous page — 96 bytes against 8 — buys back some of its cost here, in the traffic you do not generate.

The honest summary of the whole comparison, then, is not a winner. It is a shape: PQ compresses harder, RaBitQ predicts better and builds faster, and which one you want depends on whether your bottleneck is RAM or trust.

A quantizer fails quietly

Here is the failure mode worth carrying out of this chapter, whichever method you pick. A broken quantizer does not raise an exception. The index builds, queries return, latency looks fine, and the only symptom is that the answers are slightly worse than they should be — which you will attribute to the encoder, the chunking, the ranker, anything but the compression step you stopped thinking about weeks ago.

The cheap guard is to measure the quantizer directly rather than through end-to-end recall. Take a sample of queries, compute exact distances to a few thousand vectors, compute the estimated distances your index would use, and look at the relative error — the average and, more importantly, the maximum. The average tells you the typical quality; the maximum tells you whether the estimate ever collapses. PQ's MSong failure shows up in exactly this test and does not show up in a smoke test on your favourite five queries.

The second guard is a rule, not a measurement: re-run that check when the corpus changes, not when someone complains. A learned codebook is fitted to the data it saw; the day your corpus takes in a new domain, the codebook is quietly out of date. This is the whole practical difference the bound buys — with RaBitQ the check is a formality, with PQ it is a calendar item.

Eight dimensions, one byte

Take the mystique out of it with eight numbers. Normalise a vector and you get, say, \((0.3497,\ -0.1354,\ 0.6204,\ -0.4512,\ 0.2030,\ -0.2482,\ 0.4061,\ -0.0564)\). Now read only the signs: plus, minus, plus, minus, plus, minus, plus, minus. That is the code — 10101010, eight bits, one byte.

Unfolding it is just as plain. Every bit becomes a coordinate \(\pm 1/\sqrt{8} = \pm 0.3536\), and the resulting vector is the chosen hypercube vertex. One inner product between it and the original gives \(0.8734\) — how well the vertex stands in for the vector. That number is the whole quantization quality of this vector, and it took a sign read and one product to get.

Notice what is absent: no training, no lookup of the nearest centroid, no table in memory. This is why index construction is bounded by how fast you can stream vectors rather than by k-means, and why the error bound comes from the geometry of the rotation rather than from a measurement on a corpus that happened to be kind.

One honesty note about the toy: the real procedure rotates the vector by a random orthogonal matrix before taking the signs. We dropped that step so the arithmetic fits in your head — but it is the rotation that makes the bound hold for any input, so do not carry away the impression that signs alone are the method.

Distance through the codes — ADC, by hand

Compression is only useful if you can search the compressed vectors without decompressing them. The trick that makes PQ a search index and not merely a storage format is ADC — asymmetric distance computation — and "asymmetric" is the whole idea: the query stays a full, exact vector, while the database vectors are their PQ codes. You never reconstruct a database vector; you compute distance to its code through a precomputed table.

Here is the mechanism on a small case: \(D = 8\), \(m = 4\) subspaces of \(d^* = 2\), each with \(k = 4\) toy centroids. Take the query \(q = (2,1,0,3,4,2,1,0)\), split into four subqueries \((2,1),(0,3),(4,2),(1,0)\). The one-time setup is the ADC table: for each subspace \(j\) and each centroid \(c\), precompute the squared L2 distance from subquery \(j\) to centroid \(c\). That is a \(4 \times 4\) table, computed once per query and then reused for every database vector.

The ADC table (row = subspace, column = centroid index): row 0 \([0, 5, 2, 5]\); row 1 \([0, 5, 20, 10]\); row 2 \([0, 10, 5, 20]\); row 3 \([0, 13, 5, 2]\). Now take a database vector stored as the codes \([2, 0, 3, 1]\) — meaning subspace 0 → centroid 2, subspace 1 → centroid 0, subspace 2 → centroid 3, subspace 3 → centroid 1. ADC distance is just four table lookups summed: \(\text{table}[0][2] + \text{table}[1][0] + \text{table}[2][3] + \text{table}[3][1] = 2 + 0 + 20 + 13 = \mathbf{35}\).

Compare against the truth. The database vector's actual values reconstruct to \((3,2,0,3,0,4,3,3)\), and its true (un-quantized) form gives an exact distance of \(\mathbf{37}\) to the query. So ADC says \(35\), reality says \(37\): the gap of \(2\) is the quantization error — ADC measures distance to the reconstruction, which sits a little off the true vector. The estimate is close, and crucially it is consistent — every database vector is judged by the same table — so the ranking it produces is almost always the right ranking.

Now count what ADC saved. The naive way to score a query against \(N\) PQ-coded vectors would be to reconstruct each one (\(m \times d^*\) float writes) and compute a full \(D\)-dimensional distance — no cheaper than not compressing at all. ADC computes the \(m \times k\) table once, then scores each database vector with just \(m\) table lookups and \(m-1\) additions — here \(4\) lookups, no multiplies, no decompression, per vector. On a list of a million candidates that is millions of \(D\)-dimensional distance computations replaced by millions of \(m\)-entry table sums. This is why PQ is fast and not just small.

Two refinements the production world adds, named so you recognise them. SDC (symmetric distance computation) quantizes the query too and reads distances from a centroid-to-centroid table — slightly faster setup, slightly worse accuracy; ADC is the usual choice because keeping the query exact costs little and helps recall. And fast-scan / SIMD kernels (FAISS's PQFastScan) pack the lookups so a CPU does many in one vector instruction — the same arithmetic, laid out to run at memory speed. The idea you computed by hand is exactly the idea running in the 12-millisecond search hop of the latency budget.

The memory ledger, by hand

The worked example showed the headline trade — \(32\) bytes down to \(4\), an \(8\times\) win — on one toy vector. But PQ has two knobs, \(m\) (subspaces) and \(k\) (centroids per subspace), and at billion scale the choice between them is a real budget decision measured in gigabytes of RAM that cost real money. Let us keep the full ledger.

The formula is small. A code uses \(\log_2 k\) bits, so \(k = 256\) gives \(8\) bits = \(1\) byte per subspace, and \(k = 16\) gives \(4\) bits = half a byte. Bytes per vector \(= m \times \dfrac{\log_2 k}{8}\). The float32 baseline is \(\text{dim} \times 4\) bytes. Compression is their ratio. And at a billion vectors, since one byte per vector is one gigabyte of index, index size in GB \(\approx\) bytes-per-vector (decimal GB).

Four configurations on the ledger. (A) 768-d, \(m=96\), \(k=256\): \(96 \times 1 = 96\) bytes/vector vs \(768 \times 4 = 3072\) → compression \(\mathbf{32\times}\), index at \(10^9\) vectors \(= \mathbf{96}\) GB. (B) 768-d, \(m=192\), \(k=256\): \(192\) bytes/vector vs \(3072\) → \(\mathbf{16\times}\), index \(= \mathbf{192}\) GB (finer subspaces, better recall, double the RAM). (C) 768-d, \(m=96\), \(k=16\): \(96 \times 0.5 = 48\) bytes/vector vs \(3072\) → \(\mathbf{64\times}\), index \(= \mathbf{48}\) GB (4-bit codes, half the RAM of A, lower recall). (D) 128-d, \(m=8\), \(k=256\): \(8\) bytes/vector vs \(128 \times 4 = 512\) → \(\mathbf{64\times}\), index \(= \mathbf{8}\) GB.

Read those four rows as a Pareto front and the engineering becomes a conversation about machines. Configuration A (\(96\) GB) fits comfortably on a single large-memory server; B (\(192\) GB) buys recall by doubling \(m\) but now needs a bigger box or a shard split; C (\(48\) GB) halves the RAM of A by dropping to 4-bit codes, trading recall for fitting on a cheaper machine; D shows the same \(64\times\) is easy on a 128-d vector because the float baseline was smaller to begin with. The same billion vectors can demand \(8\) GB or \(192\) GB depending only on these two knobs — a 24-fold spread in hardware cost from settings that all "just run PQ".

The deeper lesson is that \(m\) and \(k\) buy recall in different currencies, and you should know which you are spending. Raising \(m\) (more, finer subspaces) lowers quantization error and lifts recall, at a linear cost in bytes — it is the expensive, reliable lever. Lowering \(k\) from \(256\) to \(16\) halves the bytes per code but coarsens every codebook, costing recall — it is the aggressive memory lever you pull when even PQ-at-1-byte will not fit. In practice teams fix \(k = 256\) (the byte-aligned sweet spot) and tune \(m\) against a recall target, then reach for 4-bit codes or OPQ only when the RAM ceiling forces it.

And this is the half of the index-design picture PQ owns. HNSW and IVF answered "which vectors do I look at?"; PQ answers "how many bytes does each vector cost, and what recall does that buy?" The two halves compose into the real production indexes — IVF-PQ, HNSW-PQ — and the next beat finally lays all five families side by side on one decision axis so you can pick deliberately rather than by reflex.

What PQ quietly costs

PQ is the lane that lets a billion vectors live in RAM, and its memory math is so seductive that it is easy to forget you bought that compression with something. You bought it with lossiness, and unlike HNSW's or IVF's approximation — which can be tuned back to exact by widening \(ef\) or \(nprobe\) — PQ's loss is baked into the stored representation. The bytes are smaller because information was thrown away on the way in, and no amount of search effort recovers it.

The recall ceiling is structural. Once a vector is stored as its codes, the finest distinction PQ can ever make is between two different reconstructions. Two database vectors that quantize to the same codes are, to ADC, identical — their true difference is invisible. That is why a representative recall@1 for 4-subvector PQ against exact search sits around \(0.70\): roughly three in ten top-1 answers are displaced by quantization, no matter how long you search. You cannot tune your way past it; you can only spend more bytes (raise \(m\)) to raise the ceiling.

This is why PQ almost never ships alone — it ships with a re-rank. The standard production pattern is: use PQ (usually inside IVF-PQ) to cheaply shortlist a few hundred candidates from a billion, then re-rank that shortlist with exact full-precision distances on the few candidates' true vectors. PQ's lossy distance is good enough to find the right neighbourhood; exact distance on the shortlist restores the precision PQ gave up. The lossy index does recall, the exact re-rank does precision — the same division of labour as the cross-encoder cascade, one level down.

The independence assumption can bite. PQ's whole trick — quantize subspaces independently — assumes the subspaces carry roughly independent, comparably-scaled information. When dimensions are strongly correlated or one block of coordinates dominates the variance, naive slicing wastes codebook capacity on the redundant blocks and starves the informative ones, and recall suffers more than the byte budget predicts. The standard fix is OPQ — learn an orthonormal rotation that decorrelates and balances the dimensions before splitting — and it is rarely optional on real embeddings. (TurboQuant's random rotation is a data-free cousin of the same move.)

And one cost that is pure operations, straight from the codebook-training beat: the codebooks are learned from the data, so when the data drifts, the codebooks go stale exactly as IVF's centroids do. A codebook trained on last year's distribution quantizes this year's vectors badly, and recall erodes silently. Re-training the codebooks and re-encoding the corpus is a recurring batch job — another line item below the Iceberg's waterline.

So place PQ correctly: it is the memory lane, not a search lane, and it is lossy in a way the others are not. Reach for it when you are RAM-bound at scale, always pair it with a re-rank to win back precision, run OPQ on correlated embeddings, and budget for codebook re-training on drift. Used that way it is the difference between an index that fits and one that does not. Used naively — PQ alone, no re-rank, no rotation, never re-trained — it quietly serves a worse ranking and tells no one.

Three lanes, two axes

We have now built three lanes and it is tempting to ask "which one is best?" — but that is the wrong question, and asking it correctly is the point of this turn. HNSW, IVF, and PQ are not three competitors on one axis; they answer two different questions, and the production indexes you actually deploy are combinations of answers, not winners of a contest.

Here are the two orthogonal axes. Axis 1 — how do I avoid looking at every vector? This is the search-acceleration axis, and it has two strategies: walk a navigable graph (HNSW) or probe a partition of cells (IVF). Axis 2 — how few bytes can each vector cost? This is the compression axis, and PQ is the answer (raw float32 being the no-compression baseline). The key insight is that the two axes are independent: you choose a search strategy and, separately, a storage representation, and almost any pairing is a valid index.

That is exactly why the real FAISS index zoo reads like a Cartesian product. Flat = no acceleration + no compression (exact, the baseline). HNSW = graph acceleration + raw vectors. IVF = cell acceleration + raw vectors. IVF-PQ = cell acceleration + PQ compression — the billion-scale workhorse. HNSW-PQ = graph acceleration + PQ compression — low-latency and compressed. The names are literally the two choices concatenated, and once you see the grid, the bewildering FAISS index strings stop being a vocabulary to memorise and become a sentence to read.

Framing it as two axes also dissolves the false rivalries. "HNSW vs PQ" is a category error — they are not on the same axis, so they do not compete; they compose (HNSW-PQ). "HNSW vs IVF" is a real rivalry, because they are on the same axis (both are search-acceleration strategies), and that is the one genuine either/or in the lecture — the one the HNSW-bounds and IVF-bounds beats already adjudicated (in-memory low-latency vs shardable/RAM-bound). PQ never competes with either; it stacks underneath whichever you chose.

So the comparison beat that follows is not a leaderboard. It is a small table whose rows are the criteria you actually care about — search complexity, build and update cost, memory, recall, and the workload each is best for — and whose columns are the five families. Read across a row to compare the families on one criterion; read down a column to characterise one family. The worked comparison turns that table into a decision tree, so that by the end you can be handed a workload and derive the index rather than guess it.

The decision matrix, read aloud

Here is the comparison matrix in prose — five index families down the side, five criteria across the top — read so you can later glance at the table and hear the reasoning behind each cell. The five families are the Cartesian product from the previous turn: flat, HNSW, IVF, IVF-PQ, HNSW-PQ.

Search complexity. flat is \(O(N{\cdot}D)\) — the full scan we are escaping. HNSW is \(O(\log N)\) — the logarithmic descent. IVF is \(O(nprobe{\cdot}\tfrac{N}{nlist}{\cdot}D)\) — scan only the probed cells. IVF-PQ is \(O(nprobe{\cdot}\tfrac{N}{nlist})\) ADC lookups — same cells, but each distance is a table sum, not a \(D\)-dim computation. HNSW-PQ is \(O(\log N)\) ADC lookups — the graph descent with compressed distances.

Memory / compression. flat is \(1\times\) (raw float32); HNSW is \(1\times\) plus graph overhead; IVF is \(1\times\) plus the inverted lists. The two PQ families compress: IVF-PQ and HNSW-PQ both sit at the PQ toy figure of \(8\times\) (and the realistic 768-d config reaches \(32\times\), from the memory ledger). Representative recall. flat is \(1.0\) by definition (exact); HNSW and IVF are tunable (via \(efSearch\) and \(nprobe\) respectively) and reach high recall; IVF-PQ and HNSW-PQ inherit PQ's lossy ceiling, a representative \(0.70\) recall@1 for 4-subvector PQ before re-ranking.

Now read the build / update column, because it is where the families diverge most in operations. flat has no build (append-only) — trivially updatable. HNSW has a high build cost but supports incremental insert (and the awkward deletes from its bounds beat). IVF must train centroids once, then insert is cheap. IVF-PQ trains centroids and codebooks. HNSW-PQ pays the high graph build plus codebooks. The pattern: every step toward speed-and-compression buys query-time cheapness with build-time and training cost — you front-load the work.

Reading the matrix top-to-bottom gives the best-for row, which is the decision tree in one line each. flat: small \(N\), or as the exact ground-truth baseline you measure recall against. HNSW: low-latency in-memory recall when the corpus fits in RAM. IVF: large \(N\) where cheap training and easy sharding matter. IVF-PQ: billion-scale and RAM-bound — the canonical big-index default. HNSW-PQ: when you want HNSW's latency and PQ's memory at once.

Turn that into the decision tree you can run in your head. Does the whole corpus fit in RAM as raw vectors? If yes and you want lowest latency → HNSW; if it barely fits → HNSW-PQ to claw back memory. If it does not fit raw → you need compression, so IVF-PQ (cheap to build and shard) is the default, dropping to 4-bit codes or finer \(m\) per the memory ledger. Tiny corpus or need a recall ground-truth? → flat. And in every compressed case, remember the lesson from the PQ-bounds beat: pair it with a re-rank, because the matrix's \(0.70\) recall is the pre-re-rank number, and exact re-scoring of the shortlist is what turns a billion-scale index into a billion-scale answer.

FAISS, vector DBs, the serving path

These structures don't float in the void — they live in software. FAISS is a library: you build an index (IndexFlat, IndexIVFPQ, IndexHNSWFlat) in your own process and call it. A vector database (the index plus storage, filtering, updates, replication, an API) is a service you query over the network. The rule of thumb: FAISS when the index fits one process you control; a vector DB when you need it managed, filtered, and shared.

Either way, a query is now a request with a budget. It embeds the query, runs ANN search, re-ranks the shortlist (the Lecture 10 cross-encoder, now sitting behind ANN), checks a cache, and responds — all under a latency SLA. That budget is the next climb.

Reading a FAISS factory string

FAISS hands you a tiny domain-specific language for building an index — the factory string you pass to index_factory(d, "…") — and it is not arbitrary syntax to memorise. It is a sentence in the two-axis grammar from the comparison beat: read left to right, it spells out preprocess → coarse-quantize → encode. Once you can parse it, you can read any production index off its config line.

The grammar has three slots, applied in order. Slot 1 — a preprocessing transform (optional): things like OPQ16_64 (an OPQ rotation into 64 dims with 16 subspaces) or PCA80 (PCA down to 80 dims). Slot 2 — the coarse quantizer / search structure: IVF4096 (an IVF with \(nlist = 4096\) cells), or HNSW32 (an HNSW graph with \(M = 32\)), or nothing (flat). Slot 3 — the per-vector encoding: Flat (store raw float32), PQ16 (product-quantize into 16 bytes), PQ16x4 (16 subspaces of 4 bits), or SQ8 (scalar quantize to 8 bits). Components are joined by commas.

Parse the canonical billion-scale string OPQ16_64,IVF4096,PQ16 slot by slot. OPQ16_64 → preprocess: an OPQ rotation producing a 64-dim space split into 16 subspaces, decorrelating before PQ (the PQ-bounds beat's required step). IVF4096 → search: an inverted file with \(nlist = 4096\) Voronoi cells — at query time probe the nearest \(nprobe\) of them. PQ16 → encode: product quantization into \(m = 16\) one-byte codes, so each vector costs \(16\) bytes regardless of the original dimension. The whole string says, in one line: rotate to decorrelate, partition into 4096 cells, store each vector as 16 PQ bytes — i.e. an IVF-PQ index, the billion-scale workhorse, fully specified.

Two more strings to make the grammar reflexive. HNSW32,Flat = an HNSW graph at \(M = 32\) over raw float32 vectors — the low-latency in-memory default, no compression. IVF65536_HNSW32,PQ16x4 = an IVF with 65536 cells whose coarse quantizer is itself an HNSW graph (so even ranking the centroids is fast when \(nlist\) is huge), encoding each vector as 16 four-bit PQ codes (8 bytes). That second one looks intimidating until you parse it slot by slot — and then it is just three design decisions concatenated.

This is the practical pay-off of the two-axis framing: the factory string is the two axes plus a preprocessing slot, serialised. When you read …IVF…,PQ… you know instantly it is cell-partition acceleration plus PQ compression — IVF-PQ — and everything the comparison matrix said about that family applies. The string is not a magic incantation; it is the index's design, written down. Learn to read it and the entire FAISS catalogue becomes legible, including the strings you will meet in vector-DB configs that wrap FAISS underneath.

One caution worth carrying: the factory string fixes the structure, but the runtime knobs — \(nprobe\) for the IVF, \(efSearch\) for the HNSW — are set after the index is built and tuned per the recall-vs-work sweeps from the climbs. The string decides what you built; the knobs decide how hard you search it. Both have to be right, and only the first one lives in the factory string.

Three families of vector engine

The previous beat drew a single line — library or service. Step across it and the service side looks like a noisy bazaar: product after product, each calling itself the fastest vector store alive. Sort them by origin rather than by marketing and they collapse into three families. Purpose-built engines — Qdrant, Milvus, Weaviate — were designed around the vector from day one: the vector is the primary object and everything else hangs off it. Extensions to a database you already run — pgvector for PostgreSQL, the vector search built into Elasticsearch and OpenSearch — add vectors to a store that already holds your relational rows or your inverted index. Managed services — Pinecone is the archetype — hand you an API address and keep the machines out of sight.

Open almost any of them and the engine room is familiar. Under the API sits an HNSW graph, or IVF cells, or PQ codes — exactly the structures this chapter assembled by hand. The knobs carry names you already know, dressed in each vendor's vocabulary: the width of the candidate frontier, how many cells to probe, how many bytes a compressed vector costs. Almost none of these products laid a new lane — and the ones that did say so out loud: the disk-resident graphs of the DiskANN line, ScaNN's own blend of partitioning and quantization, quantization schemes that do not reduce to plain PQ. The closed proprietary engines are an exception of a different kind: their index is simply not published, so what you have is their word and no way to check it. Almost all of them operate the lanes you have already learned to walk.

So the choice between families is not a choice of mathematics — it is a choice of operations. Where does the payload live next to the vector, and can you query it? How do updates land, and when do they become visible? What does the API promise about consistency and about replicas? How do filters compose with the search? Those are the questions that decide which engine fits, and not one of them is answered by an ANN paper. The next beats take the two that bite hardest — filtering and updates — and open them up.

When a filter meets the graph

In production almost nothing is asked as a bare nearest neighbours of \(q\). The real request is nearest neighbours of \(q\) among the documents that satisfy a predicate: this workspace, in stock, written in this language, visible to this user. That extra clause is metadata filtering, and it quietly voids the guarantee the whole chapter has been building. The graph was wired, and the cells were fitted, over the entire corpus. The predicate changes the set the answer must be drawn from — and changes nothing about the structure that finds it.

Two obvious strategies sit at the two extremes. Post-filtering searches first and applies the predicate to whatever came back. It never returns a forbidden document, so it looks correct — but the shortlist was chosen with no knowledge of the predicate. When the predicate is selective, when only a thin slice of the corpus passes it, nearly the whole shortlist is thrown away and the user receives a handful of results, or none at all, while the true matching neighbours sit just outside the shortlist that was fetched. That is a collapse of recall, and the more selective the filter, the more complete the collapse. The rough arithmetic — a shortlist of depth \(k\) leaves about \(s\cdot k\) survivors, where \(s\) is the fraction of the corpus the predicate keeps — holds only while the predicate is unrelated to closeness to the query; "written in this language" on a multilingual corpus is very much related, and then the estimate misleads in both directions. The standard cure is to re-ask with a larger shortlist, and it does work — but the cost grows roughly as \(k/s\), so a harsh predicate walks you straight back into the linear scan you were avoiding. Pre-filtering goes the other way: materialise the set that satisfies the predicate, then scan it exactly. Always correct, and genuinely fast when the surviving set is tiny — but it is the linear scan whose death opened this lecture. Fast and wrong, or right and linear.

So real engines do neither at the extremes: they push the predicate into the traversal — but they push it in two different ways, and which way decides how the search fails. One school walks only on nodes that pass: a candidate is tested against the predicate before it is admitted to the frontier, so the walk no longer happens in the graph at all but in the subgraph the predicate induces (Qdrant, ACORN, recent Lucene). The other walks through failing nodes, using them as transit and admitting only passing ones into the answer (Weaviate, older Lucene), which leaves the graph whole. For the first school the price is paid in connectivity. The graph's speed is its edges — the whole point of the HNSW-bounds beat — and a selective predicate switches off most of the nodes, so what survives can fall apart into islands with no surviving edge between them. A walk that starts on one island cannot step to another: it halts at a node whose every admissible neighbour is farther away, which is precisely the local minimum the \(ef\) climb escaped by widening the frontier. Except here widening barely helps, because the missing candidates are not merely unvisited — they are unreachable. For the second school nothing tears, because nothing was removed from the graph; the failure simply wears a different face. Hops are spent on nodes that can never become answers, the walk grows steadily dearer, and it exhausts its budget and stops early — before it has gathered enough passing neighbours.

Either way the engine wants to know, before it starts, how much of the corpus the predicate admits, and it switches lane on that: very selective, take the pre-filter's exact scan, cheap exactly because the set is small; barely selective, traverse as usual, since the graph is nearly intact. Engines know it in different ways. Qdrant genuinely estimates the predicate's cardinality from its own statistics; Weaviate, Lucene, OpenSearch and Milvus count the passing documents exactly, from a materialised allow-list or a bitmap handed to the traversal. The awkward middle is where engines genuinely differ and where vendor prose gets vague. And know the exception, because it is the one you are most likely to deploy: with an approximate index pgvector applies the filter after the index scan by default — that is precisely the post-filter this beat opened with, collapsing recall exactly as described — until you build a partial index shaped to the predicate or turn iterative scanning on. Keep that in mind when the next beat praises keeping vectors beside the rows. The consequence for you is blunt: the recall of a filtered search is a property of your predicates and your engine's filtering strategy, not of the index structure — measure it on the filters your product actually issues, never on the unfiltered benchmark.

Why "just add the vector" is not free

Every engine offers an insert call and a delete call, and both look like a line of code. The insert almost keeps its promise: adding a node to an HNSW graph is a local operation, and assigning a vector to an IVF cell is one comparison against the centroids. The delete does not. Most implementations leave the node exactly where it was and mark it dead — the tombstone the HNSW-bounds beat already named. The graph is not rewired, so the buried node keeps doing its old job as a waypoint on other nodes' routes while contributing nothing to any answer. Traversals spend hops on the dead, the surviving edges describe a corpus that no longer exists, and the quality of the walk erodes in proportion to how much of the index is now buried. There is no error to catch and no metric that jumps; there are two cures, and both cost machine time: a periodic full rebuild, or the segment merge — compaction — that sweeps the tombstones out along the way and performs that rebuild piecewise. In most implementations you need one or the other, and on a high-churn corpus it is a standing tax.

The partitioned and compressed lanes age in their own way, and the mechanism is the same in both: a structure fitted to a past distribution keeps being applied to a present one. The IVF centroids were trained on a sample of the corpus as it stood; the coarse-quantizer beat's whole promise — that probing the nearest few cells is enough because neighbours share a cell — rests on those centroids sitting where the data is dense. Feed the index a stream of fresh documents about a topic nobody indexed before, and they pile into whichever cell happens to be closest, or split along a boundary that was drawn without them. Cells go lopsided, the fat cell slows every query that probes it, and the recall the sweeps promised quietly slips — exactly the strain the IVF-bounds beat described, arriving now not from a bad choice of parameters but from the passage of time. The PQ codebook ages identically: its centroids were fitted, subspace by subspace, to the vectors available when it was trained. New vectors landing in regions the codebook never learned are reconstructed by a distant centroid, the residual grows, and the approximate distance that the ADC table computes drifts away from the true one. Neither failure announces itself.

So an update path really runs on two clocks. The fast clock is the write itself — insert, tombstone, visible in moments. The slow clock is the maintenance the write incurred: rebuilding the graph, re-training the centroids and the codebook on a fresh sample, and re-running the recall-versus-work sweeps afterwards, because the old knob settings were tuned against the old structure. Engines hide the slow clock inside words like segment and compaction: writes land in a small fresh segment that is often searched exactly, tombstones accumulate there, and a background job later merges segments and drops the dead — which is a rebuild wearing a friendlier name and consuming real machine time. This is the part of the iceberg that the demo never shows, and it is why an honest capacity plan budgets not only for serving the index but for continually rebuilding it.

Don't pick an engine by its benchmark

Here is the tempting rule: run the benchmark, compare throughput at a fixed recall, take the winner. It feels admirably quantitative, and it measures the wrong thing. The families all run the same structures — the same graph, the same cells, the same codes — so at equal recall much of the throughput gap is a question of build parameters, of where the knobs were left, of the hardware, of batching, of how the vectors were loaded, and of who ran the test, which is usually the vendor on a corpus chosen to flatter it. Part of the gap is real, though, and it is not the mathematics either: implementation quality — vector instructions on the CPU, how the data is laid out in memory, how well the engine uses all its cores, whether it supports quantized distance at all. Those differences are genuine, they survive at equal recall, and no amount of knob-turning closes them; they tell you how much hardware you will buy, not which engine your product needs. And a number produced on somebody else's data, somebody else's embedding and no filters at all is still an answer to a question you never asked. Goodhart the Trickster is standing right beside that leaderboard: make throughput the target and you will get an engine tuned to win throughput contests.

What actually decides is operational, and every question on the list has a wrong answer that hurts for years. Filtering: do your queries carry predicates, and how selective are they? The previous beat showed that filtered recall is a property of your predicates — an engine that shines unfiltered can crumble on the filters your product issues. Churn: how fast does the corpus change, and can you afford the rebuild rhythm the previous beat priced? A high-churn corpus makes the maintenance path, not the query path, the thing you are really buying. Consistency with the system of record: if a document deleted from your primary database must vanish from search in the same breath, then keeping vectors inside that database — pgvector next to the rows — beats any separate service, because a single PostgreSQL transaction covers both. That argument is about PostgreSQL and nothing else: the vector search inside Elasticsearch and OpenSearch is an extension of the same kind, but those stores have no multi-document transaction to enrol your write in, so you are back to living with a window of disagreement. And take the caveat of the previous beat with the recommendation: filtering beside the rows is the thing pgvector does last rather than during, so budget a partial index for the predicates you actually issue. A separate service, meanwhile, hands you a synchronisation path to write, monitor and debug forever. Who operates it: a managed service buys down the ops load with money, a self-hosted engine buys control with somebody's on-call nights, and there is no third option in which the work disappears.

So invert the procedure. Write the operational requirements down first, strike out every engine that cannot meet them, and only then measure the survivors — on your corpus, your embeddings, your filters, your latency target, with the knobs of each candidate tuned as carefully as the vendor tuned its own. The measurement is still mandatory; it is simply a check on a shortlist rather than the rule that produces one. Run it in that order and the shortlist is usually short and the decision unglamorous — which is the point. The lanes were never the hard part of this lecture; keeping them open is.

The latency budget

A production search may set an SLA at p99, the slow tail. Here we use an illustrative single-request budget, not measured component quantiles or an end-to-end p99. Add only the sequential work on the response's critical path.

client→API \(3\) + embed query \(8\) + ANN search over \(10^6\) quantized vectors \(12\) + re-rank top-100 with the cross-encoder \(60\) + response \(4\) \(= \mathbf{87}\) ms, in this assumed configuration, below the \(200\) ms SLA. An exact scan of \(10^6 \times 768\)-d vectors would cost \(\approx 520\) ms for the search hop alone — it blows the budget by itself. A warm cache hit returns in \(\approx 5\) ms.

The async cache/log tail adds work but does not delay this response. Measure end-to-end p99 separately: component p99 values do not add. ANN, compression, caching and a bounded reranking stage are design levers. Whether exact search fits the SLA depends on hardware, batching, concurrency and corpus size; the illustrative timings are not a benchmark.

The warm path and an illustrative search budget

Cache hit, illustrative path. client→API \(3\) + cache lookup \(1\) + response \(1\) \(=\mathbf{5}\) ms. The cold critical path is \(87\) ms, about \(17\times\) as long. The cache skips encoding, ANN and reranking; its value depends on hit rate, freshness rules and memory cost.

Assumed search-hop comparison. On the illustrative \(10^6\times768\)-d corpus, suppose exact search takes \(520\) ms and ANN takes \(12\) ms: \(520/12\approx43\times\). The exact hop alone exceeds the \(200\) ms budget in this scenario; the ANN request path totals \(87\) ms. These are representative assumptions, not measured hardware results or universal limits.

Benchmark both alternatives on the target workload before deciding. Exact search may fit on a smaller corpus or different hardware; ANN trades recall for work. A single illustrative response below the budget does not prove an SLA: measure the full latency distribution under load, including queueing and cold-cache behavior.

The Iceberg returns

A working notebook is a trap. The Iceberg returns (Sculley et al., from Lecture 1): the demo that finds neighbours on your laptop is the easy 10% above the waterline. The 90% below it is everything that keeps the lanes open in production — serving under the SLA, monitoring recall and latency, rebuilding the index as documents are added and deleted, re-training the IVF centroids and the PQ codebook as the data drifts, and the cost of the RAM that holds it all.

"The demo worked, so we're done" is the most expensive sentence in search infrastructure. The ANN structures are the easy part; the operations around them are the lecture's real warning.

So carry an ops checklist, because every lane in this lecture left a chore below the waterline. Index lifecycle: schedule periodic full rebuilds (HNSW tombstones accumulate; IVF cells drift), and re-train the IVF centroids and PQ codebooks on a fresh data sample whenever the distribution moves. Monitoring: track recall on a held-out gold set and p99 latency over time — recall erodes silently under drift, and cell imbalance fattens the tail without throwing an error. Capacity: budget the RAM the index actually needs (the PQ memory ledger is the starting point) plus headroom for the graph overhead and the cache. Tuning: keep the recall-vs-work sweeps (\(efSearch\), \(nprobe\)) reproducible so you can re-tune after a rebuild. Correctness: assert the metric matches the encoder's training objective, and rescore the PQ index's retrieved candidates with full-precision vectors when exact reranking is required. None of this shows up in the notebook; all of it shows up in the on-call rotation.

The lanes are open

The lanes are open. A graph to walk, cells to probe, a codebook to fold the maps small — and a serving path that delivers any record in tens of milliseconds, forever. The Ship can now reach any star in the galaxy of information almost instantly.

But reaching a record is not the same as answering a question. Finding the passage about the cardiac cycle is not the same as telling the captain how the heart pumps blood. Next the Ship learns to speak: it stops merely finding and starts answering, grounding its words in what the lanes brought back. That is The Oracle — and the spine gains its fourth and final leg, Generate.
