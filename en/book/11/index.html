The Crucible of Negatives — hard negative mining in contrastive dense retrieval

Open slides →

← 10 12 / 22 12 →

Same code, a different blade

In the last chapter you built the Scout: a bi-encoder that turns a query and a passage into two vectors and learns by pulling the right passage close and pushing the wrong ones away. We took the wrong ones from whatever happened to be in the batch, and moved on. That choice was never innocent. Two teams clone the same dense-retriever code, train on the same data with the same architecture. One ends up finding the right passage almost every time; the other barely beats keyword search. The only thing they chose differently was the negatives — the wrong answers each query is taught to push away.

The team that built one of the first strong dense retrievers said it plainly: how you select negative examples is often overlooked, but can be decisive. This chapter is that sentence, unfolded.

Picture the retriever as a blade and training as a forge. You cannot sharpen a blade against air — it is the opponents it spars against that give it an edge. Choose them badly and the edge never forms; choose them worse and it dulls.

Why opponents at all?

A dense retriever maps a query and its true passage to nearby points and everything else far away. Training pulls the query toward its positive \(d^+\) and pushes it away from negatives \(d^-\).

Drop the push and the model cheats: it can shrink the whole space to a single point, where every passage is equally close and the loss looks great. Negatives are the only force stopping that collapse. They are not a detail of training — they are the training signal that gives the space its shape.

One stop lit, and two neighbours that look closer than they are

Put the forge on the map before we light it, because this hour lights exactly one stop of the four — Rank — and both of its neighbours will spend the chapter tapping you on the shoulder. All hour you will be training an encoder, and training an encoder sounds like Get Data. All hour you will be computing scores, and computing scores sounds like Measure. Neither is what this hour hands the Ship.

Get Data stays dark, and the test is mechanical: name one thing about the Archives that the choice of opponents moves. The tokenizer is the same. The architecture is the same. The width of the vector, the format of the index, the way a passage becomes a point — all the same, before and after the crucible. Feed the two blades from the opening the same corpus and the same shapes land in the same store. What differs is which passage ends up above which when a query arrives, and a statement about above-and-below is a statement about order.

Measure stays dark, and this test is the sharper one, because the chapter is thick with scoring. The loss, the temperature, the band of hardness you are about to learn to aim at — every one of those numbers lives inside the loop that shapes the model, and none of them is ever reported about a finished system. When we ask at the end whether the forging worked, we borrow the scoreboard L5 built and change not one line of it. Keep that as the rule of reading: a number the system reports about itself, to you belongs to Measure; a number that exists only inside training belongs to whatever the training is for. Here it is for the order the Scout brings back — so one stop, and the whole hour lands on it.

Pick the positive out of N

The contrastive loss — InfoNCE — turns retrieval into a multiple-choice question: among the positive and a batch of negatives, score each by similarity, take a softmax at temperature \(\tau\), and ask the model to put all its probability on the positive. The loss is \(-\log P^+\).

Read it once and the whole field clicks into place: better negatives make the multiple-choice question harder and more informative, and the temperature \(\tau\) controls how sharply the model is forced to choose.

Score the query q against its true match d⁺ and the five candidate negatives n₁..n₅. The bars are cos(q,·): the lineup runs from an easy random negative up to the hardest near-miss.

A softmax over sim/τ is the InfoNCE target — it should put its mass on d⁺. The loss is L = −log P⁺. At τ=0.2 the mass is still spread across the hard negatives.

Halve the temperature to τ=0.1 and the softmax sharpens in the MIDDLE — P⁺ climbs further and the loss drops again, the easy negatives fading toward zero. The climb is monotone: lower τ, sharper target.

Drop the temperature toward zero (τ=0.05) and the softmax SHARPENS hardest — P⁺ climbs and the loss falls, but the gradient now piles onto the single hardest negative. Temperature is the hardness amplifier.

Temperature and the ceiling

Lowering \(\tau\) sharpens the softmax: the probability piles onto whichever candidate has the highest cosine — so the loss falls when the positive is already first, and rises when a negative is. Temperature is the hardness amplifier — it decides how much a near-miss dominates the gradient.

There is a common myth that more negatives let InfoNCE maximise mutual information. The honest statement is narrower: here \(N\) is the number of candidates the softmax chooses among — one positive plus \(N-1\) negatives — and more negatives raise the ceiling \(\log N\) that the bound \(I \ge \log N - L_N\) can reach, which the bound only saturates. Because it is a lower bound, InfoNCE can certify at most \(\log N\) nats of MI no matter how good the encoder is (Poole et al. 2019) — so for high-MI tasks the bound stays loose and large batches are a hard requirement, not a nicety. That ceiling, not a magic maximisation, is why retrievers scale the negative count.

Temperature is mining you already own

Temperature is usually introduced as a knob that sharpens the softmax, which is true and tells you nothing about how violently it sharpens. Take two negatives and divide their weights: everything cancels except one exponential, \(w_i/w_j = e^{(\cos(q,d_i^-)-\cos(q,d_j^-))/\tau}\). One opponent's share of the push over another's depends on nothing but the gap between their cosines — and that gap is divided by \(\tau\) before it is exponentiated. Halve the temperature and the ratio is squared.

So \(\tau\) does not measure hardness; it selects on it. A lineup whose cosines sit a finger apart behaves like a crowd of equals at a large \(\tau\) and like a single opponent at a small one, without a single passage changing. Read that as a strategy and lowering the temperature is mining you already own: it re-spends the gradient budget on the hardest candidates in the batch, for free, with no index and no extra pass. Read the limit and you see what it cannot do — a temperature only sharpens the order already in front of it, and can never bring into the batch a passage that is not there. Mining changes the set; \(\tau\) changes the weights over the set.

Which is why the two knobs must not be turned as if they were independent. Mine aggressively and the cosines at the head of the lineup crowd together; drop \(\tau\) as well and an exponential of a divided gap hands almost the entire step to one candidate. That is the setting in which a single mistaken opponent owns the update — the failure the coming beats are about, reached not through a bad passage but through an unlucky pair of settings.

A bigger batch is not a harder batch

Two claims about \(N\) now stand side by side, and they are about different things. One is the ceiling: the bound certifies at most \(\log N\), so more candidates raise what it is allowed to say. The other is hardness: with more candidates, the hardest one in the batch is drawn from a larger sample. The first is arithmetic and always holds. The second is a statement about sampling, and it holds only under a condition that ablation tables rarely print.

The condition is that the extra candidates are new. Draw more passages from the same broad corpus and the hardest of them creeps up slowly, because hardness lives in the thin tail of the similarity distribution and each doubling of the sample buys one more step into a tail that is thin by definition. Fill the batch with duplicates and near-clones and you buy nothing whatever: a second copy of a passage already present adds mass to the softmax in a direction the first copy already covered, so the push it produces is the push you already had, counted twice. Count is not difficulty.

Which is why the honest way to buy hardness is to change the distribution rather than the count — precisely what the topic-clustered batch does later in this chapter: the same batch size, drawn from one neighbourhood instead of the whole corpus, and every in-batch negative arrives on topic. Keep the two claims apart when you read someone's numbers. A bigger batch always raises the ceiling. It raises the difficulty only if what you added differs from what you had.

Hardness is an angle

What makes a negative hard? Geometrically, it is one that sits close to the query in angle — a high \(\cos(q, d^-)\). An easy negative is far away and already separated; a hard one crowds the query and is easy to confuse with the right answer.

Sir Cosine returns to measure it. Lay the query, its positive, and the negatives on a unit arc by their angle, and hardness becomes something you can see.

Place the query q on a unit arc and drop its positive d⁺ and the five negatives by their angle to q — arccos cos(q,·). A small angle means a negative crowding the query: a hard one.

Three bands fall out of the angle: easy (far, wide angle), semi-hard, and hard (crowding the query). Easy negatives are already separated — they give almost no gradient.

The second channel: n₅ sits deep in the HARD band — yet it is an unlabelled positive, a paraphrase of the answer. Hardness alone cannot see it; that danger is a label property, not an angle.

The gradient weight on each negative is a Boltzmann softmax of sim/τ (Wang–Liu). At τ=0.2 the hard negatives n₄, n₅ already take most of the push — a negative is worth exactly its gradient.

Halve the temperature to τ=0.1 and the weight marches further toward the hardest — n₅'s share climbs while the semi-hard negatives lose their push. The collapse onto the nearest neighbour is gradual, not a switch.

A negative is worth its gradient

Here is the lens that unifies the whole chapter. The push each negative exerts is a Boltzmann weight — a softmax of its similarity divided by \(\tau\). An easy negative gets almost no weight, so it contributes almost no gradient: training it is wasted effort.

As \(\tau\) shrinks toward zero, the weight collapses onto the single hardest negative — a nearest-neighbour hard-max. A negative is worth exactly its gradient, and every mining strategy in this chapter is, at heart, a different way to spend the gradient budget on negatives that are worth it.

The push is one budget, and it always sums to one

The weights are a softmax, so whatever the batch happens to hold, they sum to one. That single fact turns loose talk about negatives into arithmetic: a negative does not have a gradient, it has a share of a fixed one. Adding an opponent to the lineup can only take weight away from the opponents already standing there.

Two consequences follow, and they point opposite ways. Easy negatives are harmless filler — their weight is near zero, so a great many of them dilute the hard ones by almost nothing, which is precisely why a massive in-batch stage can be piled on without blunting the edge. A second equally hard negative is the opposite case: it halves the share the first one used to hold. Easiness is free to add; hardness is rivalrous.

That is the arithmetic under a result this chapter reported and did not explain: one mined hard negative bought a large jump, and a second bought nothing further. It was never that the second negative was bad. It was that the first had already claimed the budget, and now the two of them split what one of them used to own. So ask of a mining strategy not how many negatives it produces, but how the softmax will divide the pouch between them.

The hardest can break the blade

If a negative is worth its gradient, why not always train on the very hardest? Because the early work on triplet losses found the opposite: feeding a model only the hardest negatives can collapse training — the gradients become huge and unstable, and the model folds. FaceNet warned of exactly this, so it sampled in a semi-hard band from the outset, hard enough to teach but not so hard it destabilises.

Keep this in mind — it is an optimisation failure of a genuine negative. The next act meets a darker failure, where the hardest opponent is not a negative at all.

Why the hard-max is a badly posed problem

«Feeding only the hardest collapses training» is a warning; here is the mechanism. Push the temperature toward zero and the sum inside the loss collapses onto its largest term: the objective stops being an average over the negatives and becomes a maximum over them. That is a different kind of function, and the trouble is not that it is harsh — it is that it is not smooth. The candidate holding the maximum changes from step to step, and when it changes the gradient turns to face an entirely different passage. Consecutive updates stop being small corrections of one another, and a step size tuned for a curve that bends is being applied to one that jumps.

The second defect is that an average cancels noise and a maximum does not. When the push is spread across many opponents, one mislabelled or badly-encoded passage contributes a sliver of the step and the rest of the batch outvotes it. When the push sits on one opponent, whatever error that passage carries becomes the step. The model is then being taught by a single example chosen precisely because it is extreme — the worst possible sampling rule if extremes are where your data is dirtiest, which in a mined corpus they are.

So read the semi-hard band as a repair rather than a compromise about difficulty. Sampling inside a band keeps the expected hardness high — the candidates still crowd the query — while keeping the number of genuine contributors above one, so the step is an average again and noise cancels again. The early face-recognition work paid for this discovery and named the band; every later method in this chapter is another answer to the same question, which is how to stay hard without letting one candidate own the update.

The search for good opponents

From here the chapter is a history. For fifteen years the field has been asking one question in better and better ways: where do good negatives come from? Each answer fixes a specific flaw in the one before it.

Walk the path with Séréga: random, in-batch, BM25-static, model-mined, denoised, distilled, and the modern two-stage recipe. Each anvil on the path is a step toward a hard-but-honest opponent.

Random, in-batch, BM25

The first rungs are cheap. Random negatives are almost always easy — they give little gradient. In-batch negatives reuse the other queries' positives already in the batch for free, so a bigger batch quietly buys more (and slightly harder) negatives. BM25 negatives add lexically-similar passages that look right but aren't.

But all three are static: BM25 is lexical and model-independent, so it soon stops matching what the dense model actually confuses; in-batch negatives are whatever happened to share the batch. None of them adapt to the model as it learns.

Even static, the rungs combine: DPR's strongest published configuration is in-batch negatives plus one BM25 hard negative. On Natural Questions its top-20 climbs from \(69.1\) (in-batch alone) to \(78.0\) — and a second BM25 negative buys nothing more (Karpukhin et al., EMNLP 2020). One well-chosen hard negative is worth more than a pile of easy ones.

What a lexical miner cannot see

«Model-independent» is the standard complaint against BM25 negatives, and it is worth turning into a mechanism, because the mechanism explains the weakness and the value at once. BM25 orders the corpus by shared terms. The dense space, as the previous chapter built it, exists to bridge the vocabulary gap — to bring together passages that answer the same question in different words. So the pairs a dense model confuses are, by construction, largely the ones with little word overlap; and those are exactly the passages a lexical scorer ranks nowhere near the top.

The disagreement is therefore not a quality gap that a better lexical scorer would close. Two different functions, ordering the same corpus by two different notions of similarity, put different passages on top, and the overlap between what they mine is a statement about the functions rather than about how well either is tuned. It also explains the drift: as training proceeds the dense model's confusions move, and BM25's do not move at all, because BM25 never saw the model and has nothing to move with.

Now the value, which the same argument hands over for free. A passage lexically near the query and semantically wrong is hard for a reason the model has not yet learned to handle — it is the shortcut of matching on words, held up as a trap. Pushing it away teaches something the model's own confusions cannot teach it, and it costs one offline retrieval over an index you already have. One such opponent is worth a great deal; a pile of them is not, for exactly the reason the budget beat gave.

Why in-batch negatives are free — and what gradient caching buys

Say a batch holds \(B\) queries with their positives. Encoding costs \(2B\) passes through a transformer — the expensive part, and linear in \(B\). The contrast then wants a similarity for every query against every positive, \(B^{2}\) of them, but each is a scalar product of two vectors already sitting in memory. Quadratically many, each so cheap beside an encoder pass that the entire grid disappears into the noise. That is the whole sense in which in-batch negatives are free: you pay for the encodings, and the opponents fall out of the arithmetic.

Then why can nobody simply set \(B\) large? Because backpropagation does not need the vectors, it needs the activations that produced them, and those grow with \(B\) until the device refuses. Gradient caching goes around it, and its trick is that the loss touches the batch only through the embeddings. Encode in chunks while keeping no graph; compute the loss and the gradient with respect to the embeddings — cheap, since only the vectors are involved — and store it; then re-encode chunk by chunk, this time with the graph, and backpropagate each chunk against its stored gradient. Peak memory becomes the size of one chunk, and the price is a second forward pass.

That exchange is what made stage one of the modern recipe possible at all: batch size stopped being a property of the hardware and became a quantity you buy with compute. Notice what it does not buy. The extra candidates are still drawn from the same corpus, so the ceiling rises while the hardness barely moves — exactly what the earlier caveat said. Uniformity is what the huge batch is for; the edge still has to be mined.

Let the model pick its opponents

The turn that changed dense retrieval: stop guessing what is hard, and let the model mine its own. Index the whole corpus with the current model, retrieve each query's nearest non-positives, and train against those. These are the negatives the model actually confuses — and they keep up with it as it improves.

This single idea — approximate-nearest-neighbour negative mining — lifted dense retrievers past strong lexical baselines and made dynamic hard negatives the centre of the craft.

One statistic makes the why concrete. Take the negatives each strategy mines and ask: what fraction are the model's actual hardest? In-batch negatives overlap the true-hard set by 0%, BM25's lexical negatives by about 15%, and ANCE's self-mined negatives by 100% — by construction they are what the model confuses. That overlap is the whole win, and the scores follow: ANCE reaches MS MARCO MRR@10 0.33 and NQ top-20 81.9, refreshing its index every ~10k batches so the negatives keep up.

The sparring ghosts

Mining from the model's own index has a catch: re-indexing millions of passages is slow, so the index is refreshed only now and then. Between refreshes the model keeps improving while its negatives are frozen — they drift from hard to easy, like sparring ghosts still shadow-boxing a position the fighter has long left.

Later work closed the gap by going per-step: freeze the document side, train the query encoder, and the negatives stay genuinely current — sharper opponents, and far cheaper than re-indexing the world each round.

Two ways for a negative to be wrong

Staleness and the impostor are both cases of a negative not being what the trainer thinks it is, and it is worth standing them side by side before the next act, because they fail in opposite directions. A stale negative has drifted toward easy: the model moved on, and the frozen passage no longer crowds the query. An impostor has drifted nowhere at all — it is exactly as hard as it looks, and simply carries the wrong sign.

Now read each through the budget. The stale negative's error makes its own weight small, so the mistake removes itself from the step: what you lose is the compute you spent producing it, and the cost is money. The impostor's error makes its weight large — it sits near the query precisely because it answers it — so the mistake claims the biggest share of the push and spends it dragging a correct answer away. One failure is self-limiting; the other is self-amplifying, because the better the retriever gets, the higher it ranks the unlabelled positive and the more of the budget the impostor takes.

That is also the reading order of the field. The cheap failure was found and fixed first, with fresher indexes and per-step mining — and every one of those fixes made the expensive failure worse, because each of them mined harder. The next act is what happens when the whole gradient budget lands on an ally.

What if the opponent is a friend?

Mining the hardest negatives surfaces a quiet disaster. Real datasets label barely one correct passage per query, against a corpus of millions — yet many other passages are also relevant, just never labelled. When the miner reaches for the hardest candidates, it scoops up these unlabelled positives and hands them over as negatives.

This is the Impostor: an ally wearing an enemy's mask. Push it away and you train the model to reject a correct answer — and, as the next beats show, you drag the true positive down with it.

The density is measured, not hypothetical. RocketQA found that on MS MARCO about 70% of BM25's top-retrieved passages are actually relevant — yet only ~1.1 positives are labelled per query over an 8.8M-passage collection. So a miner that simply grabs BM25's hardest non-positives is, most of the time, grabbing a true answer in disguise. Naive BM25 hard negatives poison training until they are denoised (Qu et al., NAACL 2021).

Two axes, not one

The impostor exposes a confusion the field carried for years. Hardness is one axis — \(\cos(q, d^-)\), how close a candidate sits to the query. But danger lives on a second, related diagnostic axis — \(\cos(d^-, d^+)\), how close it sits to the positive.

A worthy hard negative is close to the query and far from the positive: push it freely. An impostor is close to the query and close to the positive: push it and the positive comes along. From a query-only view the two look identical — same hardness, opposite truth. You cannot tell them apart without the second axis, or a label.

Related signals, biased samples

The two coordinates answer different questions, but they need not be orthogonal or statistically independent. Both compare the same candidate with nearby vectors. If the query and positive have identical unit vectors, the two cosines are identical. More generally, fixing their mutual similarity constrains the candidate's possible cosine pairs before any mining takes place.

The miner then changes the joint distribution further: it selects candidates with high query similarity. When the positive also lies near the query, this can enrich the sample with passages near the positive. The strength of the dependence comes from both embedding geometry and sampling; the word 'two' never promised independence.

Measure both signals when they help diagnose the sample, but do not mistake proximity to the positive for a relevance label. A near duplicate with a changed entity or negation can still be a true negative. Validate a denoising threshold against labels or a trusted evaluator, and track false rejections as well as false negatives that survive.

Random negatives pull n₁ from far away — easy, almost no gradient. Recall sits at the baseline; the model barely learns a boundary from negatives this loose.

In-batch negatives reuse other queries' positives (n₂) for free — a touch harder, a touch better. This is the baseline every later strategy is measured against.

BM25-static hard negatives (n₃) are lexically close but FROZEN — model-independent, so they soon stop matching what the dense model actually confuses. Recall barely moves off the baseline. Note the plateau: random, in-batch and BM25-static all cluster within a hair of each other — a deliberate flat run before the dynamic miner breaks it.

The dynamic miner reaches for the model's own hardest near-misses — n₄ AND the impostor n₅. With no denoising it pushes n₅ (an unlabelled positive) away, dragging d⁺ down: recall DROPS below in-batch. This is the RocketQA inversion.

Add a denoiser: a cross-encoder filters the suspected positive n₅ out of the mined set, keeping the worthy-but-true n₄. Recall recovers — and now clears every other strategy.

Both n₄ and n₅ are equally hard to the query — the miner grabs both. But on the cos(·,d⁺) axis they split: n₄ is far from the positive and safe to push; n₅ sits right in d⁺'s neighbourhood — it is an unlabelled positive.

Mine both as negatives with no filter and push them away. Because n₅ lives right next to d⁺, pushing n₅ drags the true positive down with it — recall falls BELOW the in-batch baseline. This is the inversion.

Note what the miner could NOT see: on the hardness axis n₄ and n₅ are twins. The damage lives entirely on cos(·,d⁺) — a label property. A query-only view of 'hardness' is blind to the impostor.

Add a cross-encoder denoiser: it scores the mined candidates and strikes the suspected positive n₅, keeping the worthy-but-true n₄. Recall recovers — above in-batch and every other strategy.

Three cures

Once you see the impostor, three families of cure appear. Filter it out: score each mined candidate with a strong cross-encoder and drop the ones that look too much like a positive — the move that first turned the inversion back into a gain. Sample around it: prefer ambiguous negatives near a sweet spot rather than always the very hardest. Co-train against it: pair the retriever with a noise-robust ranker that learns to discount likely false negatives.

All three accept the same truth: at equal hardness, only something that knows about relevance — a filter, a teacher, a label — can tell a worthy negative from an impostor.

The denoiser is a classifier too, and it has two errors

A filter with a threshold is a classifier, and no classifier has only one error. Set the threshold permissively and impostors survive the sieve — the failure the cure was bought to fix. Set it aggressively and it removes the candidates that most resemble the positive, and by the second axis those include the genuinely hard true negatives: the very ones sitting closest to the boundary you are trying to sharpen.

The budget makes the second error costlier than it looks. The candidates a strict filter discards are the ones holding most of the softmax weight, so what remains is not «the same set, slightly smaller» — it is a set whose push is now dominated by the middle of the band. Both errors surface as a weaker retriever, which means a curve of recall against threshold cannot tell you which error you are making. Only looking at what was thrown away can.

And the teacher is not an oracle either. It was trained on the same sparse labels that created the impostor, so where it is wrong it tends to be wrong about the same passages the miner is wrong about — the two sets of errors are not independent, and they do not average out the way independent noise would. None of this argues against denoising, which the measurements settle. It argues for reading a cure as a trade with two prices, and for reporting the threshold as loudly as the recall it produced.

The teacher unmasks the impostor

The duel now becomes tutelage. Instead of a blunt right/wrong label, a strong teacher — a cross-encoder that reads the query and passage together — grades each pair with a soft margin: how much better the positive is than the negative. The student learns to match that margin.

This is gentle with impostors by design. When a mined negative is really an unlabelled positive, the teacher gives it a high score, so the target margin shrinks toward zero or flips sign — and the student is no longer told to shove a correct answer away. The teacher quietly unmasks the impostor.

The loss itself: copy the margin, not the label

The teacher's grade is not a label, and it is time to write down what it actually is. For a triple — query, positive, mined negative — a model's margin is the difference between the two scores it hands out, \(m(q,d^+,d^-)=s(q,d^+)-s(q,d^-)\), where \(s\) is a pair's score. The student is the bi-encoder, the teacher the cross-encoder that reads each pair whole, and the objective — Margin-MSE — is the squared difference between the two margins, \(\mathcal{L}=\big(m_\theta-m_T\big)^{2}\). Nothing else is penalised: not the positive's absolute score, not the negative's, only the gap between them.

Read that once and the impostor's amnesty falls out as arithmetic rather than mercy. When the mined negative is really an unlabelled positive, the teacher scores both passages alike and \(m_T\) sits near zero — so the student's target is near zero, and what gets punished now is separating that pair at all. A genuinely worthy negative keeps a wide teacher margin and is pushed exactly as hard as before. Nothing was thrown out of the batch; the target did the disarming.

Set it beside InfoNCE and you can see what the contrastive objective never had. InfoNCE knows a direction but not a degree: the positive must win, so every negative is shoved away, and the harder it is the harder the shove. Margin-MSE swaps the direction for a number to aim at, and overshooting is penalised the same as falling short. Relevance finally entered training as a single quantity — somebody else's margin — which is also why the modern recipe can afford to mine greedily.

Manufacture hard negatives for free

There is a cheaper way to get hard negatives than mining a giant index every few thousand steps: manufacture them. Cluster the queries by topic first, then build each batch from a single topic. Now the in-batch negatives are all on-topic — hard for free, with no index to refresh.

Combined with margin distillation and a balanced sampler, this turns a heavy training pipeline into one that fits on a single modest GPU in under two days — and still generalises across benchmarks. Concretely, TAS-B reaches MS MARCO MRR@10 ≈ 0.34 on a single 11 GB GPU in under 48 h. Be honest about the limit, though: on BEIR zero-shot it still sits about 2.8% below BM25 on average (relative performance in BEIR Table 2, not metric points) — an in-domain win, not an out-of-domain one.

The modern recipe

Today's strongest open embedding models converge on one two-stage shape. Stage one: a massive in-batch contrast — tens of thousands of easy negatives at once — to spread the space out evenly. Stage two: fine-tune with a few mined hard negatives, filtered so no impostor survives.

The huge batch is what a gradient-caching trick makes affordable on real hardware; the filtered hard negatives are what sharpen the boundary. Massive in-batch for the spread, a careful handful of hard-but-honest negatives for the edge — the whole chapter, in one line.

Why two stages, not one

The recipe reads like two good ideas glued together, and it is not — it is one sum, sampled twice. Split the contrastive loss into its halves: \( \mathcal{L}=\underbrace{-\cos(q,d^{+})/\tau}_{A}+\underbrace{\log\sum_{c}e^{\cos(q,c)/\tau}}_{U} \), where the sum inside \(U\) runs over the positive and every negative in the batch. The alignment term \(A\) pulls the positive in and does not care how many negatives are standing around; only \(U\), the repulsion term, ever sees them. So every question about which negatives is a question about one log-sum-exp.

And that log-sum-exp is an estimate — of how densely the corpus crowds around this query. Feed it tens of thousands of cheap negatives from inside the batch and the estimate is broad and nearly unbiased, a fair sample of the whole corpus; the push it produces is spread across the sphere, and that spreading is what uniformity means, not a slogan about geometry. Feed it a handful of mined hard negatives and it does the opposite: the softmax hands almost all of its weight to the nearest candidates, so those few own the direction of the push and decide where the boundary falls. Broad sample, even heat; sharp sample, one edge.

Which finally answers the question the recipe leaves hanging — why the hard set stays a handful. Bias the sample far enough toward the edge and \(U\) stops estimating the corpus at all; it estimates the neighbourhood of the boundary, and the model tidies that neighbourhood beautifully while the rest of the space goes untended. The impostor is the very same defect with the sign of the error flipped: there the sample was biased toward things that should have been pulled in, here toward a region too narrow to be the corpus. One sum, two samplers, and every choice in this chapter is a choice about the sample.

Which rung is yours

The ladder is a history, and a history is easy to misread as a ranking with the newest rung on top. Resist that. Every rung is still running in production somewhere, because the flaw each one fixes is not always the flaw you have. Two budgets decide which rung is yours, and neither of them is «how modern would you like to be».

The first budget is labels. Ask how completely relevance is marked in your data. If a query's correct answers are all annotated, the top of any ranking holds few unlabelled positives, the impostor is rare, and greedy mining is safe as well as cheap. If one answer is marked per query against a corpus of millions, the top of every ranking is mostly correct answers nobody wrote down, and mining harder without a relevance signal will invert your recall exactly as it did in the ablation. The label budget decides whether you may mine greedily; the mining strategy does not get to decide it for you.

The second budget is compute. An index refresh, an extra cross-encoder pass over the mined candidates, a batch large enough to need gradient caching — each is a real bill. Where that bill binds, the move is to manufacture hardness rather than mine it, by drawing each batch from one topic. And where neither budget binds, the two-stage recipe is not a fashion: it is simply what you get when both constraints are lifted and you can afford uniformity and a clean edge at the same time.

An honest toy

The widgets in this chapter run on a small synthetic corpus we train ourselves, reproducibly. Be honest about what it shows. The corpus is constructed to be dense in unlabelled positives, so the impostor's damage is demonstrable by design, not discovered by luck.

What the toy earns is the direction, not the magnitude: the recall ladder, the drop when hard negatives go undenoised, the recovery when they are filtered, and the two-axis split — all reproduce across many random seeds. The real-world numbers belong to the papers; the shape of the story is what you can rerun yourself.

Forged by worthy opponents

A dense retriever is forged by the opponents it trains against. Easy negatives teach nothing; the hardest are often impostors that corrupt the edge; effectiveness lives in a narrow hard-but-honest band — and the field's whole history is the search for it.

Séréga raises a keen, true-edged blade. But one blade is still one army, and the lexical host keeps winning battles the dense one loses — exact names, rare codes, a misspelt word nobody paraphrased. The next chapter, The Alliance, marches the two under one banner: late interaction, learned sparse, hybrid fusion, and a captain trained to order the ranking. (The space this blade lives in has troubles of its own — a narrow cone, unwanted hubs — and The Curved Map comes back for them later.)
