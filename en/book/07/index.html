The Council of Attention

I'm Serega, and last chapter we drew the Map of Meaning — gave every word coordinates, watched "couch" and "sofa" drift into the same neighbourhood, and cornered the Lexical Gremlin at last. But we left the room with one move still on his side of the board, and he knows it. The map gives each word exactly one vector. One point, forever. And some words refuse to hold still.

Take "bank". Say it out loud in two sentences. "I sat on the river bank" — and "I deposited cash at the bank." Same four letters, two genuinely different things, and our static map is forced to cram both into a single dot somewhere in the hopeless middle between "river" and "money", truly close to neither. The word's meaning isn't a fixed property of the word at all. It's decided, every time, by the company in the sentence — by who's sitting next to it at the table.

So picture the sentence as a council. Each word is a councillor with a vote, and before any word fixes its meaning it looks around the table and decides who to heed. "bank" sitting next to "river" leans one way; "bank" sitting next to "deposited" leans the other. The whole chapter is the machinery of that council — a mechanism called attention, the engine called the Transformer, and the training that finally tunes the room for search. The crew has too many voices. Today we learn who to listen to.

One word, two minds

Let's make the Gremlin's last move precise, because the whole chapter is built to defeat exactly this. A static embedding — word2vec, GloVe, every method from last chapter — is a fixed lookup table: one row per word type, learned once, frozen. "bank" maps to a single vector \(v_{\text{bank}}\), end of story. The model trained that vector on every sentence "bank" ever appeared in, the riverbanks and the savings banks all averaged together, so \(v_{\text{bank}}\) ends up at a blurry compromise that's a bad description of both senses. This is polysemy, and a single vector simply cannot hold two meanings at once.

Why does this wreck search specifically? Because retrieval lives or dies on matching intent, and intent is carried by context. A query "river bank erosion" and a document about "central bank interest rates" share the token "bank" — and with a static embedding their "bank" vectors are identical, nudging the two toward a false match. The thing that should disambiguate them — the surrounding words — is exactly the thing a per-word-type vector throws away. "apple" the fruit vs "Apple" the company; "python" the snake vs "python" the language; "jaguar" the cat vs the car: every one of them is a landmine in a search index built on frozen vectors.

So the fix writes itself, even if building it is the hard part. We need an embedding that is not a property of the word, but a function of the word in its sentence — a vector for "bank" that comes out different when "river" is nearby than when "deposited" is. A vector computed fresh, on the spot, from the company the word is actually keeping right now. That is a contextual embedding, and producing one is the job of the machine we spend this chapter building. The static map was a phone book; we're about to make it read the room.

A vector per context

Here's the shift in one sentence: stop asking "what does this word mean?" and start asking "what does this word mean here?" A contextual embedding is a vector produced for a specific occurrence of a word — "bank" in this sentence, at this position — not for the word in the abstract. Feed the model "I sat on the river bank" and it returns a "bank" vector that has drifted toward the water; feed it "I deposited cash at the bank" and the same input token comes back pointing at money. One word type, as many vectors as it has contexts.

It is worth pausing on how strong a claim that is. The static map had a fixed vocabulary of frozen points; the contextual model has no fixed vector for "bank" at all. It has a process that, given a sentence, computes a vector for every token on the fly, each one shaped by all the others. The vocabulary lookup is still there at the very bottom — "bank" still starts as some initial embedding — but that's just the raw clay. The output is sculpted by context before you ever see it.

And it pays off precisely where the Gremlin lives. "couch" and "sofa" in similar sentences now produce nearly identical contextual vectors — synonymy handled. "bank" in two different sentences now produces two well-separated vectors — polysemy handled, the move that static embeddings couldn't make. The only question left is the mechanism: how does a model let every token's vector depend on every other token in the sentence? The answer is the central idea of modern NLP, and it is shockingly simple. It's called attention.

Don't take "well-separated" on faith — put a real number on it. Run three sentences through an actual contextual model (DistilBERT) and read off the vector at the "bank" token in each: a river-bank ("She sat on the grassy bank of the river") against a money-bank ("She deposited her paycheck at the bank downtown") sit at cosine \(0.6465\) — two genuinely different points — while two money-bank uses (the second adds "He waited in line at the bank to cash a cheque") sit at \(0.9466\), nearly on top of each other. That \(0.30\) gap is the model telling meaning apart from spelling. And the contrast is the whole chapter: a static word2vec "bank" is one frozen vector, self-cosine exactly \(1.0000\), so no cross-sense gap is even possible — context here pulls a single starting point into two real places, \(0.6465\) apart, decided entirely by the company each "bank" keeps.

What "knows its sentence" means, exactly

Let's make the difference testable. A static embedding is \(h_i = E[w_i]\): a function of one index — the \(i\)-th token's own identity, and nothing else. A contextual encoder is \(h_i = f_i(x_1,\dots,x_n)\): a map from a whole sequence to a whole sequence. The test that separates them fits on one line — the cross-derivative \(\partial h_i/\partial x_j\) for \(j \neq i\). Nudge the neighbour "river" a little and ask whether the vector sitting on "bank" moves at all.

For a lookup table that derivative isn't small — it is identically zero, by construction. \(h_i\) simply doesn't take \(x_j\) as an argument, so no perturbation of a neighbour can ever reach it. Hence a claim worth saying out loud: no amount of further training turns a static table into a contextual model. Feed it more sentences and you sharpen the same compromise; training cannot create wiring between positions that the object doesn't have. The gap between L6 and this chapter is one of type, not of size — a bigger table is still a table.

That rewrites everything ahead as a single engineering question: how do you build a sequence-to-sequence map whose cross-derivatives are non-zero — cheaply, and for every position at once? A slow answer is easy to invent: read the sentence, then rewrite the vectors one at a time. What you need is one that computes all \(i\) in parallel and learns which neighbours to heed instead of being handed a list. That is attention, and it arrives next.

What context costs at serve time

That comparison table has a row people read last: the price. A static embedding is a table lookup — the vector for a word exists before the sentence does. A contextual vector does not exist at all until you run the model; the price of one embedding here is a full pass through the whole stack of layers. That is not bookkeeping trivia, it is the difference between a table you index into and a machine you run.

First consequence — what can still be paid in advance. A document does not change, so its vectors are computed offline, once, and put on a shelf: that shelf is the index. A query, however, arrives new, and no amount of precomputation helps — every query pays one encoder pass before the search proper has even started. So the depth of the encoder is not only a quality decision, it is a latency decision, and it is billed per query, forever.

Second consequence — what you can no longer cache. In the static world the cache was keyed by word: one vector for bank, reused everywhere. Contextual vectors live on the occurrence, not on the word: the same word in another sentence is another vector, so a word-keyed cache is worthless and only a passage-keyed one ever hits. Hence the quiet change in the unit of the whole pipeline — you index pieces of text, not words — and hence why chunking, later in the course, turns out to be a design decision rather than a question of formatting.

Let each token attend

The attention idea, stripped to its bones: a token's new meaning is a weighted blend of all the tokens in the sentence — including itself. That's it. To compute the contextual vector for "bank", we don't reach for a fixed lookup; we look at every other word in the sentence, decide how relevant each one is to "bank" right now, and mix their vectors together in proportion to that relevance. "river" gets a big weight, "the" gets almost none, and the blend that comes out is "bank"-in-this-sentence.

Go back to the council. Each councillor, before speaking, polls the whole table: how much should I weight what each of you is saying? A word answers that poll with a set of weights — one per token — that are all positive and sum to one, a little probability distribution over "who I'm listening to". Then its new representation is just the weighted average of everyone's contribution under those weights. "bank" next to "river" pours most of its attention onto "river" and "sat", and barely any onto "I" or "the"; the resulting blend leans watery. "bank" next to "deposited" pours its attention elsewhere, and the blend leans monetary. Same machinery, different neighbours, different meaning out.

Two things make this the right idea and not just a nice metaphor. First, it's content-based: the weights come from how well words match, not from fixed positions, so the model decides what's relevant on a case-by-case basis. Second, every token does this in parallel and for itself — forty tokens, forty simultaneous polls of the same table, forty contextual vectors out in one shot. The only thing left to specify is how a word decides how much to heed each neighbour. That calculation — turning a table of words into a table of weights and then into a blend — is scaled dot-product attention, and we'll now walk through it on real numbers, every step.

A blend, not just any sum

Two constraints on the weights do far more work than they look. Write the output as \(h_i=\sum_j \alpha_{ij} v_j\) with \(\alpha_{ij}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j \alpha_{ij}=1\). Non-negativity means a neighbour can only add its own share and never cancel someone else's: attention pulls, it never subtracts. Summing to one means the output's scale doesn't depend on how long the sentence is — a long sentence doesn't hand back a proportionally longer vector. That is why the raw scores are normalised into a distribution instead of being used as they are.

Together they say something sharper: \(h_i\) is a convex combination, so it lands inside the convex hull of the values — the set of all blends with non-negative weights summing to one. Geometrically: the output never points in a direction that no value points in. Attention rearranges what the sentence already contains; it cannot invent a new direction. Two consequences to carry forward. An attention layer on its own adds no non-linearity — which is exactly why the block will later need a feed-forward network. And one thing stays unspecified: where the \(\alpha\) come from. That is the whole of what comes next.

One sentence, ten steps, and not a single number invented along the way. Start where every neural retriever starts: “the cat sat” becomes the ids 0, 1, 2 — three addresses in a vocabulary of five words. Nothing is computed here, and that is worth saying out loud: a lookup is not a computation. The id carries no meaning at all; row 1 is not “more” than row 0, it is simply somewhere else.

Now the ids are used as addresses. E is a 5×4 table — one row per word in the vocabulary — and E[ids] simply PULLS three of those rows out. Row 1 IS the embedding of “cat”: (0, 1, 0, 1). It was copied, not computed. Still no arithmetic has happened in this chain, and the ledger on the right says so: 3 tokens have become a 3×4 matrix, and nothing was multiplied to do it.

The first arithmetic of the whole chain, and it is a single addition. The sinusoidal PE rows are ADDED into the embeddings, element by element: emb[1][1] = 1 plus PE[1][1] = 0.540 gives x[1][1] = 1.540. Position is not concatenated beside the vector — it is folded INTO it, which is why the shape stays 3×4 instead of growing. And look what x has become: no longer a tidy row of ones and zeros, but the messy real input the projections have to cope with.

x meets the first learned matrix. Look at Wq before you look at anything else: 1.771, −1.751, 1.410, −0.828… nothing round, nothing pretty. That is honest — a learned matrix IS ugly — and this one was not even invented: it is SOLVED, so that the Q, K and V it produces are exactly the ones this chapter already prints beside you. Now do one cell by hand. Take the row of x for “sat” and column 1 of Wq: 0.909·(−1.751) + (−0.416)·0.082 + 1.020·0.938 + 1·1.669 = 1. Four hideous products, and out falls a clean integer. The weights are ugly; the OPERATION is clean. K = x·Wk and V = x·Wv come from the SAME x — three views of one input, not three inputs.

The contraction — the operation this whole family draws as a cup and has never once shown. Take row 0 of Q and row 0 of K, multiply them term by term, add the four products: 1·1 + 0·0 + 1·1 + 0·0 = 2, and that is scores[0][0]. Four numbers in, one number out: the axis d has just died. The 3×3 score matrix is nine of these, one for every (query token, key token) pair — and THAT is the axis whose growth is the quadratic cost of attention.

Nine cells, one divisor. Every raw score is divided by √dₖ = √4 = 2: the cell we have been following, 2, becomes 1; the biggest score, 4, becomes 2; the 3 in the corner becomes 1.500. Watch the ledger: the shape does not move, 3×3 in and 3×3 out — a divide changes only the size of the numbers. Without this box the scores grow with d and softmax saturates; ncd-attention deletes the box and watches the gradient die.

The triangle, opened. exp is applied to every scaled score (after subtracting the row's maximum — a shift that cancels in the ratio), the row is summed, and each exponential is divided by that sum. Row 0: the exponentials are (1, 0.368, 1), their sum is 2.368, and 1 ÷ 2.368 = 0.422. Every row now sums to 1. And note what does NOT happen: the shape is unchanged, 3×3 in and 3×3 out. softmax reweighs; it never reshapes.

The second contraction. Row 2 of the attention weights — the row for “sat”, which spends 0.576 of its attention on itself — meets column 0 of V and collapses: 0.212·1 + 0.212·0 + 0.576·2 = 1.364 = ctx[2][0]. The key axis dies at this cup and the shape returns to 3×4 — but those rows are no longer the tokens you put in. Each one is now a MIX of all three, weighted by how much attention that token paid to the others.

Here the axis n dies. The three context vectors — one per token — are averaged into a single point in ℝ⁴. Down column 0 that is (1.267 + 0.579 + 1.364) ÷ 3 = 1.070, and the same for the other three coordinates. This one contraction is what makes a passage indexable: the sentence stops being a sequence and becomes an address. Everything downstream — ANN, HNSW, the entire vector index — exists only because this collapse happened.

The last step, and the index is not a second machine: both documents went through the SAME encoder, just earlier and offline — “cat sat” → (−0.559, 3.653, 1.527, −1.233), “dog ran” → (−0.918, 4.659, 1.736, −2.500). Score them against the query and the relevant document wins: 3.225 against 2.901 on the dot product, 0.407 against 0.271 on the cosine. Now look at how thin that margin is, and do not look away from it. Nobody trained this encoder: its weights were solved to fit three tokens, not learned from a corpus, so it has no idea that “cat sat” is an answer and “dog ran” is not. Pulling relevant pairs together and pushing irrelevant ones apart is exactly what contrastive learning does — and contrastive learning is the second half of this lecture. One more honest note: here the raw dot product and the cosine happen to rank the same way, but the cosine is the one that survives contact with vectors of different lengths.

Each token carries a Value vector. Here we plot those Values as points in a 2-D plane — “the”, “cat” and “sat” each sit somewhere in value space. Attention is about to decide where the query token should land.

The query “cat” asks every token how much it should heed it. Those are its attention weights — drawn here as edges whose thickness is the weight. “cat” leans hardest on itself, then on “sat”, and barely on “the”.

Now follow the weights: the query slides off its own point to the weighted average of all three value points — output = Σⱼ wⱼ·Vⱼ, each value pulled in with its own weight (.665·cat + .245·sat + .09·the). The heavy weights drag the blend toward whom “cat” heeds most; the light ones barely tug. That blended point is where attention places “cat”.

This is not a new picture — it is the same worked example, seen as geometry. The blended point matches the attention output row for “cat” (within rounding). “Attention = a weighted pull toward whom you heed” is exactly what the numbers said.

Read the formula as a set of axes

The formula fits on one line, and that line quietly hides the part that actually breaks in practice. Spell out the indices: \(s_{ij}=\sum_a q_{ia}k_{ja}\), and then \(h_{ia}=\sum_j \alpha_{ij}v_{ja}\). Every letter has a job. \(i\) and \(j\) run over tokens, \(a\) runs over features; the first line contracts \(a\) and keeps both token axes, the second contracts \(j\) and hands the feature axis back. That is the entire operation — which axis gets summed away, and which ones survive. Say it in those words and you have already written the einsum; the diagram of arrows and boxes is the same sentence, drawn.

Two further axes ride along without ever being contracted: the batch and the head. They are spectators — the identical contraction runs independently for every batch element and every head, which is exactly why multi-head attention costs no new mathematics, only a reshaped tensor. And now the failure mode this reading exposes. To the machine, the head axis, the token axis and the feature axis are all just integers, so swapping two of them that happen to have the same length raises no error whatsoever. The shapes still line up, the loss still goes down, the model merely learns something worse. Take the softmax along the wrong axis and you get columns that sum to one instead of rows: a perfectly valid tensor, of exactly the right shape, with completely the wrong meaning. The worst bug is the one that never crashes.

So stop checking shapes and start checking invariants — claims about what an axis means, which a transposition would violate. The weight matrix sums to one along rows, and only rows. Under a causal mask, editing a later token must leave every earlier token's output unchanged to the last digit. Padding a batch must not move the embedding of the real sentence at all. Each of those is one line to test, and each catches a whole class of silent transposition that no shape check will ever see. Carry the habit forward: everything left in this chapter is another contraction over another axis, and the axis you forgot to name is where the bug will live.

The price of listening to everyone

There is a bill at the bottom of that beautiful scroll, and it's worth reading before we go further. Look again at the score matrix: every one of the \(n\) tokens computes a dot product against every one of the \(n\) keys, so the table of scores has \(n \times n\) entries. That means attention's cost — both the compute and the memory to hold that table — grows like \(n^2\), the square of the sequence length. Double the sentence and you quadruple the work; a passage ten times longer costs a hundred times more. This is the famous quadratic bottleneck, and it is the single hardest fact about scaling Transformers.

Put numbers on it so the wall is real. A 512-token passage needs a score table of about \(262{,}000\) entries per head; a 4,000-token document needs \(16\) million; a 32,000-token book chapter needs over a billion, per head, per layer. The model never "runs out of ideas" at long context — it runs out of memory holding all those pairwise scores. That is why early BERT capped inputs at 512 tokens, why long-document search has to chunk passages, and why "context length" is the number every LLM release brags about: it's expensive precisely because of this \(n^2\).

The field has fought back along two fronts, and both are worth knowing. One is to stop computing every pair: sparse attention lets each token attend only to a local window plus a few global tokens (Longformer, BigBird), and linear attention rewrites the math so cost grows like \(n\) instead of \(n^2\) (Performer). The other keeps the exact same softmax but computes it smarter: FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) never materializes the full \(n \times n\) table in slow memory at all — it streams the computation in tiles through fast on-chip memory, giving the identical answer with a fraction of the memory traffic. Same council, same votes — just a cleverer clerk tallying them. For search this matters directly: it's the difference between embedding a tweet and embedding a whole legal contract.

Self-attention: querying yourself

Let's name what we just did. Each token produced three vectors from its own embedding by multiplying it through three learned weight matrices: a query \(q\) ("what am I looking for?"), a key \(k\) ("what do I offer to others?"), and a value \(v\) ("what do I actually contribute if attended to?"). A token's query is dotted against every token's key to score relevance; those scores are scaled and softmaxed into weights; and the weights mix every token's value. Query, key, value — three roles every word plays at once.

The crucial word in this chapter's title is self. In the worked example, \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) all came from the same three tokens — "the", "cat", "sat". Every token queried the same sequence it belongs to, itself included. That's self-attention: a sentence attending to itself, each word rebuilt from a blend of its sentence-mates. (Contrast it with cross-attention, where queries come from one sequence and keys/values from another — that's how a translation decoder peeks at the source sentence; we'll meet it later. Here, source and target are one and the same sequence.)

Notice what self-attention bought us, concretely, in the numbers you just scrolled. The output row for "cat" was not "cat"'s original embedding — it was a new vector, \((0.579,\ 1.996,\ 0.910,\ 0.425)\), built mostly (weight \(0.665\)) from "cat"'s own value but with real contributions from "the" and "sat". "cat" walked into the layer as a context-free embedding and walked out contextualized — its meaning now reflects the sentence it sits in. Do this, and the Gremlin's polysemy trick is broken: "bank" near "river" and "bank" near "money" leave the layer as different vectors, because they attended to different neighbours. One layer of self-attention is the whole engine of context. Stack a few, and you have a Transformer.

One step in that scroll deserves its own explanation, because it looks arbitrary and isn't: why divide the raw scores by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) before the softmax? Here's the statistics. A score is a dot product of two \(d_k\)-dimensional vectors — a sum of \(d_k\) little products — so if the components are roughly unit-scale, that sum's variance grows like \(d_k\) and its standard deviation like \(\sqrt{d_k}\). In our toy example \(d_k = 4\), so \(\sqrt{d_k} = 2.0\) and the scores barely swing. But at a realistic \(d_k = 64\), raw scores spread with std \(\approx \sqrt{64} = 8\) — they routinely swing \(\pm 8\). Feed numbers that large into a softmax and it saturates: one weight rockets to nearly \(1\), the rest collapse to nearly \(0\), and the gradient through that near-one-hot is almost zero. Attention stops learning. Dividing every score by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) rescales the spread back to variance \(\approx 1\), keeping the distribution smooth and trainable.

The cleanest way to hold this is to read \(\sqrt{d_k}\) as a fixed temperature. A softmax with a small divisor runs hot in the wrong sense — peaky, low-entropy, near one-hot, each token heeding essentially one neighbour; a large divisor flattens it — high-entropy, each token spreading its attention thinly over many. \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) is the value that lands the distribution in the sweet middle, sharp enough to be selective, soft enough to pass gradient. And you've met this knob before — it's the same softmax-temperature dial as the \(\tau\) in the InfoNCE contrastive loss later in this chapter: there you tune \(\tau\) to control how hard negatives are pushed; here \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is a fixed temperature that keeps a deep stack of attention layers stable. Same mathematics, two different jobs.

Why the query and the key are two different matrices

Three projections looked like three names for the same idea. They are not, and the algebra says why in one line. Substitute the projections into the score and the two matrices collapse into one object: \(s_{ij}=(x_iW^Q)(x_jW^K)^{\top}=x_i\,W^QW^{K\top}x_j^{\top}=x_i\,M\,x_j^{\top}\). Attention never uses \(W^Q\) or \(W^K\) separately — it only ever uses their product \(M\), a bilinear form that takes two tokens and returns a number. Two facts follow immediately. The head width \(d_k\) is not a size, it is a rank budget: \(M\) can have rank at most \(d_k\), so a narrow head is a head restricted to a low-rank notion of relevance. And nothing forces \(M\) to be symmetric.

That asymmetry is the point. Tie the two matrices together — set \(W^Q=W^K\) — and \(M\) becomes symmetric and positive semi-definite, which costs you two things at once. Scores become mutual, \(s_{ij}=s_{ji}\), so a token cannot heed a neighbour more than that neighbour heeds it back; and \(s_{ii}=x_iMx_i^{\top}\ge 0\), so every token is pushed toward finding itself the most relevant thing in the room. Language is not like that. An adjective must attend to the noun it modifies far harder than the noun attends back; a pronoun must hunt for its antecedent while the antecedent goes about its business. Relevance in a sentence is a directed relation, and a symmetric form cannot express one.

Then there is the third matrix, and it earns its keep for a different reason: it separates how a token is found from what a token contributes. The key is an address, the value is the cargo. Because \(W^K\) and \(W^V\) are learned independently, a token can advertise one property and deliver another — a verb can be findable by its grammatical role while contributing its meaning, and the same token can be located by one head for its tense and by another for its topic without the two uses interfering. Fuse keys and values and every token would have to be retrievable by exactly the content it hands over. Three matrices, three genuinely different jobs: what I am looking for, how I can be found, and what I am worth once found.

Where the square root actually comes from

The chapter told you the divisor keeps the softmax from saturating. That is the consequence; here is the derivation, and it is three lines. Treat the components of a query and a key at initialisation as independent, mean zero, variance \(\sigma^2\). A score is their dot product, \(s=\sum_a q_ak_a\). Each term has mean \(\mathbb{E}[q_ak_a]=\mathbb{E}[q_a]\mathbb{E}[k_a]=0\) and variance \(\mathbb{E}[q_a^2k_a^2]=\sigma^2\sigma^2\), and independent terms add their variances, so \(\mathrm{Var}(s)=d_k\sigma^4\) and the spread of scores is \(\sigma^2\sqrt{d_k}\). The width of the head sets the width of the score distribution — through its square root. Divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) and the spread returns to \(\sigma^2\), the same at every head width you might choose. Nothing about the model is being corrected; a scale that the architecture accidentally introduced is being removed.

Now the part that makes it urgent rather than tidy, and it lives in the derivative. The softmax has Jacobian \(\partial p_i/\partial s_j=p_i(\delta_{ij}-p_j)\). Read what happens as one weight approaches one and the rest approach zero: every entry of that matrix contains a factor of some \(p\) that is going to zero, so the whole Jacobian goes to zero with it. A saturated attention row does not merely make a confident choice — it stops passing gradient backwards through the choice, so the scores that produced it can no longer be corrected. Saturation is not a cosmetic problem about peaky pictures; it is a layer quietly disconnecting itself from training.

One honest caveat and one payoff. The assumption is only about scale at initialisation — trained queries and keys are neither independent nor unit-variance, and the model is perfectly free to grow the scores back if a sharp distribution is what the task wants. That is why the constant is fixed rather than learned: it does not decide how peaky attention should end up, it only guarantees that a deep stack starts in the regime where gradient still flows and can decide. And the payoff is that you now recognise the same object twice in this chapter. \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is a temperature on the softmax over neighbours, fixed by an argument about width; \(\tau\) later is a temperature on the softmax over candidates, tuned by an argument about negatives. One knob, two placements, and in both cases what it controls is how much gradient reaches the losing options.

Attention is retrieval, softened

The three names — query, key, value — were borrowed from us, not from linguists. A search engine takes a query, compares it against the keys of the stored entries, and returns the contents of the best matches. Attention does exactly that inside one sentence: every token issues a query, every token advertises a key, the dot product scores the match, and what comes back is contents. If you understand how a search engine works, you already understand the shape of the attention mechanism.

There is exactly one difference, and everything else follows from it: a search engine cuts — it keeps the top few and throws the tail away — whereas attention never cuts, it keeps everybody with some weight. That softening is precisely what makes the mechanism learnable. A hard cut-off has no derivative: nudge a score slightly and the selected set either does not change at all or flips wholesale, so the gradient has nothing to say. A softmax weight, by contrast, moves smoothly, and the gradient can ask the model to listen to river a little harder. Attention is retrieval you can differentiate, which is why it turned out to be the learnable part.

And the bill for softness is the same wall we already met. Since nobody is cut, every pair has to be scored: no shortlist, no early exit, no index inside a sentence. Then notice where the identical trade resurfaces one scale up: across a million documents you cannot score everyone, so real search goes back to the hard version — cut first, refine later. Soft inside the sentence, hard across the archive; the rest of the course is a negotiation over that border.

Who is allowed to look at whom

Self-attention lets every token see every other token — but should it always? That single question splits the Transformer world in two. In BERT, the answer is yes: every token reads the whole sentence, left and right, both directions at once. That's a bidirectional encoder, and it's exactly what you want for understanding — to know what "bank" means you need to peek at "river" whether it came before or after. Search lives here: a retrieval model wants to read the full query and the full document, so retrieval encoders are bidirectional, BERT-style.

But a model that writes text — GPT — can't be allowed to peek ahead. When it's predicting the next word, the words to its right don't exist yet; letting it attend to them would be cheating, learning to copy the answer. So a generation model wears a causal mask: before the softmax, every score from a token to any later token is set to \(-\infty\), so attention to the future is exactly zero. Each token may attend only to itself and the past. That one triangular mask is the entire difference between an encoder that reads and a decoder that generates — the same machinery, with a curtain drawn over the future.

From this fall the three architecture families worth keeping straight. Encoder-only (BERT, and every dense retriever in this course) reads bidirectionally and embeds — it's the family search uses. Decoder-only (GPT, Llama) is causal and generates one token at a time; here a trick called the KV-cache earns its keep — since past tokens never change, their keys and values are computed once and reused, so each new token costs \(O(n)\) instead of recomputing the whole \(n^2\) table. Encoder-decoder (T5, the original translation Transformer) does both: an encoder reads the source, and a decoder generates the target while cross-attending back to the encoder — which is finally where the cross-attention we name-dropped earlier earns its place. One mask, three families, and search firmly in the encoder camp.

Many heads, many councils

One round of self-attention has a quiet limitation: it produces one set of weights per token, so it can only express one notion of relevance at a time. But "bank" cares about its neighbours in several different ways at once — which word is its grammatical subject, which adjective modifies it, which earlier noun it refers back to. A single attention head, forced to average all of those into one weighting, blurs them together. So the Transformer runs several heads in parallel, and that is multi-head attention.

Mechanically it's the same recipe, replicated. With \(h\) heads (the original Transformer used \(h = 8\); our worked example carried \(2\)), the model holds \(h\) independent sets of query/key/value matrices. Each head projects the tokens into its own lower-dimensional subspace, runs the full \(\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V\) there, and produces its own contextual blend. Crucially, because each head has its own learned projections, each one is free to specialize — head 1 can learn to track the subject-verb link, head 2 the adjective it modifies, head 3 long-range coreference — and they don't interfere, because they live in separate subspaces.

Then you reassemble. The \(h\) per-head outputs are concatenated back into one wide vector and passed through a final learned projection matrix \(W^O\) that mixes the heads and returns to the model's working dimension: $$\mathrm{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)\,W^O.$$ In council terms: instead of one meeting, you convene several simultaneous sub-councils, each weighing the room by a different concern, and then a chair (\(W^O\)) blends their conclusions into one verdict. The cost is modest — splitting the dimension across heads keeps the total compute about the same — and the payoff is a representation that captures many kinds of relationship at once. That richer blend is what gets stacked into a Transformer.

What the heads actually learn

I claimed head 1 learns subject-verb, head 2 modifiers, head 3 coreference — but that's the kind of tidy story that deserves to be checked, not asserted. So researchers went and looked, probing the attention weights of a trained BERT. The headline finding (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019): the heads really do specialize, and often interpretably. Some heads are almost purely positional — they attend to the previous token, or the next one, acting like a learned n-gram. Others track syntax: a head that reliably points each verb at its direct object, another that links a determiner to its noun. Others light up on rare or special tokens. The metaphor of "different councillors with different jobs" turns out to be more literal than you'd guess.

Two honest caveats keep this from being magic. First, not every head is interpretable — plenty learn diffuse, redundant patterns, and you can prune a large fraction of heads after training with little loss, which tells you the model trains more heads than it strictly needs and keeps the useful few. Second, an attention weight is not a clean "explanation": a head attending hard to a token doesn't prove that token caused the output, so read heatmaps as hints, not proofs. Still, the specialization is real, and it's why multi-head beats single-head: a council of specialists genuinely sees more than one generalist could.

Hold on to one structural fact under all of this, because it explains both the power and the earlier flaw. Attention is permutation-equivariant: permute the input tokens and the output vectors come out permuted the same way, with identical values — the mechanism has no built-in notion of "first" or "third". That equivariance is exactly what lets all tokens be processed in parallel and lets a head's learned pattern apply anywhere in the sentence. It's also precisely why, with nothing added, the model is order-blind — the very problem the next move was built to fix.

Attention is order-blind

Now a flaw, and it's a fundamental one. Look again at how attention works: every token's query is dotted against every token's key, the weights are softmaxed, the values are blended. Nowhere in that computation does the order of the tokens enter. Shuffle the words in the sentence and feed them in a different order, and — apart from the labels moving with them — the math produces the exact same set of blends. Unmasked self-attention without positional signals is permutation-equivariant, \(f(PX)=P f(X)\): to it, a sentence is a bag of tokens, not a sequence.

That is a catastrophe for language, where order is meaning. "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the identical multiset of words; only the order distinguishes a normal Tuesday from front-page news. "The cat sat on the mat" and "The mat sat on the cat" — same bag, very different scenes. A model that genuinely cannot see order would treat all of these as interchangeable, which makes it useless for anything beyond keyword soup. The very property that lets attention process all tokens in parallel — treating them as an unordered set — is the property that blinds it to sequence.

So we have to put the order back in by hand. Since the attention mechanism itself won't look at position, we inject position into the input vectors before attention ever runs: we add, to each token's embedding, a special vector that depends only on where the token sits — first, second, seventeenth. Now "cat" in position 3 and "cat" in position 8 arrive at the attention layer as slightly different vectors, and the model can finally tell where each word is. The question is what those position vectors should be — and the original Transformer's answer is a beautiful little trick with sines and cosines, which we'll see next, exact.

Why position is added, not glued on

First make the blindness a theorem rather than an observation, because the fix depends on knowing exactly where order can and cannot enter. Let \(P\) be a permutation matrix and shuffle the input rows: the scores become \((PX)W^Q\big((PX)W^K\big)^{\top}=P\,S\,P^{\top}\), the softmax is taken row by row so it commutes with a relabelling of rows and columns, and the blend comes out as \(P\,\mathrm{Attn}(X)\). Shuffle the input, get the identically shuffled output — attention is permutation-equivariant, exactly and for every choice of weights. No training run, no clever loss, no amount of depth can break that, because it is a property of the operation and not of the parameters. Order has to be written into the vectors before the first attention, or it never exists at all.

So we add a position vector to each token's embedding, and every reader has the same objection: adding corrupts the meaning, whereas concatenating would keep the two apart. Watch what the very next operation does to that objection. The first thing that touches \(x_i+p_i\) is a linear map, and a linear map distributes: \(W^Q(x_i+p_i)=W^Qx_i+W^Qp_i\). Push it into the score and it splits into four readable terms — content against content, content against position, position against content, position against position. That is precisely the decomposition concatenation would have produced, obtained without spending a single extra dimension. Adding is not mixing two signals into mush; it is superposition, and a linear layer can pull the parts back out as long as the position vectors do not sit along the same directions the content uses. In a space of several hundred dimensions there is room for that, and training arranges it.

Two things fall out of the four-term reading, and both are worth carrying. First, three of those terms mention absolute indices and only one of them — position against position — depends on the pair at all; that lopsidedness is the honest reason absolute encodings feel like a bolt-on, and it is the gap the relative schemes in the next beat are built to close by making the score a function of \(i-j\) directly. Second, it explains the failure people actually hit: an absolute stamp for an index the model never saw during training is a vector from nowhere in particular, and the content terms it multiplies against were never fitted to it. The model does not "forget" long documents — it is being handed a coordinate it was never taught to read.

Attention is order-blind — “dog bites man” reads the same as “man bites dog.” So we add a fixed position signal to each token. Here is the grid: one row per position, one column per dimension, each cell a sine or cosine value.

Read it position by position, top to bottom. Position 0 is the all-sin-0 / all-cos-1 baseline; each next row shifts the phase a little. No two rows are identical, so every position carries a unique fingerprint.

Now read it dimension by dimension, left to right. The leftmost columns flip sign quickly down the grid — short wavelengths — while the rightmost columns barely change — long wavelengths. Low dims encode fine position, high dims encode coarse position.

Every value stays in [−1, 1], and the whole grid is computed from a closed-form formula — fixed, not learned. The model gets order for free, and the same pattern extends to positions it never saw in training.

Why sinusoids and not just the index

Start with the obvious idea, so that its failure teaches something: just add the position number to the vector. It breaks in two places at once. The number is unbounded — deep inside a long document the position stamp outgrows the meaning it is being added to, and one and the same coordinate is forced to say both where and what. And it is uncalibrated — the model has no way to know whether a difference of one is a lot or a little, because the scale drifts with the length of the text. What you want is a stamp that is bounded in size and still different in every slot.

A ladder of sinusoids gives you both. Each coordinate oscillates at its own rate: the fast ones change between neighbours and therefore carry local order, the slow ones barely move across a whole passage and therefore carry roughly-where-in-the-text. Read across, a row of the grid is a fingerprint — no two positions share a combination. Read as a whole, it is a division of labour: fine detail at one end, a coarse coordinate at the other. And every value stays inside a fixed range, whatever the position.

What settled the choice, though, is a third property, and the picture does not show it. Shifting a position by a fixed offset acts on its sine-cosine pairs as a rotation, and a rotation is a linear map that does not depend on where you started. So the token \(k\) steps back is expressible as one and the same fixed linear operation on the positional part — identical at the start of the text and at the end. That is why an absolute stamp can carry relative distance at all, and it is exactly the property that modern schemes stop leaving to luck and build in on purpose, which is the next beat.

How modern models really tell position

The sinusoid grid you just scrolled is the original answer, and it's lovely, but it has a quiet weakness: it's an absolute code. It stamps each slot with its absolute index — position 5, position 600 — and adds that stamp to the token's embedding at the very bottom of the stack. That works, but it ages badly. A model trained on sentences up to 512 long has never seen the stamp for position 5,000, so it generalizes poorly past its training length, and absolute position turns out to be a slightly awkward thing to bolt onto a mechanism that really cares about relative distance — "how far is this word from that one".

So modern Transformers mostly switched to two cleverer schemes, both built around relative position. RoPE — rotary position embedding (Su et al., 2021) — doesn't add anything; it rotates each query and key vector by an angle proportional to its position. The beautiful consequence: when you take the dot product of a query at position \(i\) with a key at position \(j\), the rotations combine so the score depends only on the difference \(i - j\). Relative distance falls straight out of the geometry, for free, at every layer. ALiBi is even blunter: skip position embeddings entirely and just subtract a penalty proportional to distance from each attention score, so far-apart tokens are gently discouraged. Both share a headline virtue — they extrapolate, staying coherent on sequences far longer than anything seen in training.

Why does a search course care about a detail of position encoding? Because retrieval increasingly means embedding long things — a full document, a transcript, a contract — and the position scheme is exactly what decides whether a model trained on short passages still behaves at 8,000 tokens. RoPE in particular is now near-universal in the open models you'd actually fine-tune into a retriever. Same idea as the sinusoid — tell the model where each token sits — but engineered so the answer survives lengths the training never showed it.

Assemble the block

Step back and count what we have. Multi-head self-attention lets every token rebuild itself from the whole sentence. Positional encoding tells it where everything sits. Those two pieces are the heart of the Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017, "Attention Is All You Need") — but they are not the whole engine. Attention is one component of a small, repeating unit, and the rest of that unit is what makes the thing actually trainable and deep.

Here's the reveal: attention by itself has two practical problems. First, it's entirely linear in its values — a weighted average of vectors is still just a blend, with no way to apply a genuinely non-linear transformation to what it found. Second, stacking many attention layers on top of each other makes training unstable; the signal degrades and gradients misbehave as depth grows. The Transformer's design solves both with two extra ingredients wrapped around attention — a feed-forward network and residual connections with layer normalization — and those, plus attention, form the block that gets repeated \(N\) times to make a deep model.

Think of it as upgrading the council from a single vote into a full deliberative chamber. Attention is the round of listening — each member gathering a weighted summary of the room. Then each member needs a round of private thinking (the feed-forward network), a way to not forget what they already knew while they update (the residual connection), and a moderator who keeps everyone speaking at the same volume (layer normalization). Run that whole procedure, then do it again, and again — depth by repetition. The next step assembles the block piece by piece, in order; let's watch it come together.

Start at the bottom: each token’s embedding, with its positional encoding added in, enters the block. Data flows upward through the stack.

First sublayer: Multi-Head Attention. Every token attends to every other (and itself) across h parallel heads — the Council deciding who to heed.

Then Add & Norm. A residual skip carries the input straight past attention and adds it back — so gradients flow and nothing is lost — and LayerNorm rescales the sum to keep it stable.

Second sublayer: a position-wise Feed-Forward network — two linear layers with a ReLU between them — applied to each token independently. This is where most of the parameters live.

Another Add & Norm wraps the FFN: residual skip plus LayerNorm again. Every sublayer in the block follows the same x → sublayer(x) → add → norm recipe.

That is one encoder layer. Stack it ×N — 6 in the original Transformer, 12 or 24 in BERT — and depth lets the model build ever richer contextual representations. The output is a context-aware vector per token.

Five tokens — “the cat sat on mat” — start as five points spread across the plane. This is the cloud the block will reshape: watch what each sublayer does to it, geometrically.

Attention lets every token blend in a weighted share of its neighbours. Geometrically the points are PULLED toward each other — the cloud contracts. The faint trails show where each point moved.

Add & Norm: the residual is added back, then LayerNorm recentres the cloud to mean ≈ 0 and rescales it to unit size — the points land on a ring. The shape is preserved, the scale is tamed.

The FFN reshapes each point on its own — a nonlinear bend applied per token, with no mixing between tokens. The cloud warps, but no point heeds another here; this stage is purely per-position.

Add & Norm again — recentre and back onto the unit ring. That is one whole encoder block, seen as geometry: blend, tame, reshape, tame. Stack many of these and meaning is refined layer by layer.

Here is the cat token's attention-output vector x — four numbers, [0.579, 1.996, 0.91, 0.425], the very vector we built with attention. They sit off-centre: their mean is 0.98 and their variance is 0.38. The dashed line marks the mean. A raw vector like this can drift in scale from layer to layer, which makes training unstable.

First move: subtract the mean. Every bar drops by μ, so the vector now straddles zero — the dashed baseline slides from 0.98 down to 0. The shape is unchanged; we have only recentred it.

Second move: divide by the standard deviation. The bars rescale to unit spread — now the mean is 0 and the variance is 1. Geometrically the vector has been pulled onto the unit circle (a unit sphere in higher dimensions): same direction, fixed length. That is what LayerNorm means by “normalise.”

Last move: the learned scale γ and shift β. Each dimension is multiplied by its own γ and offset by its own β, bending the bars back into a useful shape. Normalisation fixed the scale; γ and β give the model the freedom to undo or reshape it where that helps.

What LayerNorm does to a vector, geometrically

The formula subtracts a mean and divides by a standard deviation, which sounds like statistics and is actually geometry. Subtracting the mean of a token's own coordinates is subtracting its component along the all-ones vector \(\mathbf{1}\) — that is an orthogonal projection onto the hyperplane \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}x=0\). Dividing by the standard deviation then fixes the length of what remains, since after centring the deviation is just the norm rescaled by the width. Put the two together and every token, whatever it arrived as, leaves lying on one fixed sphere inside one fixed hyperplane. LayerNorm keeps a token's direction and destroys exactly two numbers: an offset along \(\mathbf{1}\) and an overall scale.

Now the fact this explains. A residual stack only ever adds: each sublayer writes its result on top of the stream, so the stream's magnitude grows with depth, and without intervention a layer at the top would see inputs on a completely different scale than the same layer would at the bottom. Weights mean something only relative to the scale of what they multiply, so that drift alone makes a deep stack untrainable. LayerNorm removes it by construction: every sublayer sees an input of fixed size, so one set of learned weights means the same thing at any depth. And this is why the learned gain and shift are bolted back on afterwards — the projection throws away two numbers indiscriminately, and \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) hand back a per-feature scale and offset that the model chose, rather than one it inherited from whatever the previous layer happened to output.

Keep one distinction sharp, because conflating the two is why people are surprised later. LayerNorm centres each token over its own coordinates — a private operation, no other vector consulted. The cure for the anisotropic cone centres the cloud over the corpus mean — a shared direction subtracted from everybody. They are different subtractions along different vectors, and doing the first a hundred times does nothing about the second. A stack packed with LayerNorms can still hand you embeddings crowded into one narrow cone, because nothing in the normalisation ever looked at another sentence. That is the trap waiting at the end of the chapter, and it is already visible from here.

Why a modern block looks different

If you crack open a Transformer written today and lay it next to the 2017 diagram, one small thing has quietly moved, and it matters more than it looks. In the original block, layer normalization sits after the residual add: \(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x))\) — this is post-norm. Modern blocks put the norm inside, before the sublayer, and add the raw input back outside: \(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x))\) — pre-norm. A trivial-looking reshuffle of one operation; a large difference in whether a deep stack trains at all.

Here's the why. The residual connection's whole job is to give signal a clean, unblocked path from the bottom of the stack to the top. In post-norm, a LayerNorm sits on that path at every layer, and stacking dozens of them squeezes and re-scales the gradient over and over — deep post-norm models are fragile, often refusing to train without a careful learning-rate warmup and a lot of babysitting. In pre-norm, the residual highway runs uninterrupted; the norm only cleans the input handed to each sublayer, never the highway itself. The result is a stack you can make very deep and train stably with far less fuss. That single move is most of why a 100-layer Transformer is even possible.

It's worth naming the supporting cast that travels with this design, because you'll meet them in any real implementation: the residual keeps a token from forgetting what it already knew while a sublayer updates it; LayerNorm keeps every token speaking at the same scale so no feature drowns out the others; and the position-wise feed-forward network — usually four times the model's width — is where most of the parameters live and, intriguingly, where a lot of the model's stored knowledge turns out to sit. One line of research reads that FFN as a big key–value memory (Geva et al., 2021): the first weight matrix's rows act as keys — pattern detectors each token is matched against — and the second matrix's rows are the values retrieved and added back when a key fires. It's the same query-against-keys-then-fetch-values shape as attention, only the keys and values are fixed parameters rather than other tokens — a soft lookup table holding much of what the model knows. For search this is mostly under the hood, but it's the reason the encoders you'll fine-tune are deep, stable, and trainable in the first place.

One question that design quietly settles is which normalization, and the answer is never BatchNorm — always LayerNorm. The difference is the axis they normalize over. BatchNorm normalizes each feature across the batch — to standardize feature 3 it pools feature 3's values over all the examples in the batch. LayerNorm normalizes each token across its own features — one token, self-contained, no reference to any other example. For sequences that gap is decisive. Sentences have variable lengths and get padded, so per-feature batch statistics are polluted by meaningless pad positions; batch size at inference is often one, where a per-batch statistic is undefined; and BatchNorm has to keep separate running statistics for train versus test, a moving target. LayerNorm sidesteps all of it by being batch-independent: a token's normalized output depends only on that token, so it's identical whether the batch holds one sentence or a thousand, and identical at train and test time. For retrieval that's not a nicety but a correctness property — the same sentence must embed to the same vector regardless of what else shares its batch. That's why every Transformer reaches for LayerNorm.

The representation x enters the block riding a highway — six numbers, length ‖x‖ shown at the checkpoint. Hold onto this lane: x will be carried all the way through, never thrown away.

First sublayer: attention. Its output enters from a side branch and is ADDED to x — the running vector becomes x + attn(x). The faint vector underneath is the old x: it is still there. The skip connection lets x bypass the block entirely; the sublayer only adds a correction.

Add & Norm — the first LayerNorm. The running vector is renormalised: its length settles to 2.4495 (that is √6, the L2 norm of a unit-RMS six-dimensional vector). Normalising keeps the highway from drifting in scale as adds pile up.

Second sublayer: the feed-forward network. Again its output enters from a side branch and is ADDED — x becomes (LayerNorm output) + ffn(x). Same pattern as attention: a side branch, a “+”, the old vector still beneath. Two adds so far, never a replacement.

Add & Norm again — the second LayerNorm. The length returns to 2.4495 = √6. That is one whole block: two adds and two norms. The highway carried x the entire way; each sublayer only nudged it, which is why very deep stacks still train.

From tokens to a sentence vector

Stack that block a dozen or two dozen times, train it on a mountain of text, and you have BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) — a deep Transformer encoder that reads a whole sentence both directions at once and hands back, for every token, a richly contextual vector. "bank" near "river" and "bank" near "deposited" emerge from the top layer as genuinely different points. The polysemy that defeated static embeddings is, at the token level, solved. The map can finally read the room.

But search usually wants one vector for a whole query or a whole document, not a vector per token. So how do we pool a sentence's worth of contextual token vectors into a single sentence embedding? BERT's classic recipe prepends a special token, [CLS], to every input and trains it so that its top-layer vector serves as a summary of the entire sequence — read off [CLS], and you have a one-vector representation of the sentence. The common, often-better alternative is mean pooling: just average all the token vectors from the top layer. Either way, a variable-length sentence collapses to a single fixed-length vector you can drop straight onto the Map of Meaning.

And right there is the bridge to retrieval. Embed the query into one vector, embed every document into one vector, and search becomes nearest-neighbour on the Map — cosine again, Sir Cosine still measuring angles, but now over contextual vectors that actually understand polysemy and word order. This is the dense-retrieval dream. There is just one catch, and it's a sharp one: the vectors that fall out of an off-the-shelf BERT are not yet good for search. They were trained to predict masked words, not to make relevant query-document pairs land close. To fix that we need to train the space for retrieval — which is the last act of the chapter.

What stacking actually buys

"Depth is repetition" is true and slightly maddening, because after a single layer every position already depends on every other — the cross-derivative we opened the chapter with is non-zero for every pair after one round of attention. In a convolutional stack depth buys reach: each layer widens the window until distant pixels can finally influence each other. Here the window is the whole sentence from the start. So the question is honest and worth answering: if layer one already sees everything, what is layer two for?

Depth buys composition, not reach. Layer one blends raw tokens; layer two blends things that are already blends, and — this is the part that matters — layer two computes its attention weights from the contextual vectors layer one produced. The question a layer asks depends on what the previous layers found. By the chain rule, \(\partial h^{(L)}_i/\partial x_j\) is a sum over every path \(j\to m\to\dots\to i\) through intermediate positions, so while the pair stays the same, the number of routes between them multiplies with depth. That is what lets resolution happen in stages: find the verb, then find its object, then decide who the pronoun refers to — each step needing the previous step's answer, none of them expressible as a single weighted average of the input.

And depth buys the thing a hundred attention layers could never provide on their own. Attention returns a convex blend, so it can only produce directions the sentence already contains; stack a thousand of them with nothing in between and you still have one big blend. The feed-forward network between the attentions is what supplies a genuinely new direction per position, and the block alternates the two deliberately: mix across positions, then think within a position, repeat. That alternation is what the size ladder of encoders is really a ladder of, and it is the practical knob when a retriever has to be cheap — fewer repetitions of the pair, not a wider single round.

Which pooling makes a search vector

I waved at three ways to squeeze a sentence into one vector — let's choose between them properly, because the choice quietly decides how good your retriever is. Option one, [CLS] pooling: read off the single special token's top-layer vector. Option two, mean pooling: average all the token vectors. Option three, last-token pooling: take the final token's vector, which is the natural pick for decoder-only models where only the last position has seen the whole sequence. For a bidirectional retriever, here's the honest verdict: a [CLS] token from a model that was never fine-tuned for retrieval makes a surprisingly bad sentence vector — it was shaped by pre-training objectives (masked-LM / next-sentence prediction), not by any similarity goal — so the practical default is mean pooling, and this is exactly the recipe SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) settled on. The same [CLS] token becomes excellent only after you fine-tune it for retrieval, which is the next beat.

Then there's a gotcha that silently corrupts mean pooling if you're not careful: padding. To run a batch, short sentences are padded with filler tokens up to the longest one — and if you naively average all positions, you've averaged in those meaningless pad vectors and diluted your embedding with garbage. The fix is the attention mask: every pad position's score is set to \(-\infty\) before softmax (so no real token attends to padding), and the mean is taken over real tokens only. Forget the mask and your batch-of-one embeddings won't match your batch-of-many embeddings — a genuinely common "why is my retrieval flaky" bug.

One last artifact worth recognizing, because it explains weird attention heatmaps. Trained Transformers develop attention sinks: heads dump a large share of their attention weight onto a few specific tokens — very often [CLS], [SEP], or simply the first token — not because those tokens are meaningful, but because softmax must assign its weights to something, and a sink is a convenient place to park leftover attention (Xiao et al., 2023). It's a reason raw [CLS] is a noisy summary, a reason heatmaps light up on punctuation, and a quiet warning: an attention weight is where the model put its budget, not always where the meaning is. Pool with the mask, fine-tune for search, and these artifacts stop mattering.

Train the space for search

Why aren't pre-trained BERT vectors good for search out of the box? Because of what they were trained to do. BERT's pre-training objective is masked-language-modelling: hide a word, predict it from context. That makes superb token representations for grammar and meaning — but nothing in that objective ever asks the model to place a question near its answer, or a query near a relevant document. The geometry that's perfect for filling in blanks is not the geometry where relevance equals nearness.

What we actually want is a space with a single, blunt property: a query and a document that answer it should be close; a query and an irrelevant document should be far. Nearness must mean relevance, exactly the way it had to mean similarity on last chapter's map — but now tuned for the retrieval task specifically. We can't get there by predicting masked words. We have to state the goal directly and optimize for it: pull things that should match together, push things that shouldn't apart.

That is contrastive learning, and it is the training principle behind every modern dense retriever — SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020), and their descendants. You feed the model pairs that belong together (a query and its relevant passage, two paraphrases, a sentence and its translation) and pairs that don't, and you reshape the space until the belong-together pairs sit close and everything else sits far. Sir Cosine is the judge: the loss is written directly in terms of cosine similarity, so the model is optimized on the very metric search will use. The next step does the contrastive objective exactly, on real numbers.

From one negative to all of them at once

The blunt way to say "pull together, push apart" is the triplet loss: take an anchor \(a\), its positive \(p\), one negative \(n\), and demand that the positive win by a margin, \(\max\!\big(0,\ m-\cos(a,p)+\cos(a,n)\big)\). It works, and it has two chronic diseases. The margin \(m\) is a cliff you have to guess: once the pair clears it the loss is exactly zero and the gradient with it, so the overwhelming majority of triplets teach nothing and you spend your life mining ones that still hurt. And it judges one negative at a time, while retrieval will ask the model to beat a whole corpus.

InfoNCE replaces the demand "beat this one by \(m\)" with "rank the positive first among the whole set", and the replacement is literally a classification: $$\mathcal{L}=-\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\cos(a,p)/\tau\big)}{\exp\!\big(\cos(a,p)/\tau\big)+\sum_{n}\exp\!\big(\cos(a,n)/\tau\big)}$$ — cross-entropy over candidates, with the positive as the correct class. Differentiate it and the cure for both diseases falls out on one line. Writing \(P(\cdot)\) for the softmax weights inside the log, the gradient with respect to a negative's score is \(P(n)\), and with respect to the positive's it is \(P(p)-1\). Each negative is pushed away in exact proportion to how much the model currently believes it. No margin to tune, no dead zone: a negative the model has already dismissed carries a weight near zero and simply stops consuming capacity, while whatever is still confusable gets essentially all of the gradient. Hard-negative weighting, for free, recomputed every step.

That leaves \(\tau\) doing one clean job: it decides how concentrated that automatic weighting is. Small \(\tau\) sharpens the softmax until nearly all the pressure lands on the single hardest negative, which is the triplet loss again, only with the hard one chosen for you instead of mined; large \(\tau\) flattens it until every negative is pushed about equally, which is a mean, not a contest. So triplet-with-mining and InfoNCE are not rival philosophies — they are the two ends of one dial, and \(\tau\) is where you sit on it. Which makes the next question the only one left: if the loss automatically concentrates on the hardest candidates present, everything depends on which candidates you put in front of it.

Start with the anchor “cat” at the centre. Every other word is placed by its cosine to it — the angle IS the distance. Positives (kitten, dog) want to be close; negatives (airplane, computer, france) want to be far. Right now the off-the-shelf space is messy.

Sir Cosine measures each pair. dog scores .922 and kitten .639 — both high — while airplane is .365, computer .353, and france only .091. The positives already out-score the negatives, but not cleanly enough for retrieval.

The contrastive objective is the gradient drawn as a force: a PULL arrow on every positive (toward the anchor) and a PUSH arrow on every negative (away). Nothing has moved yet — this is just the direction each word is about to be nudged.

Now take the step: the dots LAND at their trained positions. The kitten/dog cluster tightens around “cat”, while airplane, computer and france slide out to the rim. The space has re-shaped — near now really means relevant.

InfoNCE turns the cosines into a softmax: the positive “kitten” already gets probability 0.8877, so the loss is just −ln(0.8877) = 0.1191 — small, and shrinking. The triplet loss agrees: with margin 0.2 the gap is already wide enough, so its loss is 0.0. Cosine lives inside the loss; in-batch negatives come for free.

The negatives are the hard part

Look back at the loss you just computed. The positive — query and its right answer — is handed to you by your labels. The negatives are where all the difficulty (and all the art) lives. The cheapest source is in-batch negatives: for each query in a batch, treat every other query's positive as a negative for this one — free, since they're already in memory, and DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) showed this alone beats BM25. But in-batch negatives are easy: a random passage about cooking is trivially far from a query about astrophysics, so once the model gets the gist, easy negatives stop teaching it anything.

So the strong move is hard-negative mining: deliberately fish for negatives that look relevant but aren't — run BM25 (or the current model) on the query, take the top-ranked passages that aren't the labeled answer, and train against those. These are the confusable cases right on the decision boundary, and learning to push them away is what sharpens a retriever from "okay" to "genuinely good". But hard negatives carry a sharp, famous hazard: the false-negative trap. The whole reason a mined passage looks relevant is that it often is relevant — just never labeled. Train the model to shove a truly-good document away from its query and you are actively teaching it the wrong lesson; this labeling sparsity is the deepest pitfall of contrastive retrieval, and real systems fight it with score thresholds, a denoising teacher, or simply discarding too-similar mined negatives.

Step back and there's a clean way to see what the contrastive loss is really optimizing, due to Wang & Isola (2020): two forces, in tension. Alignment pulls positive pairs together — answers should sit on top of their questions. Uniformity spreads everything else evenly over the sphere — embeddings should use the whole space, not bunch up. Push only alignment and you get the disease called representation collapse: the model discovers it can make every pair "close" by mapping all inputs to nearly the same vector — loss looks great, retrieval is useless because nothing is distinguishable. The temperature \(\tau\) in InfoNCE is your dial between these forces: a small \(\tau\) (we used \(0.1\)) sharpens focus onto the hardest negatives and spreads the space hard; too small and training turns unstable. And normalizing every vector to the unit sphere first is what makes "uniformity" a coherent goal at all — it's why cosine, not raw dot product, is the metric. Negatives, temperature, normalization: the three knobs that turn a generic encoder into a search encoder.

The batch is the exam

In-batch negatives look like a free lunch, and they are, but notice what they quietly did to the objective. With in-batch negatives the loss is no longer a fixed function of your data — it is a function of your batch size, because the batch is the set of candidates the positive has to beat. A model that guesses uniformly scores \(-\log(1/B)=\log B\) on a batch of \(B\), so the number you print rises with the batch even for a model that learned nothing. Two runs at different batch sizes are two different exams, and comparing their losses is a category error. Compare retrieval metrics on a held-out set, never training loss across batch settings.

Then the reason everyone reaches for bigger batches anyway. The denominator of InfoNCE is an estimate of the true normaliser — the score of the positive against everything in the corpus — and a bigger sample estimates it better, so the gradient points in a less noisy direction. Bigger batches also raise the chance that something genuinely confusable is present, and by the previous beat's gradient rule, the loss automatically spends itself on whatever is hardest in the room. That is why retrieval training reaches for tricks that inflate the effective batch — recomputing encoder activations to fit more pairs in memory, gathering candidates from every device — rather than simply running more steps. But the returns fall off exactly where you would expect: once random passages are all dismissed with near-zero softmax weight, extra ones cost memory and contribute nothing, which is the case for mined negatives said from the other side.

Two structural traps ride along with the trick, and both get worse as you turn the knob up. The first is that the exam is one-directional: each query is scored against a pool of documents, so the space is trained to answer "which document for this query" and not "which query for this document". Real recipes symmetrise, averaging the loss with the transposed problem, so the geometry is shaped from both sides rather than one. The second is sharper: the bigger the batch, the more likely it contains a passage that genuinely answers this query while being labelled as someone else's positive — and the loss will dutifully push a correct answer away. The false-negative trap scales with exactly the knob you were turning up to improve training, which is why large-batch recipes come with de-duplication and score thresholds rather than without them.

Smaller, cheaper, distilled

Two production-flavored ideas before we close the contrastive thread, both about making these vectors cheap enough to actually deploy — and both pointing straight at the next chapter. The first answers a nagging tension: do you want a fat 768-dim vector (accurate, but heavy to store and slow to search over millions of documents) or a slim 64-dim one (cheap, but blunter)? Matryoshka embeddings (Kusupati et al., 2022) refuse the choice. The trick is to train the contrastive loss at several truncation lengths at once, so the model learns to pack the most important information into the first coordinates. The payoff is a single vector you can simply chop: keep the first 64 dims for a fast, approximate first pass over the whole index, then re-score the survivors with the full 768. One model, many sizes, nested like the dolls.

The second is distillation, and it's the quiet engine behind most production retrievers. A cross-encoder — which we'll meet properly in L10 — reads the query and document together, letting them attend to each other, and is far more accurate than a bi-encoder that embeds each in isolation. But it's far too slow to run over a million documents at query time. So you use it as a teacher: have the accurate-but-slow cross-encoder score a pile of query-document pairs, then train the fast bi-encoder (the student) to reproduce those scores. The student inherits much of the teacher's judgment at a tiny fraction of the cost — and this is precisely how SBERT-style retrievers are made competitive.

Notice that both ideas are really about the same bridge we keep circling: search has to happen in milliseconds over millions of documents, so the embedding has to be not just good but cheap to store and fast to compare. Matryoshka shrinks the vector; distillation shrinks the model; and both quietly assume the bi-encoder-vs-cross-encoder split that the whole next chapter is built on. We've now trained a space where nearness means relevance, made the vectors small and fast, and pried open the anisotropic cone — which, in fact, is the one trap we still have to name before we declare the Gremlin caged.

The cone of crowded vectors

Before we declare victory, one trap — and it's the reason the contrastive step isn't optional decoration but a necessity. Take a raw, un-tuned BERT and look at the geometry of its output vectors directly. You'd hope they spread out evenly over the sphere, pointing every which way. They don't. Ethayarajh (2019) measured it: contextual embeddings from BERT, ELMo, and GPT-2 crowd into a narrow cone — they're squeezed into one corner of the space, all pointing in roughly the same direction. This is anisotropy, and it is exactly the same pathology the Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith whispered about back in L2: when everything points the same way, every pair of vectors looks similar, and your similarity measure loses its discriminating power.

See why this is poison for cosine search. If two random, totally unrelated sentences already sit at cosine 0.8 just because all BERT vectors crowd the same cone, then a genuinely relevant pair at 0.85 is barely distinguishable from noise — the whole useful signal is squeezed into a sliver of the cosine range. The raw geometry inflates similarities across the board and flattens the contrast you actually need. A nearest-neighbour search on these vectors would be swamped by false neighbours, the high-dimensional crowding the Wraith promised.

And this is precisely what contrastive training and normalization fix. By explicitly pushing negatives apart, the contrastive objective spreads the cone open — it forces unrelated things to point in genuinely different directions, restoring the dynamic range of cosine so that 0.92 and 0.37 mean something again. (Whitening and simple normalization help too, but contrastive fine-tuning does the heavy lifting.) So the contrastive step from the last beat isn't just "nice to have"; it's the cure for an anisotropy that would otherwise make dense retrieval quietly mediocre. Tune the space, open the cone — and now the vectors are ready for search.

What the cone takes away from cosine

Split every embedding into two parts: the cloud's mean \(\mu\) — the axis of that cone — and one text's own deviation \(r\), so \(x=\mu+r\). Now expand the dot product of two texts: \(\langle x_a,x_b\rangle=\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}+\langle\mu,r_a\rangle+\langle\mu,r_b\rangle+\langle r_a,r_b\rangle\). Four terms — and it pays to ask each one what it actually knows.

\(\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}\) is the same number for every pair in the corpus: a constant offset that lifts all similarities together and eats the usable range. The two middle terms involve one text each — they can shift how similar a text looks to everything, but neither expresses a relation between the pair you asked about. Only \(\langle r_a,r_b\rangle\) knows which pair this is — and while \(\mu\) is long relative to \(r\), it is also the smallest term in the sum. The ranking survives in the trailing digits, and Sir Cosine is left squinting at them.

Now both cures read straight off that line instead of sounding like folklore. Subtract \(\mu\) from every vector — that is centring, whitening's first step — and the constant offset is gone exactly, not approximately. Contrastive training attacks the other side: pushing negatives apart grows the spread of \(r\) relative to \(\mu\), so the one discriminating term stops being the smallest. That is why the two cures compose rather than compete, and why normalising onto the sphere afterwards keeps the geometry stable.

Why the length is thrown away

The loss is written on cosine and not on the raw scalar product, and that is a decision worth defending rather than a notational habit. Factor the score: \(\langle a,p\rangle=\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert p\rVert\cos\theta\). An unnormalised objective can therefore be improved along two completely different routes — rotate the vectors so the angle closes, which is the work you wanted, or simply grow the norms of whatever the model is already confident about, which costs no rearrangement at all. Gradient descent takes the cheap route whenever one exists. What you get is a space where the score tracks how long a vector is — and length, in a trained encoder, tracks how often and how consistently the text's material was seen, not what it is about. The metric would be reporting corpus statistics dressed as relevance.

Normalising removes that route exactly rather than approximately. On the unit sphere there is no norm left to inflate, so the only remaining way to lower the loss is to move directions — the optimiser is forced into the work you actually asked for. Bounding the score also makes the temperature meaningful: \(\tau\) divides a quantity that now always lives in a fixed range, so one setting means the same thing for every pair, whereas on unnormalised scores the same \(\tau\) would be aggressive for short vectors and toothless for long ones. Alignment and uniformity, the two forces the objective decomposes into, are both statements about directions on a sphere; without the sphere neither one is even well-posed.

Two honest riders. The norm is not noise, and throwing it away is a real cost — it carries a usable signal about how well the corpus determined this text, and some systems keep it deliberately for calibration. But a retriever must rank documents of wildly different length and popularity against one query, so invariance to magnitude is a requirement, not a simplification. And the choice pays for itself downstream: for unit vectors \(\lVert a-b\rVert^{2}=2-2\cos\theta\), so ordering by cosine and ordering by Euclidean distance are the same ordering. Every index built for one serves the other unchanged — which is exactly why the nearest-neighbour machinery of the chapters ahead can assume a sphere and never look back.

The small print that keeps it from exploding

Three little numerical details have ridden along quietly under everything we built; they never make the headline, but get any one of them wrong and the model produces NaN, or worse, silently bad vectors. First, softmax overflow. Softmax exponentiates its inputs, and \(e^x\) overflows to infinity around \(x \approx 710\) in double precision — a perfectly ordinary attention score can blow up. The fix every real implementation uses is to subtract the row's maximum before exponentiating: \(\mathrm{softmax}(s)_i = e^{\,s_i - \max_j s_j} / \sum_k e^{\,s_k - \max_j s_j}\). Subtracting a constant from every score leaves the softmax mathematically identical (the constant cancels top and bottom) but keeps every exponent \(\le 0\), so nothing ever overflows. The same trick is why FlashAttention can stream attention in tiles — it tracks a running max.

Second, the LayerNorm epsilon. LayerNorm divides each token vector by its standard deviation to put everyone on the same scale — but a token whose features happen to be (nearly) all equal has standard deviation (nearly) zero, and dividing by zero is the end of the run. So the formula carries a tiny constant \(\varepsilon\) in the denominator, \(\dfrac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}}\), typically \(10^{-5}\) — invisible in normal use, decisive in the degenerate case. It's the same family of trick as the softmax max-subtraction: a numerically-motivated guardrail that changes the answer by essentially nothing while making the computation never blow up.

Third, a deeper look at why the anisotropic cone forms, and a second cure for it. The crowding isn't uniform — it's driven by token frequency. A handful of rogue dimensions, strongly correlated with how often a token appears, come to dominate every vector and drag them all into a shared direction; rare-word and frequent-word vectors end up with systematically different norms and positions in the cone. Contrastive fine-tuning fixes this by force, but there's a cheaper, training-free alternative worth knowing: whitening — a linear transform (center the vectors, then multiply by the inverse square root of their covariance) that re-rounds the squashed cloud back into an isotropic ball, restoring cosine's dynamic range without touching the model's weights. Whitening, normalization, contrastive tuning: three increasingly heavy hammers for the same anisotropy nail.

Where the modality actually lives

Before the parade of domains starts, locate the seam, otherwise everything is tokens stays a slogan. The block never sees text. It sees a matrix: \(n\) rows, each one a vector of the model's working width. Everything that knows what kind of input arrived happens strictly before that matrix — how the raw input was sliced into pieces, and which single linear map turned a piece into a vector, plus the position saying where the piece sat. After the matrix, no part of the machinery can tell a pixel from a word.

The porting recipe reads straight off that seam. To move the architecture onto a new sense you replace the slicer and that one map, and leave the block untouched: same attention, same normalisation, same feed-forward network, same stack. And conversely: when a new model calls itself a transformer for something, the only interesting question is what it chose as a piece and how it turned that piece into a vector — the rest you already know. The real difficulty lives there too, because in choosing the piece you are choosing what can no longer be attended to separately: inside a piece there are no weights at all.

It also explains how a single contrastive objective can pull two senses into one space. If both towers end at the same contract — \(n\) vectors of one width, pooled down to one — then a loss that only ever sees vectors physically cannot tell that one side was pixels. The beats that follow are not a restatement of that claim but a test of it: patches instead of words, a shared space for images and captions, and the same reading on a graph.

Did you know? The same block sees images

Pause for a Serega did-you-know, because the punchline of this whole chapter is that nothing we built was about words specifically. Take the Vision Transformer (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021). It wants to classify an image — no words anywhere — and here's what it does: chop the image into small fixed-size patches (16×16 in the original ViT), flatten each patch and run it through a linear layer to get a vector, add a positional encoding so the model knows which patch sat where, and then... feed the whole bag into the exact same Transformer encoder block you just learned. Patches are tokens. An image is a sentence of patches. The council convenes; each patch attends to the others; the same multi-head self-attention decides which regions heed which. Not an analogy — literally the same code.

Now stack a second did-you-know on top, because it ties this chapter's other half — contrastive learning — to vision in one stroke. CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) trains an image encoder and a text encoder together, in one shared embedding space, with a contrastive loss that is InfoNCE — the same loss from our scrolly. Show it a batch of (image, caption) pairs; pull each image toward its true caption and push it away from all the others' captions. The result is a space where a photo of a dog and the words "a dog" land at nearly the same point. Sir Cosine doesn't care that one input was pixels and the other was letters — once both are vectors in a shared space, relevance is just nearness, exactly as it was for query and document.

Sit with what that buys you, because it's the bridge to L12. If text and images share one cosine space, then you can search images with a text query — type "a dog on a skateboard", embed the words, and nearest-neighbour over a billion image vectors. The dense-retrieval machine we built this chapter, pointed at pixels. That's the foundation of modern multimodal search, and of document-image retrievers like ColPali that skip OCR entirely and embed the page as a picture. Same encoder, same contrastive loss, same cosine — a different sense organ bolted on the front.

Everything is a sequence of tokens

Here is the unifying frame, and once it clicks you can't un-see it. There's a single sentence that quietly contains the whole architecture: a Transformer is a graph neural network operating on a complete graph. Self-attention is message passing — each token gathers a weighted message from its neighbours — and the "graph" is simply one where every token is connected to every other (that's why it's all-pairs, that's where the \(n^2\) came from). Drop the all-connected assumption and you get a real GNN; keep it and add learned edge weights via softmax and you get attention. GAT — the Graph Attention Network (Veličković et al., 2018) — makes the link explicit: attention weights over a node's actual graph neighbours. Same council, different seating chart.

So watch the same machinery light up across one field after another, each just a different choice of what a "token" is. Audio: wav2vec 2.0 chops a waveform into short time-windows, masks some, and contrastively predicts them — InfoNCE on sound; speech becomes a sequence of audio tokens. Proteins: ESM and ProtBERT treat an amino-acid chain as a sentence in a 20-letter alphabet, run masked-language-modelling over it, and the embeddings put structurally-similar proteins near each other — so homology search becomes vector cosine, quietly replacing the classic BLAST alignment. Genomics: DNABERT and Enformer tokenize DNA into k-mers and attend across tens of thousands of bases to predict gene activity — the same long-context \(n^2\) tension we hit earlier, on a genome. Brain & body signals: EEG and ECG transformers slice a recording into time-windows-as-tokens, and the positional grid you scrolled is already a time axis.

Step all the way back and the whole expedition collapses to one idea: everything is a sequence of tokens in a learned space. Words, image patches, audio frames, amino acids, DNA k-mers, graph nodes, brain-signal windows — pick your sense organ, tokenize, embed, attend, and you get a space where nearness means relatedness. The Council of Attention doesn't care what the councillors are made of. That's why one architecture ate machine learning, and why the search machine we built — embed, then find the nearest neighbour — is not a trick for text but a general way to retrieve anything that can be turned into a vector. Which brings us all the way home, to the creature this whole journey was about.

What the cage is made of

Before you accept the verdict, look at the machinery: neither win over the Gremlin was ever written down as a rule. Polysemy first. A contextual vector is the token's own starting embedding plus the attention-weighted mixture of its neighbours, \(h_i=x_i+\sum_j \alpha_{ij} v_j\). "bank" starts from the same \(x\) in both sentences — the input is identical. What differs is the second term, because the neighbour sets are disjoint: one sentence hands over "river" and "sat", the other "deposited" and "cash". Two identical inputs, two different additions, two separated outputs. No sense inventory, no disambiguation rule, nothing typed by hand.

Synonymy is the half that usually feels like magic, and it rests on the triangle inequality. Contrastive training never puts "couch" and "sofa" side by side; they are simply positives of the same anchor — the same query, or the same document. On the unit sphere the angle between vectors is a metric, so \(\theta(u,u^{\prime})\le\theta(u,a)+\theta(a,u^{\prime})\). Pull both toward the anchor and you have provably pulled them toward each other — transitively, through material in which the two words never co-occur.

That is what the cage is made of: geometry, not a synonym list. And it explains the property that matters for search — the win transfers to pairs the training data never mentioned together. Any two texts that serve the same query get squeezed toward the same region, so the vocabulary gap closes for wordings you have never seen. The Lexical Gremlin wasn't out-argued; he was fenced in by a metric.

The Gremlin, caged

Stand back and look at what the whole expedition just accomplished. Back in First Contact (L1), the Lexical Gremlin wedged a brick wall between every pair of words that meant the same thing but were spelled differently — "couch" and "sofa" scored a flat zero, as unrelated as "couch" and "crocodile". In L6 we cornered him: the static Map of Meaning let synonyms cluster, and "couch" and "sofa" finally drifted into the same neighbourhood. But he had one move left — polysemy, the single frozen vector that couldn't hold "bank"'s two minds. Today we took that move away.

Watch both halves of the win close together. Synonymy: "couch" and "sofa" in similar sentences now produce nearly identical contextual vectors and collapse to nearly the same place — and even the static baseline already separates related from unrelated: on our worked numbers "cat" and "kitten" sit at cosine \(0.639\), much closer than "cat" and "airplane" far off at \(0.365\). Near means related, with no synonym list anywhere. Polysemy: "bank" near "river" and "bank" near "money" now leave the encoder as different vectors, because self-attention rebuilt each from its actual neighbours. The two tricks that defined the Gremlin for six chapters — synonyms-far and senses-collapsed — are both broken. The Gremlin is caged.

That's the big payoff of the whole arc, and it earns a clean statement of how we got here: contextual embeddings from a stacked Transformer, each token rebuilt by multi-head self-attention with positional encoding so order survives, pooled to a sentence vector, then contrastively tuned (InfoNCE, in-batch negatives, loss \(0.1191\) on our example) so nearness means relevance and the anisotropic cone is pried open. The crew finally knows who to listen to. But notice we built a single vector per query and per document — a bi-encoder, fast but coarse. What if, instead, the query and document could attend to each other before we score them? That richer, slower idea — the cross-encoder — and how to combine the two into a fast-then-accurate cascade is exactly where L10 picks up. The council is seated; next, we send it to work on retrieval.
