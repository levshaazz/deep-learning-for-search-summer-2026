The Star Catalog

I'm Serega, and I'm staring at a sky of a billion documents. One of them answers the question on the screen. Just one. The other 999,999,999 are noise, and from where I'm floating they all look exactly the same — tiny points of light, indistinguishable, infinite.

I can't fly past each star and check. Do the arithmetic and it's almost funny: even at a microsecond per document, a billion of them is seventeen minutes of pure looking, per query — and a user gives me about one second before they leave. So I won't search the sky at all. I'll do what every astronomer figured out centuries ago, and what every search engine quietly reinvented: don't search the sky. Search a catalog of it.

That's the whole job today. Build the catalog, then learn to rank what it returns — because finding the right stars and putting the best one first are two different problems, and the second one is sneakier than it looks.

Let's name the naive plan first, so we can watch it die. Read every document at query time and check whether it matches. That's called a linear scan, and it is a complete non-starter. Ten milliseconds to open and read a single document, times a billion documents, is months of wall-clock time for one search. Multiply by the thousands of queries arriving every second and you haven't built a search engine, you've built a space heater.

And the budget you're up against is brutal. A user perceives anything under about 100 milliseconds as instant, gives you maybe a second before they feel the lag, and is gone by two or three. That 100 ms isn't even all yours — the network round-trip, the page render, and the other services in the request each take a slice, so the actual time the ranker gets to think is a fraction of it. A scan that needs months to touch a billion documents has overspent the budget by eight orders of magnitude before it has ranked a single result.

And don't fix it by buying more machines. You can shard the scan across a thousand servers and you've still turned months into hours — for a budget that's supposed to be one second. The problem isn't horsepower. The problem is that you're reading the whole library to answer every question, and the library keeps growing.

We need the answer before we've touched even a thousandth of the sky. That means doing the expensive work once, ahead of time, and turning each query into a cheap lookup against what we precomputed. Hold that thought — precompute once, look up forever — because it's the spine of everything that follows.

The slowest shard sets the clock

The budget was quoted as a single wall, and that made it sound like one machine racing one clock. A real engine does not look like that. The sky is cut into pieces — shards — and a query is sent to all of them at once, each searching its own patch and reporting back. Nothing can be shown to the user until the last reply lands. So the number the user experiences is not the average time a shard takes. It is the maximum over shards: \(L = \max_j L_j\).

That single change of operator rewrites what "fast enough" means. Suppose each shard is slow on some rare, unlucky query with probability \(p\), independently — a cold cache, a garbage collection pause, a neighbour on the same machine. The chance that no shard is slow is \((1-p)^m\) across \(m\) shards, so the chance the user waits is \(1-(1-p)^m\), and it climbs toward certainty as the sky is cut finer. A rare event on one shard becomes the ordinary experience of the whole engine. This is why serving teams quote high percentiles of latency rather than means: a mean tells you about a shard, and the user lives at the tail.

Now push that back onto the design. Each shard's slice of the wall must hold in its bad case, not on average, and it must keep holding as the corpus grows — because adding documents adds shards, and adding shards raises the chance that one of them is having a bad day. A linear scan fails both tests at once: its cost is proportional to the shard's contents, and its bad case is its ordinary case. What we need instead is a structure whose per-query work depends on the query's words rather than on the size of the collection they were drawn from. That is the whole specification of the catalog, and it is why the next move is not a faster scan but a different question.

Build the catalog

Here's the trick, and it's almost embarrassingly simple once you see it. Stop asking each document "do you contain this word?" — that's the question that forces you to visit every document. Flip it around. Keep a list, for every word, of which documents contain it. Ask the question once, store the answer, reuse it forever.

That flipped table is the inverted index. "Inverted" because the natural direction is document → words (a document is just a list of the words inside it), and we've turned it inside out into word → documents. Picture a card catalogue: one card per word, and on each card a column of document IDs — a constellation card pointing straight at its stars. A query stops being a journey across the sky and becomes a single reach for the right card.

What's inside a catalog card

A card with a list of document IDs is the cartoon version. The real thing has more on it, and the extras are what make search fast rather than merely possible. The card splits into two halves: a dictionary that maps each term to where its list lives (and stores its document frequency, df — how many documents contain it, which we'll need for ranking in a minute), and the postings list itself. Each entry in that list — a posting — usually carries not just the document ID but the count of how many times the word appears there (its term frequency, tf), and often the exact positions, so we can later tell that "new york" the city is different from a document that happens to mention "new" and "york" a paragraph apart.

Then there's how the catalog gets built in the first place. You can't sort a trillion postings in memory, so indexers stream the corpus in blocks, sort each block to disk, and merge the sorted runs together — the same merge-sort idea your data-structures course beat into you, just at planetary scale. The two classic recipes have names, both laid out in Manning, Raghavan & Schütze (2008): BSBI (block sort-based indexing) sorts blocks of term-IDs; SPIMI (single-pass in-memory indexing) skips the global sort entirely and grows per-term lists on the fly. Either way the output is the same shape, and that's why production engines like Lucene store the index as immutable segments that get merged in the background: building and serving never stop fighting for the same machine.

And the lists get compressed, hard, with a one-two punch. First, the document IDs on a card are sorted, so you store the first ID and then the gaps — for a list 3, 8, 12, 30 you keep 3, +5, +4, +18, tiny numbers instead of full IDs. Second, variable-byte coding packs each small gap into as few bytes as it needs: one byte carries seven data bits plus a continuation bit, so any gap below 128 fits in a single byte. Run the arithmetic on that list — four 32-bit IDs is 16 bytes naively; four gaps, each under 128, is 4 bytes. That's a clean \(4\times\) compression, and on a popular word's list the ratio is far higher. More of the list fits in cache, so the lookup the user is waiting on touches memory instead of disk. None of this is glamorous. All of it is the difference between a demo and an engine.

Last piece: matching documents to a multi-word query is a merge of sorted lists, and you can make it skip. To find documents containing both "space" and "team", walk the two sorted lists together like a zipper; with skip pointers you can leap over chunks of one list that can't possibly match. Start from the rarest word — its list is shortest, so it does the least work. That instinct, rarest first, is going to come back the moment we start ranking.

A postings list is sorted doc IDs: 3, 8, 12, 30. Stored naively, each is a full 32-bit integer — four bytes apiece, sixteen bytes for four documents, and most of those bits are zero.

Because the list is sorted, keep the first ID and then only the gaps between neighbours: 3, +5, +4, +18. The huge numbers collapse into a handful of tiny ones — same information, far less of it.

Now pack each gap with variable-byte coding: one byte = 1 continuation bit + 7 data bits, so it encodes 0–127. Every gap here is below 128, so each fits in a single byte — continuation bit 0, no second byte needed.

Add it up: sixteen naive bytes became four. That is a clean 4× compression on this list — and on a popular word's much longer list the ratio climbs far higher, so more of it fits in cache and the lookup hits memory, not disk.

Why the skips are spaced by a square root

The merge under every boolean query is embarrassingly plain: two cursors on two sorted lists, look at the two heads, emit the document if they are equal and advance both, otherwise advance the smaller one. One pass, no backtracking, cost proportional to the sum of the two lengths. That is already the good algorithm — you cannot intersect two sorted lists in less than a look at each, unless you are allowed to not look. Skip pointers are exactly that permission: sparse forward links that let a cursor leap over a run of postings which cannot possibly match.

How far apart should the links be? Put one every \(s\) postings in a list of length \(n\) and count the work of one search. You hop from link to link — at most \(n/s\) hops — until the next link would overshoot your target, then walk forward inside the block, at most \(s\) steps. Total \(n/s + s\). Minimise it: the two terms move in opposite directions, they are equal when \(s^2 = n\), and the cost there is \(2\sqrt{n}\). So the textbook's about a square root is not folklore or a tuning result — it is a one-line optimisation, and it is the same shape you get every time a cost splits into a coarse phase and a fine one.

Then the small print, which is where engineering lives. Skips pay when the lists are lopsided — a short list probing into a long one, so most of the long list is skipped over — and pay nothing when both lists are the same length, because almost every posting is a genuine candidate and there is nothing to leap over. They also have to be rebuilt whenever the list changes, which is one more reason a production catalog is written as immutable segments and merged in the background rather than edited in place. Keep the shape in mind rather than the constant: a cheap coarse index over an expensive fine one, tuned so the two phases cost the same. You are going to meet it again with scores in place of document IDs.

Boolean queries and phrases

Before we rank anything, let's see how far the catalog gets us on matching alone — because the merge we just built is more flexible than "both words must appear". The boolean model, the oldest retrieval model there is, maps the three logical operators straight onto set operations over postings lists. AND is intersection: for postings cat → [D1, D2, D3] and dog → [D1, D2], the query cat AND dog returns \([D1,D2,D3]\cap[D1,D2] = [D1, D2]\) — and notice it's non-empty, unlike a disjoint space AND team, which would co-occur in no document, because here the words genuinely co-occur. OR is union: dog OR mouse = \([D1,D2]\cup[D2,D3] = [D1, D2, D3]\), which broadens recall when you'd rather over-return than miss. NOT is set difference: cat AND NOT mouse = \([D1,D2,D3]\setminus[D2,D3] = [D1]\), keeping the cat documents that don't also mention mouse. Three operators, three set operations, all of them a single linear pass over sorted lists.

There's one optimization a real query planner never skips, and it's the same instinct from the skip-merge: for a multi-term AND, intersect the rarest term first — the one with the smallest df. The running intersection can only shrink as you fold in more lists, so starting from the shortest list means every later merge runs against an already-tiny result. Rarest first, again. This is the standard machinery in Manning, Raghavan & Schütze (2008), the inverted-index textbook this whole section follows.

Boolean matching has one blind spot the card-of-doc-IDs can't fix on its own: it can tell you both "new" and "york" appear in a document, but not that they appear next to each other as the phrase "new york". For that you need the positions we tucked into each posting — the positional index. Each posting now carries, per term, the list of token offsets where it occurs: say new → D5:[3, 11] and york → D5:[4, 27]. A phrase query is then a two-stage merge. First the ordinary doc-level AND: both "new" and "york" land in D5, so D5 is a candidate — necessary, but not yet sufficient. Then the positional check: is there an offset \(p\) of "new" and an offset \(q\) of "york" with \(q - p = 1\)? Test \(p = 3\): "york" sits at \(q = 4\), and \(4 - 3 = 1\) — a hit, the phrase "new york" really occurs. Test \(p = 11\): the nearest "york" is at 27, a gap of 16 — not adjacent, a false co-occurrence rejected. Two appearances of both words, but only one genuine phrase.

Generalize that gap and you get proximity search for free: a phrase is the special case \(\text{gap} = 1\); "within \(k\) words" is just \(\text{gap} \le k\). The cost is real — storing every position bloats the index well beyond a plain doc-ID list — but it's the price of telling "new york" the city from a document that merely scatters "new" and "york" across different paragraphs, and exact phrase matching is non-negotiable for names, quotations, and code. Matching, though, is still binary: present or absent, phrase or no phrase. The catalog can tell you which documents qualify. It still says nothing about which are best.

Rarest first is a bound, not a habit

Twice now the rule has been stated as an instinct: when a query needs several words at once, start the intersection from the rarest one. It deserves better than instinct, because it is a bound, and the bound is one line. Intersection is monotone: adding another list can only remove documents, never add them, so \(|A \cap B \cap C| \le \min(|A|,|B|,|C|)\). The running result is a set that only shrinks, and it starts no larger than whichever list you began with.

Now count the work. Fold the lists together left to right, sorted by document frequency ascending. After the first merge you are holding a candidate set \(R\) with \(|R| \le |L_1|\), and it never grows again. Every subsequent list is not walked end to end — you probe it, once per surviving candidate, using exactly the skip machinery from the last beat. So the number of probes across the whole query is bounded by \(|R|\) times the number of remaining terms, and \(|R|\) is capped by the shortest list. Beginning with the rarest term is simply choosing the smallest available cap on every later step. Begin with the commonest and you pay for the same intersection with a candidate set that was needlessly large for the whole rest of the walk.

There is a quiet dependency here worth naming, because it changes how you read the catalog card. To sort the query's terms by rarity, the planner must know each term's document frequency before it opens a single postings list — which is why \(df\) lives in the dictionary, beside the pointer, rather than being counted from the list itself. We introduced \(df\) as an ingredient of ranking, and it is; but it earns its place in the dictionary as an ingredient of planning. A statistic that lets you decide what not to read is worth as much as one that lets you decide what to rank.

What a position costs the catalog

Phrase search was bought with positions, and the price was waved at rather than counted. Count it, because the structure of the bill explains several decisions you will meet in real engines. A plain postings list holds one entry per (term, document) pair: a word repeated forty times in a document still occupies one entry, with a count beside it. A positional list holds one entry per occurrence. So the plain index grows with the number of distinct words per document summed over the corpus, while the positional index grows with the number of tokens in the corpus — which is to say, with the corpus itself.

The ratio between them is therefore the average number of times a word repeats inside a document that contains it: document length divided by distinct terms in that document. On short documents — a title, a tweet, a product name — that ratio sits near one and positions are nearly free. On long documents it climbs, because vocabulary grows far more slowly than length; a book does not contain proportionally more distinct words than a paragraph, it contains the same words again. So the positional index is most expensive on exactly the corpora where documents are longest — and those are the corpora where every other cost is also largest.

Three practices fall straight out of that shape, and they are worth recognising as consequences rather than conventions. Positions are made optional per field, so a title can carry them and a body need not. Phrase queries run as two stages — the ordinary document-level intersection first, positional verification only on the survivors — so the expensive lists are read for a handful of candidates instead of all of them. And the fattest positional lists belong to the commonest words, which is one honest reason engines treat those words specially: not because they are meaningless, but because they cost the most and, as the next act will show, carry the least.

Matching isn’t ranking

Now watch a quieter failure. Ask the catalog for documents that contain both of two query words and sometimes you get back… nothing. A strict boolean AND is brittle: one missing word and a genuinely great document is exiled. Loosen it to OR and you get the opposite disaster — hundreds of documents, all technically matching, none of them ordered. The index tells you which documents qualify. It says nothing about which are best.

Matching is binary; relevance is a spectrum. A document that uses your rare keyword five times in two sentences is almost certainly more on-topic than one that mentions it once in a footnote — and a word that appears in every document tells you nothing at all about which to prefer. So the classic recipe has two instincts baked in: reward rare words more than common ones, and don't let a single word repeated a hundred times drown out everything else. Hold those two instincts. We're about to make them arithmetic.

Bag-of-Words

Before we can weigh words, we need to agree on what a document is to the math. Here's the move that quietly underlies all of classical IR, and it has a name worth saying out loud: Bag-of-Words. Take a document, dump every word into a bag, shake it, throw away the order. All you keep is which words appeared and how many times. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" become the same bag — which is obviously, comically wrong, and somehow works anyway for an astonishing range of tasks.

Why it works: lay every document's bag out as a row of counts over a fixed vocabulary and you've built a document-term matrix — every document is now a vector, a point in a space with one axis per word. Make it concrete with three tiny sentences — "the cat sat on the mat", "the dog sat on the log", "cats and dogs play". The shared vocabulary has eleven words (and, cat, cats, dog, dogs, log, mat, on, play, sat, the), so each sentence becomes an eleven-number row: the first is [0,1,0,0,0,0,1,1,0,1,2] — one "cat", one "mat", one "on", one "sat", and "the" twice. Geometry arrives for free. Documents that share many words sit near each other; a query is just another short vector you can compare against all of them. This is the vector space model — Salton, Wong & Yang (1975) — and it's the bridge from "a pile of text" to "something a computer can rank."

And it's immediately, concretely useful. A spam filter is mostly Bag-of-Words: it doesn't parse your email's grammar, it counts words — "free", "winner", "viagra" push the score toward spam; the words in your actual correspondence pull it back — and a naive probability model on those counts catches the junk with embarrassing reliability. The catch is the one we just admitted: throwing away order means "the movie was good, not bad" and "the movie was bad, not good" look nearly identical to the bag. Order carries meaning, and we just shredded it. We'll spend a whole later chapter buying it back. For now, the bag is enough — because the real signal isn't whether a word appears, it's how much it should count.

What the bag throws away

The bag is a lossy compression, and it pays to name exactly what it loses, because nearly every later chapter is an attempt to buy one of those losses back. The first loss is order. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce byte-identical bags: same words, same counts, opposite events. The partial fix classical IR reaches for constantly is to index n-grams — keep "new york" as its own feature alongside "new" and "york" — which re-injects a window of local order. It works, and it costs: the vocabulary explodes, because there are vastly more word pairs than words, and most pairs occur once and never again.

The second loss is subtler, and it shapes the whole engineering. A real corpus has a vocabulary of hundreds of thousands of terms, sometimes millions — that is the number of axes in the space — while any single document touches a few hundred of them. So every document vector is almost entirely zeros: sparse and high-dimensional at the same time. Write that matrix out densely and you would be storing an ocean of nothing. Nobody does, of course: the inverted index is the sparse representation, holding only the axes a document actually touches. The catalog and the vector space are one object seen from two sides.

The third loss is the one that ends the chapter. The axes are terms, and distinct terms are orthogonal — perpendicular, sharing nothing at all. So "couch" and "sofa" are as unrelated in this space as "couch" and "crocodile": the geometry has no way to express that two axes mean the same thing. That is not a tuning problem you can fix with better weights; it is built into what the axes are. Remember the shape of this hole. The Gremlin comes back to collect on it.

The bag has a second customer

The spam filter went past in a single clause, and it deserves better, because it is the cheapest place to watch a second model get built on the same representation — and watch it arrive at the shape of the ranker we are about to build, starting from a completely different question. Retrieval asks: how well does this document fit that query? Classification asks: which of two classes makes these counts more likely? One bag of word counts, two customers, and their answers turn out to be cousins.

The classifier fits in three moves. Assume each class — junk and real mail — emits words at its own rate, estimated by counting words in a pile of labelled messages. Then the chance that a message came from a class is that class's own prevalence times the chance of each of its words under that class, all multiplied together — the naive part being that the words are multiplied as though they were independent, given the class. Compare the two classes by their ratio and take the log: the product collapses into a sum, one term per word, each term the log of how much likelier that word is under junk than under real mail, counted as many times as the word occurred. A score that is a sum over the words present, each carrying its own weight, decided by statistics of a collection. Hold that silhouette; you will meet it again in an act with no email in it.

Two consequences are worth more than the filter itself. First, a word that occurs at the same rate in both classes has a log-ratio of exactly zero and drops out of the sum — nobody wrote a stoplist, the arithmetic silenced it, and for precisely the reason the ranker will silence its own ubiquitous words: a factor with no power to separate contributes the neutral element. Second, the independence assumption is plainly false — "credit" and "card" travel together, and multiplying them as independent counts the same evidence twice — and the filter is famously effective anyway, because the double-counting lands on both classes and largely cancels in the comparison, which is the only thing that was ever asked for. Remember that trade rather than the formula. It returns a few acts from now wearing the name "independence given relevance", and it is what turns the odds of relevance into a sum over the query's terms.

Documents and queries in one space

Bag-of-Words gives you a row of numbers per document. The vector space model — Salton, Wong & Yang (1975) — adds the single assumption that turns those rows into a search engine: put the query into the same space, on the same axes. A query is just a very short document — a vector that is zero almost everywhere and non-zero on the handful of terms you typed. From the arithmetic's point of view there is nothing special about it any more, and that is the whole trick.

Once both live in one space, relevance becomes geometry. "Which documents match?" turns into "which document vectors lie closest to the query vector?", and closeness is something arithmetic answers with one number per document. That number is what you sort by, and sorting is what ranking is. This is the bridge the last few beats kept promising: from a pile of text, to counts, to a single total order over a billion star-charts.

Two things stay deliberately unspecified, and each owns the beats that follow. The model never says which number goes on each axis — raw counts, weighted counts, anything — and the rest of this chapter is one long argument about that choice. It also never says which notion of closeness you use: the plain reach of a dot product, or the angle between two directions. Those are the next two questions, in that order, and they have different answers.

Why raw counts fail

Try the obvious thing first and watch it fail, because the failure names both corrections we need. Put raw counts on the axes and rank documents by how much of the query they contain. Now take a real document about a space mission and sort its own terms by count: the top of the list is "the", then "of", then "and". The words you actually asked about — "nasa", "shuttle" — sit far below them, buried by grammar. Rank by raw count and every document in the collection is, apparently, about the word "the".

Two independent things are wrong here, and each needs its own correction. Inside a document: repetition is evidence, but not proportional evidence — the tenth occurrence of a word does not carry ten times the news of the first. Across the corpus: a word that appears in every document cannot separate them, however often it appears in any one of them. Neither correction substitutes for the other. Damp repetition and "the" still towers; punish ubiquity and a single passing mention still counts as much as an obsession. That is why the classical weight is a product of two factors rather than one cleverer count.

Notice what both corrections are made of: statistics of the collection, not judgements about language. Nobody hand-writes a stoplist — the corpus itself confesses that "the" is everywhere, and the same procedure will quietly discover that "space" is a stopword inside an archive of space articles. Both numbers can be computed once, at index time, and stored on the term's card right next to its postings list. The catalog was already the right place to keep them; we just hadn't filled in that column yet.

TF-IDF: rare words carry the signal

So we weight the counts in the bag with two ideas that fight in opposite directions. The first is term frequency \(tf\): a word that appears more often in a document is, all else equal, more about that document. The second is inverse document frequency \(idf\) — Spärck Jones (1972) — and it's the clever one. A word that shows up in every document — "the", or "space" inside a corpus that's all about space — is useless for telling documents apart, no matter how often it appears. A word that shows up in only a handful of documents is gold: when it matches, it really means something.

Multiply them — \(tf \cdot idf\) — and you get a weight that rewards documents rich in the query's rare words while shrugging off the common ones. Watch it bite on two documents: A = "The car is driven on the road", B = "The truck is driven on the highway". With $$idf = \log\frac{2}{df}$$ the words they share — "the", "is", "driven", "on" — all have \(df = 2\), so \(idf = \log(2/2) = 0\), and they contribute nothing to either score even though they're most of the text. Only the distinguishing words survive: "car" and "road" (\(df = 1\), idf = 0.6931) carry document A; "truck" and "highway" carry document B. Here \(tf\) is length-normalized — count over document length — so "car" (once in A's seven tokens) has \(tf = 1/7\), and each distinguishing word lands a \(tf \cdot idf = \tfrac{1}{7} \cdot 0.6931\) of about 0.099. The math is doing exactly what your intuition wanted — it's learning which words are worth listening to, from the corpus alone, with nobody labelling anything.

TF-IDF is a genuinely great first instrument, and for years it was search. But it has two flaws you can feel if you push on it. First, \(tf\) is linear: a word that appears ten times scores ten times as high as a word that appears once — but the tenth mention almost never adds ten times the evidence. Second, it has a weak sense of length: a long document accumulates raw counts just by being long, so it can outscore a short, perfectly-on-point one for no good reason. Two flaws, both fixable. Fixing them is the most interesting story in this whole chapter.

Term frequency, and why it must bend

Take the inside-the-document half first. Term frequency is a family, not a formula, and its members differ only in how fast they let repetition count. The raw count \(\mathrm{tf}_{\text{raw}} = f\) is the naive member. Log-tf, \(\mathrm{tf}_{\log} = 1 + \log f\), is the classic damper: each doubling of the count adds the same constant, so climbing from one occurrence to two is worth as much as climbing from eight to sixteen. Augmented tf, \(\mathrm{tf}_{\text{aug}} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\,\frac{f}{\max_t f}\), divides by the largest count in that same document, so every document's own strongest term lands at the top of the scale and a long document cannot win on accumulation alone.

What they share is the point: every useful term frequency is sublinear in \(f\) — concave, flattening as the count rises. The intuition is easy to state and hard to argue with. A word that appears ten times is more relevant than a word that appears once; it is not ten times more relevant. The first occurrence carries the news "this document contains the topic at all", which is an enormous step away from zero. The tenth carries the news "still", which is a small one. A linear weight prices both the same, and that is exactly the mispricing you can feel when a spammy page repeats a keyword forty times and outranks the article that actually explains it.

Hold the shape, because BM25's headline feature is a sharper version of it. The log damper rises forever, just slowly; BM25's factor approaches a ceiling it never crosses, and puts a dial on how quickly it gets there. Same instinct, better instrument — and, as the saga will tell you, arrived at from probability rather than from taste. That difference between a damper somebody liked and a curve somebody derived is the whole reason the next act exists.

IDF: the failure, then the fix

Now the across-the-corpus half — and its edge cases, because the textbook line \(\operatorname{idf}(t) = \log\frac{N}{df(t)}\) is not the line any engine actually runs. Read it at the two extremes. A term in every document has \(df = N\), the ratio is one, and the log is exactly zero: correct and pleasing, the ubiquitous word contributes nothing. But a query term that appears in no indexed document has \(df = 0\), and you have just divided by zero. Users type unseen words constantly — misspellings, product codes, names that entered the language last week — so this is not a hypothetical.

The fix is add-half smoothing: \(\log\frac{N - df + \tfrac12}{df + \tfrac12}\). The halves in numerator and denominator keep the ratio finite at both ends and make it degrade gently instead of exploding. This is the probabilistic, Robertson–Spärck-Jones form, and it did not arrive as a patch — it falls out of the estimation, which is a story a few beats from here. But it carries an edge of its own: once a term sits in more than half the corpus, the ratio drops below one and the logarithm turns negative. A common word would then actively subtract from a document's score for the crime of containing it, and a document could improve its ranking by deleting text.

So production clamps it, by adding one inside the log: \(\operatorname{idf}(t) = \ln\!\big(1 + \frac{N - df + \tfrac12}{df + \tfrac12}\big)\). The ratio can now sink toward zero, but the whole expression cannot fall below the log of one, which is zero. A ubiquitous term contributes nothing — and never less than nothing. That is the form Lucene ships and the idf behind every worked number in this chapter. Take the general lesson with you: the canonical formula and the shipped formula differ by exactly the edge cases somebody hit at three in the morning.

Sir Cosine takes the angle

With TF-IDF weights sitting on the axes, the obvious way to compare a query vector with a document vector is the dot product: multiply the matching coordinates, add them up. It works, and it carries a bias you can predict without running it. A longer document has more non-zero coordinates and bigger ones, so it scores higher for being larger, not for being better. This is the same length problem BM25 will attack from a different direction; here it gets a purely geometric answer, and the knight from chapter two is the one holding the protractor.

Sir Cosine measures the angle instead of the reach: $$\operatorname{sim}(q,d) = \frac{\vec w_q \cdot \vec w_d}{\lVert \vec w_q\rVert\,\lVert \vec w_d\rVert}$$ Divide each vector by its own length and what remains is pure direction — the mix of words a document uses, with the sheer amount of text projected away. A long article and a short note on the same subject point almost the same way, and cosine says so out loud. There is a gift hidden in the arithmetic, too: normalize every document vector once, at index time, and the query-time comparison collapses back to a plain dot product, with no division left in the hot path.

That gives us the cosine ranker, and notice carefully that it is a second opinion rather than a better one. BM25 will deal with document length by a tunable penalty you dial per corpus; cosine deals with it by dividing it out entirely, no dial at all. Two defensible answers to the same question, and on real queries they hand you two different top-tens. Keep Sir Cosine in the room. When the council of navigators convenes, he is the one arguing with the sextant.

Two weightings, one formula

The boxed rule hides a decision nobody has stated out loud: the document and the query do not have to be weighted the same way, and in the classical systems they were not. Every score in this act is a sum of products, one per term — a number computed from the document times a number computed from the query — and there is no law forcing the two factories to run the same recipe. The old systems made that choice explicit and wrote it down, in a code you will meet in half the papers you read: SMART notation, three letters per side, a document triple and a query triple joined by a dot.

Each letter answers one question. How does repetition count on this side — raw, damped by a log, normalised against the document's own loudest term? Does rarity enter on this side at all — is the idf applied here, or left to the other side? And is the vector divided by its own length before the dot product is taken? The famous recipe reads: log-damped counts and cosine normalisation on documents, log-damped counts, full idf and cosine normalisation on queries. Note what is missing from the document side. The idf is applied once, on the query, and not because anyone got lazy.

Read the arithmetic and the asymmetry stops looking arbitrary. The idf of a term is a property of the collection, identical for every document — so multiplying it into both sides squares it, which triples the reward a rare term collects for no reason anyone has ever defended, and quietly changes the ranking. It enters once, and the practical question is only where. Putting it on the query side is the operational answer: a query is short, it is weighted at request time, and the term's document frequency is right there in the dictionary the planner just consulted. The documents, meanwhile, are weighted once at index time, and their vectors can be normalised then and frozen — which, as Sir Cosine already showed, turns query-time similarity into a plain dot product with no division left in the hot path.

So carry the reading rule, not the code. When a paper says it used the classical weighting, ask the three questions per side before you compare it with anything, because two systems that both call themselves TF-IDF can differ from each other more than TF-IDF differs from BM25. And notice that the split does not go away when the formula gets better: BM25 has a document-side factor for repetition and, in its published form, a query-side one as well — the same two desks, still weighting separately. Which of the two you are allowed to ignore is a fact about your queries, and it has its own beat waiting.

What a document is about

Here is a use for TF-IDF that is not retrieval at all, and it is the fastest way to feel that these weights mean something. Instead of scoring documents against a query, take one document and rank its own terms by their TF-IDF weight. The top of that list is a machine-written answer to "what is this about?". Point it at the shuttle document from our corpus and the winners are "shuttle", "crew", "lost" — while "the", "is" and "of" sit at the bottom with an idf of zero, contributing nothing no matter how often they occur. Two statistics, no model, and the summary is recognisably correct.

This is unsupervised keyphrase extraction, and unsupervised is the word to dwell on: nobody labelled a single keyword. The contrast came from the collection. Which also means the answer depends on the collection — and that dependence is a feature, not a defect. "Space" is a superb keyword in a corpus of general news and a useless one in an archive of mission reports, because there it is everywhere and its idf collapses to nothing. The method does not know what is important; it knows what is distinctive here, and most of the time that is what you actually wanted.

In practice it buys you tags, snippet highlighting, facet labels, and the honest baseline any fancier extractor has to beat before you pay for it. Notice the economy, too: the idf column you compute for this is the same column that feeds the ranker — one statistic, computed once at index time, doing two unrelated jobs. That is the catalog earning its keep. A card that answers "which documents hold this word?" turns out to also answer "what is this document about?", read in the other direction.

Rare here is not the same as about

That keyword list was read out with its good rows on top, which is how every demo of this method is presented and how you should stop reading it. Go one row further down the same list and a filler word is sitting there — a bare "maybe", holding a weight in the same league as "crew" and "lost". Nothing went wrong. The method did exactly what it promised, and the promise was never what the demo implied.

The mechanism is worth saying flatly, because it removes the mystery. The rarity factor is computed from one number and one number only: in how many documents the term occurs. It has no access to what kind of word this is — it cannot tell a topic word from a connective, a name from a hedge, a noun from a filler, because none of that is in the input. A grammatically empty word that happens to sit in exactly one document of the collection is, to this arithmetic, indistinguishable from a technical term that sits in exactly one document. What the weight measures is occurs here and almost nowhere else. Most of the time that coincides with what a document is about. It is not the same claim.

There is a second, sharper reason the failure showed up here, and it is about estimation rather than semantics. Document frequency is a count over the collection, and on a small collection it is a noisy estimate of how common the word really is. One accidental appearance can pin a genuinely ordinary word at the smallest document frequency there is, which the formula reads as maximum specificity. Grow the collection and the same word appears in the second document, and the tenth, and its estimated rarity collapses to where it belongs — which is why a procedure that embarrasses itself on a handful of posts behaves respectably on a real archive. Small corpora do not merely give weaker statistics; they give confidently wrong ones.

So put the repair where it can work. Not inside the weight — asking a collection statistic to know grammar is asking the wrong object. Around it: keep only candidates that are nouns or noun phrases, require a candidate to occur in at least a few documents before it is eligible at all, drop a curated list of function words at index time. Every one of those is a filter standing before or after the ranker, and every one of them uses information the ranker structurally does not have. And take the habit that outlives the example: whenever you rank by a statistic, read the top of the list with your own eyes before you believe the method. The average was never the thing you shipped — the first three rows were.

Similarity and near-duplicates

Point the same machinery at two documents instead of one and you get similarity. Weight both with TF-IDF, take Sir Cosine's angle between them, and the number that comes back is driven entirely by the rare terms they share — because the common ones sit at an idf of zero and contribute nothing to either vector. That is the right behaviour, stated as arithmetic rather than as an opinion: two documents whose only common ground is the word "the" have no common ground.

Push that cosine toward its maximum and you have a near-duplicate. Two texts whose TF-IDF vectors point almost the same way are, in practice, almost the same text — which is the basis of deduplication, plagiarism checking, and the crawler's daily chore of not indexing the same wire story from forty mirror sites. Length normalization is what lets it survive real conditions: an article and its shortened reprint have very different vectors and very nearly the same direction, so the angle catches a match that raw counts would have missed.

The blind spot rides along, as it always does. A genuine paraphrase that swaps every noun — "shuttle" for "spacecraft", "crew" for "astronauts" — shares no axes at all, so it can score near zero while any human reads the two as the same story. What we have built measures shared spelling and calls the result similarity. It is astonishingly useful, it runs on two integers per term, and it is not meaning. Hold that distinction; the whole second half of this course is spent closing it.

Why the two factors multiply

The boxed rule is a product, \(tf \cdot \operatorname{idf}\), and the product is doing work that a sum could not. Try the alternative honestly: score a term by \(tf + \operatorname{idf}\) and watch what happens to a word that appears in every document. Its \(\operatorname{idf}\) is zero, so it adds nothing on that side — but its \(tf\) is still there, and it is large, because ubiquitous words are also frequent words. A document would climb the ranking for saying "the" a great many times. Under a product the same term contributes \(tf \cdot 0 = 0\), no matter how loudly it is repeated.

So read the two factors as different kinds of quantity, and the product as the only sensible way to combine them. \(tf\) is how much evidence this document offers about the term: how many times it committed to it. \(\operatorname{idf}\) is what a unit of that evidence is worth in this corpus: a price, set by the collection, identical for every document. Evidence times price is a contribution; evidence plus price is a category error, like adding a distance to a currency. A price of zero silences its evidence entirely, and that is exactly the behaviour we wanted from a word that cannot distinguish anything.

Two things follow that are worth carrying. First, a stop-word list is an optimisation, not a correction: the ranking already gives those words a price of zero, so removing them saves index space and merge time rather than fixing scores. Second, this shape survives every later refinement — BM25 replaces \(tf\) with a saturating function of the count and still multiplies by the same idf, because it is still evidence times price. Keep the test for any new ranking feature you invent: check what your formula does to a signal that carries no information. If a useless term can still raise a score by being loud, you have added where you should have multiplied.

We have a handful of candidate documents. Let's score them against the query — “space”, “team” — and see who wins.

First, weight each query term by how rare it is: inverse document frequency. A term that appears in few documents carries more signal than a common one.

More mentions help — but with diminishing returns. Going from one to two occurrences adds a lot; from ten to eleven, almost nothing. That's term-frequency saturation.

Now correct for document length. A match inside a short, focused document counts for more than the same match buried in a long one. Short doc gets a boost; long doc gets damped.

Putting it together — rarity × saturated term frequency × length normalisation — gives each document its BM25 score. Sorted, that's the final ranking; the top bar is the winner.

BM25, by the numbers

Let me make the figure you just stepped through reproducible with pen and paper, because the whole point of BM25 is that the numbers move the way your intuition wanted — and you should watch them move. Take a toy collection of three tiny documents: D1 = "cat cat dog", D2 = "cat dog dog mouse", D3 = "mouse cat". The query is cat dog. Three docs of length 3, 4, and 2, so the average document length is \(avgdl = (3 + 4 + 2)/3 = 3.0\) — and BM25 uses \(k_1 = 1.5\), \(b = 0.75\).

Start with idf, the rare-word reward, smoothed so it never blows up. "cat" appears in all three documents (\(df = 3\)), so its \(idf = \ln\!\big(1 + \frac{3 - 3 + 0.5}{3 + 0.5}\big) = \ln(1.1429) = 0.1335\) — tiny, because a word in every document barely distinguishes them. "dog" appears in only two (\(df = 2\)), so \(idf = \ln\!\big(1 + \frac{3 - 2 + 0.5}{2 + 0.5}\big) = \ln(1.6) = 0.4700\) — three and a half times heavier. The rarer word already carries most of the signal before we count a single occurrence.

Now the saturating part, the piece that fixes TF-IDF's linearity. Each term contributes \(idf \cdot B\), where the saturating, length-aware factor is $$B=\frac{tf\,(k_1+1)}{tf + k_1\big[(1-b)+b\,\frac{len}{avgdl}\big]}$$ a fraction that rises fast for the first occurrence and then flattens, with the bracket gently penalising long documents. Take it slowly and work one term fully: "dog" in D2, which has \(tf = 2\) and length 4.

The bracket is \((1-0.75) + 0.75\cdot(4/3) = 1.25\), the numerator is \(2\cdot(1.5+1) = 5\), the denominator is \(2 + 1.5\cdot1.25 = 3.875\), so \(B = 5/3.875 = 1.2903\), and the term's weight is \(1.2903 \cdot 0.4700 = 0.6065\).

That single number is two-thirds of why D2 wins.

That was the hardest term; the rest is just bookkeeping. Sum the two terms per document and the ranking falls out. D2 = 0.1161 (cat) + 0.6065 (dog) = 0.7226. D1 = 0.1908 (cat, \(tf = 2\)) + 0.4700 (dog) = 0.6608. D3 = 0.1571 (cat) + 0 (no dog) = 0.1571. So D2 > D1 > D3 — and notice why: D1 says "cat" twice, but saturation means that second "cat" adds almost nothing (0.1908 vs a single-mention 0.1335), while D2's two "dog"s land on the heavier idf and on the saturating curve's steep part. The short, on-point D2 beats the longer-but-repetitive D1, and the barely-relevant D3 trails far behind. That is the sextant working, one number at a time.

One more query nails down the idf instinct, because in cat/dog the two words were close in rarity. Run nasa shuttle over a small real sub-corpus where the terms are genuinely far apart: "nasa" is in three documents (idf = 0.9445), "shuttle" in only two (idf = 1.2809), so the rarer word is the heavier one before any counting. The document that says "shuttle" three times and "nasa" once scores 2.8151 and wins outright; a document with "nasa" twice and "shuttle" once trails at 2.4733; and a document with a lone "nasa" and no "shuttle" sits at 0.9249. The rare term dominates — exactly as idf promised — and saturation keeps the triple-"shuttle" from running away with a tenfold score it didn't earn.

The BM25 saga

That formula you just watched — BM25 — didn't spring out of anyone's head. It's a sextant, tuned over thirty years, each notch filed off only after the previous instrument failed at sea. The "BM" stands for Best Match, and the number 25 is not a version label some marketer chose; it is literally the twenty-fifth variant the team worked through. The story of how they got there is the best engineering parable I know, so let me actually tell it.

It starts in the 1970s with a question that sounds academic and turns out to be the whole game: given a query, which documents are most probably relevant? Stephen Robertson and Karen Spärck Jones formalised it as the Probabilistic Ranking Principle — rank documents by their odds of being relevant — and from it fell out the RSJ weight (Robertson & Spärck Jones, 1976), essentially the idf we just used, born from probability rather than guessed. That first ranker, BM1, only asked is the word present or not. It completely ignored how many times the word appeared. You already know why that's not enough.

So they added term frequency — and immediately hit the linearity flaw, the same one we just found in TF-IDF. Their fix came from a piece of theory called the 2-Poisson model (Robertson, van Rijsbergen & Porter, 1981): imagine a word is "elite" to a document when the document is genuinely about that word's topic, and model the counts as a mixture of two Poisson distributions, elite and not. Work through the math and the optimal weight isn't linear in tf at all — it's a saturating curve that rises fast for the first few occurrences and then flattens. That is the deep "why" behind the shape: the second mention of a word is strong evidence, the fiftieth barely moves you. They didn't bolt saturation on for taste; the probability told them to.

Then length. A long document racks up term counts just by going on, so they normalised by document length — and now they had a fork. Normalise fully and you get BM11; don't normalise at all and you get BM15; and out in the field, neither extreme won everywhere. BM15 over-rewarded long documents, BM11 over-punished them. They even tried a separate length-correction term and a query-frequency factor, measured the lot on real collections, and — this is the engineer's move — threw the correction away when the data said it earned nothing. Tried it, measured it, deleted it. That's the whole discipline in one gesture.

The twenty-fifth variant was the one that stopped the search. Instead of choosing between BM11 and BM15, put a dial between them — a single knob b that slides from "ignore length" to "fully normalise" — and let whoever runs the engine tune it to their corpus. Set \(b \approx 0.75\), let \(k_1 \approx 1.5\) control how fast \(tf\) saturates, and you have BM25: the RSJ idf, a saturating tf, and a tunable length penalty, all in one line. This all came together at City University London on a system called Okapi (Robertson & Walker, 1994), which is why you'll still hear it called Okapi BM25; it then went and won the early TREC competitions in the early 1990s, the bake-off that turned IR from opinion into measurement.

Here's the part that should keep you humble. That worked example you just stepped through ranks D2 above D1 above D3 — a short, on-point document beating a longer one that merely mentions the words — and it does it with arithmetic from the 1990s. On the modern BEIR benchmark (Thakur et al., 2021), averaged across eighteen datasets, BM25 still scores around 0.43 nDCG@10 zero-shot — and plenty of expensive neural models, trained on GPUs that didn't exist when BM25 was finalised, fail to beat it. Thirty years of careful filing produced a baseline so good it's a little embarrassing. Respect the sextant.

The principle under the whole saga

The saga you just read starts with a question posed precisely enough to have an answer: given a query, in what order should documents be shown? The Probabilistic Ranking Principle answers it in one line — order documents by \(P(R = 1 \mid d, q)\), the probability that this document is relevant to this query. And it comes with a claim stronger than "that seems sensible": under its assumptions, that order is optimal. Any other order puts some document above one more likely to be relevant, and the reader can only lose by it. This is why the probabilistic school kept going for thirty years while everyone else tuned heuristics.

The practical consequence is a licence to rewrite the formula, and you should learn to spot when it is being used. Ranking never needs the probability itself, only the order it induces — so you may replace it by any monotone transform without changing a single result. Swap the probability for the odds \(\frac{P(R=1\mid d,q)}{P(R=0\mid d,q)}\): same order, and unlike the probability the odds factorize. Then take the logarithm, monotone again, and a product becomes a sum. Then discard any factor that is identical for every document, because a constant cannot reorder anything. The next beat spends that licence three times in four steps.

One caveat is printed on the tin, and it will matter two chapters from now. The principle judges each document independently: it says nothing about the ten results as a set. Two perfect and identical documents both belong at the top by PRP, even though showing the second one wastes a slot the reader will never get back. No diversity, no redundancy, no memory of what has already been seen. Everything we build in this chapter inherits that blindness, and measurement is where you first feel it.

Where the sum over query terms comes from

Every scorer in this chapter has the same silhouette: a sum over the query's terms, \(\sum_{t \in q}\). TF-IDF has it, BM25 has it, and it is not a design convention anyone chose — it falls out of the odds in four steps. Step one: Bayes flips the odds around into $$\frac{P(R{=}1\mid d)}{P(R{=}0\mid d)} = \frac{P(d \mid R{=}1)}{P(d \mid R{=}0)} \cdot \frac{P(R{=}1)}{P(R{=}0)}$$ Step two: notice that the second factor is a statement about relevance in general, identical for every document — so by the licence from the last beat it cannot reorder anything, and we drop it.

Step three is the load-bearing one: assume terms are independent given relevance, \(P(d \mid R) = \prod_t P(t \mid R)\). Now the likelihood ratio is a product of per-term ratios. Step four: take the log, and the product collapses into \(\sum_{t \in q} w(t)\) with \(w(t) = \log\frac{P(t\mid R{=}1)}{P(t \mid R{=}0)}\). There is the shape, derived rather than chosen. Now estimate that weight when you have no relevance judgements to learn from — the Binary Independence Model — and what drops out is the Robertson–Spärck-Jones weight (Robertson & Spärck Jones, 1976): the smoothed idf from a few beats ago, reached from probability instead of intuition. As a ranker it has a name, BM1, and one glaring gap — it only asks whether a term is present, never how often.

Be honest about step three, because it is plainly false. Terms are not independent: "nasa" and "shuttle" travel together, and seeing one makes the other far likelier. The model is wrong, and the ranking it produces is good anyway, because the errors are shared across documents and largely cancel in the ordering — which is the only thing the principle asked for. Name that pattern and keep it: an assumption you know to be false is a working assumption whenever the quantity you actually need survives it. You will meet the same trade three more times before the course ends.

The half of the formula you never run

The published Okapi formula has a twin that modern write-ups quietly drop, and it is the same idea applied to the other side of the desk. Saturation on the document side answers this document says the word many times, how much should that count? The query side asks the mirror question: the query says the word many times — how much should that count? The answer reuses the same rational shape, \(\mathcal{F}_q = S_3\,\frac{f_q}{K_3 + f_q}\), with \(f_q\) the count of the term in the query and its own constant \(K_3\), and it multiplies the per-term weight just as the document factor does.

The reason you have never had to think about it is arithmetic, not fashion. Fix the scale as the authors did, \(S_3 = K_3 + 1\), and set \(f_q = 1\) — every term appearing once, which is what a keyword query looks like — and the factor is \(\frac{(K_3+1)\cdot 1}{K_3 + 1} = 1\). It disappears without leaving a trace. Okapi was measured on TREC topics, which are paragraph-long descriptions of an information need where terms genuinely do repeat; there the factor earned its place. On short web queries it multiplies everything by one, forever, which is why a whole generation of implementations simply omit it.

It is worth knowing precisely because queries are getting long again. A natural-language question, a user's entire message pasted in, an automatically expanded query, a passage used as a query — all of these repeat words, and they repeat the wrong words most: the connective, the framing, the polite preamble. Score such a query with a library that hard-codes the single-occurrence case and a term the question happens to restate several times will outvote the term that actually specifies what is wanted. The design already contained the discipline; the convenient implementation dropped it. When you change the shape of your queries, go back and check which factors your tooling assumed away.

The constant that makes one mention the unit

There is a choice inside the saturating factor that looks like housekeeping and is actually a calibration. The factor has a ceiling \(S_1\) — the value it approaches as the count grows without bound — and the authors did not leave that ceiling free. They fixed it as \(S_1 = K_1 + 1\). Any positive number would have produced the same shape; only this one produces a scale you can read.

Substitute the most ordinary case there is — a document of exactly average length, mentioning the term exactly once. The length bracket becomes one, so the factor is \(\frac{(K_1+1)\cdot 1}{K_1 \cdot 1 + 1} = 1\), and the term's whole contribution collapses to its bare \(\operatorname{idf}\). That is what the constant was spent on: one mention, at typical length, is the unit of the scale. Everything else is now readable against it. A weight above the term's idf says more than a typical single mention; a weight below it says diluted by an above-average length; and the sign of the comparison tells you which of the two dials did the work.

It also lines the family up on one ruler. The presence-only ancestor scored a term at exactly its idf whenever the term occurred at all — and BM25 now returns exactly that value for the typical single occurrence, more when the document commits harder, less when the document is padded. So the newer ranker is a strict refinement of the older one rather than a rival on a different scale, and the two can be compared cell by cell. Steal the habit rather than the constant: when a formula hands you a free scaling parameter, spend it on making some natural, frequently-occurring case come out equal to one. A formula whose numbers mean something is one you can debug by reading.

The branch they cut off

The family tree of this formula has a branch that did not survive, and it teaches more than the branches that did. During the Okapi runs the team carried a further length correction, usually written \(G\): a bonus for documents shorter than average, scaled by the length of the query. It is not a silly idea — short, dense documents really do get under-credited by a normalisation that only punishes length — and it was in the candidate formula for years.

Look at its shape rather than its motivation, because that is where it differs from everything around it. Every other piece of the score is a per-term factor sitting inside the sum over the query's terms. \(G\) is a single additive quantity per document, sitting outside that sum; it cannot be attributed to any one query term. That breaks the decomposition the serving machinery depends on, because a term-level upper bound — the object the next act will use to skip documents without scoring them — simply has nothing to attach to. A correction that will not decompose per term is a correction you pay for twice: once in parameters, and again in every optimisation it forecloses.

In the end the data settled it without needing that argument. Measured across real collections, the best weight for the correction turned out to be zero, and the team removed it. Read the surviving formula as the residue of that discipline: they added a plausible term, measured it, found it earned nothing, and deleted it — leaving a model with fewer knobs and the same quality, which is strictly the better model. That is why this thing has aged for thirty years without collapsing. Most of what looks like elegance in a long-lived tool is really the shape of everything that was tried and thrown away.

About the topic, or merely mentioning it

The saga claimed saturation came from theory rather than taste. Here is the theory, and it needs no probability background to follow. Fix a term \(t\) and split the collection in two. In some documents \(t\) is elite — the document is genuinely about that topic, so the word keeps coming back on its own, in the summary, in the examples, in the conclusion. In the rest it merely appears in passing: one mention, an aside, a citation someone had to include. Two kinds of document, and two very different regimes for how often \(t\) shows up.

Model the count in each regime as its own Poisson process, and the counts you actually observe as a mixture of the two — that is the 2-Poisson model (Harter, 1975), read as eliteness by Robertson, van Rijsbergen & Porter (1981). Then ask the only question ranking cares about: given the count I observe, how much evidence do I have that this document is in the elite group? Work it through and the answer is not linear in the count. It climbs steeply while the count is still deciding the question, and flattens once the answer is obvious. A single occurrence moves you a long way from "passing mention". The fiftieth tells you what the second already did.

Then comes the engineering move, and it is the one worth stealing. The exact mixture weight is unpleasant to compute and needs parameters nobody can estimate per term on a real corpus. So BM25 does not use it. It uses the cheapest function with the same shape — \(\frac{f}{k_1 + f}\), which rises fast and flattens toward a ceiling — with a single knob standing in for the entire fitted mixture. Derive the shape from a principle, then approximate the shape with something you can afford to evaluate a billion times before breakfast. Most of applied search is that one sentence, executed well.

The two dials, and what turning them does

BM25 hands you exactly two knobs, and knowing what each end of each one means is far more useful than memorising the defaults. \(k_1\) governs how fast the count saturates. Turn it down toward zero and the factor leaps to its ceiling on the very first occurrence: the scorer stops caring how often a term appears and asks only whether it appears — which is BM1 again, the presence-only ranker, recovered as a limiting case of its own descendant. Turn \(k_1\) up and the curve straightens out toward raw linear tf, the exact TF-IDF behaviour the whole act set out to fix. Everything useful lives strictly between the two ends.

\(b\) is the length dial, and it is the twenty-fifth variant's entire idea. At \(b = 0\) the length bracket collapses to one and document length is ignored completely — that is BM15. At \(b = 1\) the correction applies in full — that is BM11. In between it pivots at the average length: a document longer than average is pushed down, a shorter one lifted, and how hard is yours to decide. Two algorithms that had spent years as rival answers turned out to be the two ends of a single continuum, and once you can see them that way there is nothing left to argue about — only something left to measure.

Read that as an instruction rather than a fact about the world. A corpus of tweets or abstracts, all roughly one length, gives the length dial nothing to correct: turn it down and stop paying for a correction that only adds noise. A corpus mixing one-line FAQ entries with book chapters needs it turned up, or the chapters win by sheer accumulation. The same logic runs for \(k_1\): short documents where a term physically cannot repeat much want fast saturation; long technical documents can afford a slower one. The defaults are somebody else's good guess about somebody else's collection. Yours deserves its own answer, measured — which is precisely what the next chapter is for.

Read a benchmark twice

The saga ended on a boast — thirty-year-old arithmetic still holding its own against models trained on hardware that did not exist when it was finalised — and a boast is exactly the kind of claim that deserves a second reading. Put the same systems on two different tables and the ordering flips. On a single large collection that ships with a training set, and with every model trained on it, the dense retrievers beat the lexical baseline clearly and unambiguously. On a family of diverse collections, with each model dropped in having seen none of them, the lexical baseline finishes ahead of the dense model that had just won the first table.

Nothing about either system changed between the two tables. What changed is the question the table asks. The first asks how well can this method fit a distribution it was trained on; the second asks how well does this method carry to a distribution it has never met. Those are different questions, and there is no reason on earth their answers must agree — but a number quoted without its protocol reads as though there were only one question, and that is how a field ends up with two camps each holding a true sentence.

The mechanism behind the flip is worth having, because it generalises past this pair. The classical scorer holds almost nothing that could be overfitted: two dials, set once and rarely touched, and everything else is a statistic — document frequency, average length — recomputed from whatever collection it is pointed at. Move it to a new domain and it re-estimates itself for free, on the way in. A dense retriever's knowledge lives in weights fitted to one distribution, and moving the distribution re-estimates nothing at all. A method with nothing to overfit generalises nearly for free, and pays for that by being equally unable to learn anything from the domain it is standing in.

So keep three questions to ask before you let two numbers stand next to each other. Did each system have in-domain training data, or not? Was the metric computed at the same depth of the list? And — the quiet one that catches people — were the two averages taken over the same set of collections? An average over one family and an average over another are not comparable at all, no matter how identical the metric's name; a model reporting a mean over its own chosen subset is reporting something with no counterpart in the other row. Then take the operational conclusion, which this chapter has already built the machinery for: if one method wins in-domain and another wins out of domain, the honest system does not choose. It runs both and fuses them.

How BM25 serves fast

There's a debt I've been quietly running up, and it's time to pay it. Back at the start I promised the index lets us answer before touching even a thousandth of the sky — but BM25 still scores documents, and a popular two-word query can touch postings lists with millions of entries each. If we computed the full BM25 sum for every one of them, we'd be back inside a slow scan, just with arithmetic. So how does BM25 over a billion documents actually come back in under a hundred milliseconds? The answer is that you never need every score — only the top \(k\), and there's a beautiful trick for skipping the rest.

The trick is called WAND (Weak AND), and it rides on one observation: each query term has a maximum score it could ever contribute — its idf times the largest saturating factor any document achieves, a number you can precompute and store on the term's card. So for any document, you can sum the max-scores of the query terms it contains and get an upper bound on its possible BM25 score, before doing the real arithmetic. Now keep a running threshold: the score of the \(k\)-th best document found so far. The move is ruthless — if a document's optimistic upper bound can't even beat that threshold, it cannot land in the top \(k\), so you skip it without ever computing its true score. Most documents fail this cheap test and are never fully evaluated. As the threshold climbs through the search, the bar rises and more documents get skipped, faster.

BlockMax-WAND sharpens the same blade. Instead of one max-score per whole postings list, it stores a max-score per block of postings — a few hundred entries each — so when a block's local maximum can't clear the threshold, the algorithm leaps over the entire block in one jump, the same skip-pointer instinct we built for boolean merges, now driven by scores instead of doc IDs. The effect is that top-\(k\) retrieval becomes effectively sublinear in practice: you read a small, score-promising slice of each list and confidently ignore the rest. This is the missing half of the latency story from the very first beat. The inverted index lets you find the candidate documents without a scan; WAND and BlockMax-WAND let you rank them without scoring all of them — and together they're why BM25 over Robertson & Walker's (1994) formula meets a hundred-millisecond budget on a web-scale corpus. The classical engine isn't just clever about what it scores; it's clever about how few documents it ever has to.

One footnote on the family, because the production world has a few dialects of BM25 worth knowing by name. BM25F scores documents with multiple fields — a title, a body, anchor text — each with its own length normalization, so a term in the title can be made to count for more than the same term buried in the body. BM25+ adds a small floor \(\delta\) so that very long documents can't have a matching term's contribution decay all the way to nothing. Both are tiny edits to the one formula, both catalogued in Robertson & Zaragoza's (2009) survey BM25 and Beyond — and the fact that thirty-year-old arithmetic still earns careful, named variants in modern engines tells you how stubbornly good the baseline is.

Saturation bought speed as well as quality

The skipping trick you just met needs one thing from the scoring formula, and it is easy to walk past: for every query term, a number that the term's contribution to any document cannot exceed. Without such a number there is no optimistic estimate, without an optimistic estimate there is no safe way to discard a document unseen, and the whole engine falls back to scoring everything. So ask the question the other way round: which scoring formulas even have such a number?

Not the linear one. Under raw term frequency a term's contribution is its idf times the count, and nothing in the world caps the count — a document may repeat a word as often as it likes, so the supremum over documents is unbounded and the precomputable ceiling does not exist. Under the saturating factor it does: the factor rises toward its ceiling and never passes it, so the term's contribution is bounded by that ceiling times its idf. One number per term, computable once at index time, small enough to sit on the term's catalog card beside the document frequency. The property that made the ranking better — repetition stops paying — is the very same property that makes the ranking prunable.

That coincidence is worth generalising, because it recurs. A cheap upper bound is worth nearly as much as the score itself, because it lets you not compute the score. So when you invent a ranking feature, ask two questions early rather than late: does it have a maximum, and can I know that maximum before I look at the document? A feature that answers yes to both can be served at scale; a feature that answers no to either can only be applied to a shortlist someone else produced. This is also, quietly, why the correction the authors cut off would have been awkward even had it worked — a quantity that sits outside the per-term sum has no per-term bound to offer.

One ranker is never enough

BM25 is a workhorse, but it's still one opinion. It scores documents by exact-word overlap, weighted cleverly — that's its lens, and like any lens it has blind spots. A second ranker sees the same documents through a different lens. Take cosine similarity over the TF-IDF vectors we built a moment ago: it cares about the angle between a document and the query, the overall shape of their word usage, not the same things BM25 leans on. Two competent rankers, looking at the same shelf, will hand you two different top-tens.

When two good rankers disagree, the rookie move is to crown a favourite and ignore the other. The smart move is to fuse their votes — to trust a document that both rankers liked more than one that only impressed a single judge. But there's an obstacle, and it's the one the two scorers raise the moment you line them up: BM25 might hand out numbers like 12.4 while cosine lives between 0 and 1. You cannot just add them; the bigger scale would silently win every time. We need a way to combine rankings that doesn't care how loud each ranker shouts.

Two scores, and no common unit

The first instinct when two rankers disagree is to fix the scales and add. Rescale each ranker's scores into a common interval, or centre them to zero mean and unit spread, and then sum. It is a reasonable instinct and it does work, in the sense that it produces a list. What it does not do is make the two quantities commensurable, and the difference between working and being commensurable is where fused rankings quietly go wrong.

Two structural reasons, and neither is fixed by choosing a better rescaling. First, the two scores are different kinds of object. An angle is bounded by construction — it cannot leave its interval no matter what the documents do. A lexical score has no upper bound at all: add a query term and every document's score can grow, and a query of many rare words produces numbers that a two-word query never reaches. Squeezing both into the same interval does not make them the same currency; it hides the fact that they were not. Second, the rescaling has to be re-estimated for every query, because each query has its own score distribution — and the interval version is set by two order statistics, so one document that matches unusually well stretches the range and presses every other document into a sliver near the bottom.

That is the actual case for throwing the scores away and fusing on rank. Not that ranks are elegant, but that a rank is the part of a ranker's output that survives any monotone rescaling the ranker might apply — it is exactly the information that does not depend on someone's choice of units. Be honest about the cost: you lose magnitude, so a fusion on ranks cannot know that one ranker was confident and another was guessing. You gain a rule that needs no per-query estimation and no assumption about anybody's distribution, which is why it keeps working when a new ranker is added to the council by someone who never read your normalisation code.

A council of navigators

There's a whole family of ways to merge ranked lists, and walking the family tree tells you why the winner won. The first instinct is to fix the scales: normalise every ranker's scores to a common range — min-max them into [0,1], or z-score them — and then add. That's CombSUM; its cousin CombMNZ multiplies in a bonus for documents that more than one ranker surfaced at all. It works, but it's fragile: a single ranker with a weird score distribution, one outlier blowing out the min-max range, and your careful normalisation curdles.

So a second school says: throw the scores away entirely and keep only the ranks. Borda count, borrowed from voting theory, gives a document \(m-1\) points for first place, \(m-2\) for second, and so on down each list, then sums — exactly how some elections tally preferences. More elaborate schemes like CPMF model each rank as carrying a probability mass and combine those. The appeal is obvious: a rank is a rank, immune to whatever bizarre scale a ranker reports. The drawback of plain Borda is that it treats the gap between rank 1 and rank 2 the same as the gap between rank 50 and rank 51 — which is wrong, because nobody scrolls to 51.

Which sets up the punchline, and the figure after this beat is it. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, 2009) keeps the rank-only robustness but discounts gently: a document at rank \(r\) in a list contributes $$\mathrm{RRF}(d)=\sum_r \frac{1}{k+r},\qquad k=60$$ summed across every ranker, with a single tiny constant \(k = 60\). Because the contribution shrinks as you go down — \(1/61\) at rank 1, \(1/62\) at rank 2, and so on — being near the top counts for a lot and being deep counts for almost nothing, yet the 60 keeps any single rank-1 from dominating outright. It needs no score normalisation and no per-corpus tuning, and it folds any number of rankers together. Watch the council of navigators take their two disagreeing charts and agree on a course: a document both rankers place first earns \(1/61 + 1/61 \approx 0.0328\) and tops the fused list.

Two rankers, two different orders. BM25 and cosine similarity each return the same documents — but they disagree on the order, and their scores live on scales you can't compare directly.

Forget the raw scores — each list votes with 1/(k+rank), k=60. A doc near the top of a list casts a big vote; a doc lower down casts a smaller one. The same document gets one reciprocal-rank vote from each ranker.

Sum the reciprocal ranks across lists: RRF = Σ 1/(k+rank). This document scored near the top in both rankers, so its two votes add up to the largest combined score.

Sort every document by its summed score and you get the fused ranking. A document ranked well by both lists rises to the top — rank-based fusion is robust to incomparable score scales, no calibration needed.

Now read the full table: two effects rank-based fusion makes visible. A TIE — two docs each ranker saw differently land on the same sum because their ranks just swapped — and an UPSET — a doc only middling in both lists outscores one ranked high by just one. Balanced beats lopsided.

Why a pre-neural trick holds neural search together

RRF was published before the neural era, and the reason it sits on nearly every architecture diagram today is a pattern that did not exist when it was written. The modern default is hybrid search: run a sparse ranker — BM25 over the inverted index we built in this chapter — and a dense one — embeddings, several chapters from here — over the same corpus, in parallel, and fuse their two ranked lists with RRF. You get lexical exactness where the reader's words really are in the document, and semantic recall where they are not, and you pay nothing to reconcile the two.

"Pay nothing" is the load-bearing claim, so make it precise. BM25 scores and cosine scores are not on comparable scales, and their distributions shift per query — so any additive combination has to re-estimate its normalization for every single query, and one outlier result warps a min-max range badly enough to change the order. Ranks have none of those properties. A rank is a rank; the first result of a weak ranker and the first result of a strong one are both called one, and the fusion decides between them by agreement rather than by volume. That is why a rank-only method from before the neural era is the glue holding neural stacks together, and why it is the default in most search engines and retrieval pipelines rather than something cleverer.

Now the failure modes, because a council is not automatically wise. If every ranker shares the same blind spot, fusing changes nothing — ask for "couch" and both the sparse ranker and a poorly-trained dense one miss the same "sofa" document; averaging two identical mistakes returns the mistake. If one ranker is far stronger than the rest, the weak ones inject their noise into its list, and you should weight them down or drop them outright. And because RRF reads only ranks, a ranker that was genuinely certain on one particular query has no way to say so — weighted RRF or a score-aware scheme buys that back when you need it. What makes fusion pay is diversity of blind spots, not the number of voices in the room.

What the shift constant buys, read at both ends

Rank fusion has exactly one free constant: the shift \(k\) added to a document's rank before the reciprocal is taken, so a document at rank \(r\) contributes \(1/(k+r)\). One constant that never needs tuning per corpus is a suspicious thing to be handed, so do what you do with any formula holding a free parameter — read it at its two limits and see what the parameter is actually protecting you from.

Let \(k \to 0\) and the contribution becomes \(1/r\). First place is worth twice second, several times tenth, and the whole fused ordering can be decided by whichever list happens to have put something at the top. A single confident ranker — confidently wrong, perhaps, because it matched a spurious rare word — wins the fusion outright. Now let \(k\) grow without bound: every contribution approaches \(1/k\), the differences between ranks wash out, and the fusion degenerates into counting how many lists a document appeared in at all. The rank information is gone; you are back to a show of hands. Everything worth having lives strictly between the two, and the constant is the dial that says how far down the list the top counts as the top.

That reframes the constant as a statement about depth, not about scores — which is why one value travels across corpora that share nothing else. It is calibrated to how far a reader's attention reaches, and readers are more alike than corpora are. Take the honest limit with you as well: if the consumer of your fused list is not a human scanning a page but a generator that will be handed a fixed number of passages, then the depth that matters is yours, not a reader's, and the constant deserves to be re-checked against it. A parameter that needs no tuning usually means somebody already tuned it — for a use case you should confirm is yours.

The swap fusion refuses to settle

Take the smallest disagreement two rankers can have. Both put the same pair of documents into the same two adjacent slots, and they differ only in which of the two is on top. Fuse them and the two documents come out with the same number — not close, identical. One document collects the reciprocal of the higher slot from the first list and the reciprocal of the lower slot from the second; the other collects the same two reciprocals in the other order. Addition does not care about the order, and that is the entire proof.

Read it as behaviour rather than as arithmetic. A rule that looks only at positions has, in this situation, literally nothing to go on: every vote cast for one document is matched by an equal vote for the other, and the evidence is exactly symmetric. What comes out is a tie, and the order the reader finally sees is whatever the implementation's tie-break happens to do — a document identifier, an insertion order, the iteration order of a hash table. That is not a preference. Reporting it as one is how a demonstration turns into a superstition, and it is why an honest write-up of a fused list says out loud which of its adjacent pairs are ties.

The invariant behind the example is the useful part. A fused score is a sum, over lists, of a function of the document's rank in that list — so it depends only on the multiset of positions the document occupies, and not at all on which list handed out which position. Two rankers swapping a pair leaves each document holding the same multiset it held before, so nothing can move. Turn that around and you have the condition for fusion to actually do something: a document has to gain a position somewhere without another document giving up an equal one — the lists must disagree in a way that is not a permutation of the same slots between the same documents.

Which finally says what a council of navigators is for, in one sentence: fusion does not resolve disagreements, it declines to amplify them. Documents both lists like are lifted; documents only one list liked are pushed down toward the crowd; and a symmetric disagreement is left exactly where it was, because there is nothing in the evidence that would justify moving it. If a tie matters to you — and it does the moment a fixed number of passages is handed to something downstream that will read them all — break it with something that carries information: the raw score of a ranker you trust, a weight on that ranker, a second signal. A tie broken by storage order is a decision made by nobody.

Let the links vote

Everything so far scores a document by what's inside it. But on the web there's a second signal sitting in plain sight, and it's not in any document's text — it's in the links between them. Treat every link as a vote: a page that many others point to is probably worth something. That's the seed of PageRank (Page, Brin, Motwani & Winograd, 1998), the idea that put a particular search engine on the map in the late 1990s. The twist that made it work is recursive — a vote from an important page should count for more than a vote from a nobody. Importance is defined in terms of itself.

That sounds circular, and it is, in the productive way. Picture a bored web surfer clicking links at random forever; the fraction of time they spend on each page, in the long run, is that page's PageRank. Written out, each page's score is $$PR(i) = \frac{1-d}{n} + d\sum_{j\to i}\frac{PR(j)}{\mathrm{outdeg}(j)}$$ where every page \(j\) that links to \(i\) hands over a share of its own score, split evenly across its out-links, and \(d = 0.85\) is the chance the surfer follows a link rather than teleporting. You solve it by power iteration: start everyone equal at \(1/3 \approx 0.333\), apply that update, repeat, and the numbers settle. Run it on a tiny three-page web — A points to B, B to C, C to both A and B — and watch the votes flow. B is the only page with two in-links (from A and from C), so it accumulates the most: after the first pass it already jumps to about 0.475, and as the iterations settle it converges to ≈0.40 (0.397 to be exact). A and C split the remainder — A lands near 0.215, C near 0.388 — and B holds the largest share precisely because two pages, not one, vote for it. One catch worth a footnote: a page with no outgoing links would be a black hole that hoards everyone's score, so the surfer is allowed to randomly teleport (the \(1-d \approx 0.15\) term) to keep the whole thing from collapsing.

The point for us isn't the algorithm; it's the category. PageRank is a query-independent signal — it's about the document, computed once, before anyone searches anything. BM25 is query-dependent; it only means something relative to your words. A real engine wants both, plus a dozen more signals, and that raises an obvious question: how do you combine a lexical score, an angle, and an authority number that live on completely different scales? We just built the answer to that. Let's use it.

Two different holes in the walk

The footnote at the end of the last beat put two different failures under one repair, and they are worth prising apart, because only one of them is what the teleport is actually for. Call the first one the trap: a knot of pages that link only to one another. Follow links forever and the walk falls in and never climbs out; in the long run all of the probability mass ends up inside the knot and every page outside it settles at nothing. This is a reachability failure — the walk cannot get out — and the teleport is its exact cure. Give the surfer a small chance of abandoning the links entirely and landing anywhere at all, and no set of pages can hold him: every page becomes reachable from every other, a unique long-run distribution exists at all, and the iteration has something to converge to. That existence is what the damping constant buys. It is not decoration.

The second failure looks similar and is not. Call it the dead end: a page with no outgoing links at all. Here the walk is not trapped — it stops. Read the update rule literally and this page hands its share to nobody, because the sum it would have contributed to runs over its out-links and there are none. Add up the whole vector after one pass and the total is smaller than it was: the mass that sat on the dead end has simply left the system. Iterate and everything drifts downward together. The teleport does not repair this, and the reason is worth saying precisely — the teleport describes what the surfer does instead of following a link, not what happens when there is no link to follow.

So the second failure needs a fix that conserves mass explicitly, and the two standard ones are the same idea written twice: treat a dead-end page as though it linked to every page, so its share is spread uniformly over the whole graph; or collect the dead ends' mass at each step and re-inject it uniformly. Either way the columns of the update sum to one again, and the total is preserved by construction rather than by luck. The toy graph in the figure has neither pathology, which is exactly why it is a toy — and why a caveat had to be printed under it.

Why insist on this, when the arithmetic looks like housekeeping? Because a real crawl is full of dead ends — a PDF, an image page, a page whose out-links the crawler never fetched — and getting it wrong does not raise an error. It produces a rank vector whose entries all sag together while the order still looks entirely plausible, so the bug survives every review that only ever looks at the top of a list, and surfaces much later as a threshold that has quietly stopped meaning what it meant. Name the invariant and assert it: after every iteration the entries must still sum to one. An invariant you can check in one line is worth more than an intuition you can only argue about.

Three pages, four links: A→B, B→C, C→A, C→B. Out-degrees are A=1, B=1, C=2, so C splits its vote in half. With no prior knowledge the rank vector starts uniform — every page at 1/3 ≈ 0.3333.

Apply the update to one node in full — B. Its in-links are A (whole vote, outdeg 1) and C (half, outdeg 2), with every PR₀ = 0.3333 substituted. The teleport floor is base = (1−0.85)/3 = 0.0500.

PR₁(B) = 0.0500 + 0.85·(0.3333/1 + 0.3333/2) = 0.0500 + 0.85·0.5000 = 0.0500 + 0.4250 = 0.4750. After one pass B already jumps to 0.475 — its two in-links pour in. The bars move.

Repeat the same update for every node, every pass. The vector oscillates with shrinking amplitude — after four iterations it reads (0.1993, 0.4058, 0.3949), already close to where it lands.

After 25 iterations the numbers stop moving: PR = (0.2148, 0.3974, 0.3878). B holds the largest share because two pages — A and C — vote for it, while A and C each have only one in-link.

The ranking is B 0.3974 > C 0.3878 > A 0.2148 — the link graph, not the text, decides. PageRank is a query-independent signal: computed once, about the page itself, before anyone searches anything.

One feature, not the ranking

One correction before we leave the links, because the popular story has it backwards. No modern engine ranks by PageRank. Authority is a query-independent prior on the document — computed once, ahead of every query — so it can help you choose between two pages that match your words equally well, and it can say nothing whatsoever about whether either one matches at all. In a real engine it is one column among hundreds: a text score next to authority next to freshness next to click behaviour, weighed against one another by a learned model. That is learning-to-rank, and it gets its own chapter.

There is a sharper reason to keep it inside a mixture, and he has been waiting behind the catalog for exactly this moment: Goodhart. The instant authority became a ranking signal, links stopped being honest votes about quality and became something worth manufacturing. Link farms, paid links, comment spam, whole networks of sites existing only to point at each other — an entire industry grew up to produce the measurement instead of the thing measured. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, and this is that law demonstrated at web scale, in public, for a decade.

The defence was never a cleverer authority formula. It was refusing to let one number be the ranking: discount suspicious link neighbourhoods, demote the farms, and fold authority into a mixture large enough that gaming any single component buys almost nothing. Take the transferable half with you. A signal you can precompute is genuinely worth having; a signal you rank by is a signal somebody will start manufacturing on Monday. Which is why we convened the council of navigators before this new voice ever arrived — a chorus is much harder to bribe than a soloist.

A page casts one vote, however many links it has

Inside the link-is-a-vote picture there is a detail doing most of the work, and it is easy to read past. A page does not cast one vote per outgoing link. It casts one vote, split across its links — the division by out-degree in the update rule. So the total authority in the sky is a fixed quantity that only ever gets redistributed: what one page gains, others gave up. Authority is a conserved currency, not a resource that grows when people link more.

That conservation is the whole anti-spam property, and it is worth stating as an incentive rather than as arithmetic. Link to everything and you hand each target a vanishing share, so indiscriminate linking is a bad way to manufacture importance — it costs you the value of your own endorsement without giving anyone much. And it makes the recursive definition more than a poetic flourish: a vote from a page that endorses few things is genuinely worth more than a vote from a page that endorses everything, and both facts fall out of the same division.

Now the honest limit, because a conserved quantity is harder to fake and not impossible to fake. Nothing in the arithmetic stops someone from building or buying many pages that each link to few things and all point the same way — you cannot inflate the currency, but you can acquire a great deal of it. That is the arms race, and it is why authority ends up as one feature among many rather than the ranking itself, and why the teleport term matters more than it looks: it puts a floor under every page and keeps any tight cluster from hoarding the whole mass. Carry the shape rather than the algorithm — a conserved score resists manipulation far better than an unconserved one, and no score resists it completely.

Why the walk settles, and how fast

The figure applied one update over and over until the numbers stopped moving. Stopped moving is an observation; here is why it had to happen, and — more useful — what decides how long it takes. Both answers come out of a single question: take two different starting vectors, push each through one update, and ask how far apart they are afterwards.

Split the update into its two pieces and the question answers itself. The teleport piece is the same constant vector whichever rank vector you feed in, so when you subtract the two results it cancels completely and contributes nothing to their distance. What survives is the link piece, and the link piece only moves mass around — every page hands out exactly what it holds, so it can never make a difference larger than the difference it was given. That piece then carries the damping factor in front of it. So one update multiplies the gap between any two rank vectors by at most the damping factor: the map is a contraction. A map that always shrinks distances has exactly one vector it leaves alone, and repeating it from anywhere at all walks you to that vector. Existence, uniqueness and the algorithm, out of one paragraph.

Now the speed, which is the operationally interesting half. The error shrinks by a constant factor at every step, so it decays geometrically, and the number of passes needed to reach a given accuracy is set by the damping factor and by the accuracy you demand — and by nothing else. Not by how many pages there are. A graph of three nodes and a graph of billions need the same number of passes; what grows with the web is the cost of one pass, not the count of them. That is the property that made the whole method affordable at web scale, and it is why the number of iterations is quoted as a small constant in every description you will read.

Which turns the damping constant into a dial with two ends, both worth reading. Push it toward zero and the walk forgets the link graph almost immediately: it converges in a handful of passes, and it converges to something very close to uniform — fast agreement on an answer that carries almost no information. Push it toward one and the score tracks the link structure faithfully, the shrink per step approaches nothing at all, and the pass count climbs without bound; and the closer the walk gets to pure link-following, the more the two failures of the previous beat matter, because it is the teleport that was holding them off. The customary value sits where the structure still speaks and the arithmetic still terminates. Carry the shape rather than the number: whenever an iterative rule is a fixed offset plus a contraction, you get existence, uniqueness and a convergence rate for free — and the constant that damps the rule is the same constant that prices it.

The Lexical Gremlin laughs

There's a catch, and he's grinning at me from behind the catalog. BM25, RRF, PageRank, the whole classical apparatus — every last piece of it matches on exact words. The index is a card per literal token; the scores reward literal overlap. So search "couch" and a perfect document that happens to say "sofa" scores exactly zero. Not low. Zero. As far as the catalog is concerned, those two words are as unrelated as "couch" and "crocodile."

This is the Lexical Gremlin, back from chapter one, and he is thrilled with everything we just built — because no amount of clever weighting climbs over his wall. You can patch a few cases: stemming so "running" and "runs" collapse together, query expansion to bolt synonyms on by hand. But those are buckets bailing a boat. The Gremlin wedges a brick wall between every pair of words that mean the same thing and were spelled differently, and our entire toolkit is built on spelling. To beat him for real we'll have to stop matching words and start matching meaning — give every word a position in a space where "couch" and "sofa" sit as neighbours. That's a later chapter, and it's the one he's afraid of.

The catalog is built

So here's the Ship we built, and it finds fast. An inverted index — gaps-compressed, skip-merged, segment-merged — for instant lookup instead of a doomed scan. Bag-of-Words to turn documents into vectors. TF-IDF to weight the words, then thirty years of BM25 to weight them right. PageRank for authority that needs no query. And reciprocal rank fusion, the council of navigators, to combine all those disagreeing voices into one honest order. It is the baseline every fancy neural method in this course will have to beat — and you'd be genuinely surprised how often it just wins.

Two questions trail us out of here, and each owns a chapter. First, the uncomfortable one: I keep saying "better" and "wins" — but how would we even know? Eyeballing ten results is not a measurement, and BM25's ranking might be quietly worse than I think. That's the next chapter, The Proving Grounds, where we put an honest number on a ranking. Second, the Gremlin is still out there behind his wall, and caging him for good is the chapter after — when words finally get coordinates. The catalog is built. Now we learn whether it's any good, and then we learn to read between the words.
