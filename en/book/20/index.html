The deep field

For twelve lectures the Ship learned one craft after another: to read a corpus, to measure, to rank from BM25 up to the hyperspace lanes, and at last — in the Oracle — to speak a grounded answer and let it be judged. Tonight we sail to the edge of the chart, into the deep field: the region where the most interesting questions live.

Here is one of them. "What field did the founder of Acme Corp study?" The corpus holds both halves — but apart. One document names the founder; another, somewhere else, names the field. No single record answers it. The Oracle, asked for one best passage, comes back empty-handed. Some questions no single record can answer. This is the deep field — and tonight the Ship learns to cross it.

A bag of chunks has no edges

Why does the Oracle fail here? Because naive RAG treats the corpus as a flat bag of independent passages. Embed the query, take the top-\(k\) chunks by cosine, stuff them into the prompt, generate — the whole loop from the Oracle. Nowhere in that loop is there a notion of which entity appears in which document, or that two documents might be linked.

Ask "what field did the founder study?" and the single best chunk — \(d_1\), which names the founder — never mentions the field. The field is one hop away, in \(d_2\). Flat retrieval stops at \(d_1\) and misses it entirely. The question is not a passage you can find; it is a path you must walk: company → founder → field. To answer, the corpus must be navigable, not merely searchable.

Two failures, two frontiers. When the answer is a chain across documents, build a graph of entities and relations and walk it (GraphRAG). When the record is not text at all — an image, a scanned page — search in a shared multimodal space. And once the Ship can answer the deepest questions, it must answer them responsibly. Three frontiers; one deep field.

Why top-k cannot assemble the chain

Call that failure structural, not a matter of tuning, and then prove it — otherwise the obvious objection («just retrieve more») stays standing. Flat retrieval returns \( R_k(q)=\underset{d\in\mathcal{C}}{\operatorname{top\text{-}k}}\,s(q,d) \): every document scored on its own, the set assembled afterwards out of the \(k\) best singletons. What the question actually needs is the best set, \( \max_{S}u(S) \), and the usefulness of that set is superadditive: \( u(\{d_1,d_2\})\gt u(\{d_1\})+u(\{d_2\}) \), because neither half answers anything alone and together they answer completely. A ranking of solo scores has no way to say that. It is not that it says it badly; the quantity it maximises is a different quantity.

And the geometry makes it worse rather than better. The bridging entity \(e\) — the founder — is the very thing that links \(d_1\) to \(d_2\), and it is absent from the query, because if the asker knew it they would not be asking. So \(s(q,d_2)\) is computed against a question that never names the one term \(d_2\) shares with the answer path. Whatever score \(d_2\) collects is accidental similarity, and it competes for its seat against every document that resembles \(q\) more honestly.

Now the objection can be answered exactly. Yes, raise \(k\) far enough and \(d_2\) will eventually be swept in — alongside everything else that looks like \(q\), at no guaranteed depth, and paid for in precision. That buys a chance, not a mechanism, and a pipeline built on chance fails on the questions that matter most. Only two things change the shape of the problem, and both make \(e\) visible instead of latent: ask twice, putting \(e\) into the second query once the first hop has revealed it, or store the edge \(d_1 - e - d_2\) ahead of time so the link is looked up rather than rediscovered. The first is the agentic loop; the second is the graph the next beat builds.

The constellation map

Turn the flat bag into a map. Read every document, pull out the entities it mentions and the relations between them, and store each as a triple — (head, relation, tail). Now the scattered passages become a constellation: nodes joined by edges, and the same entity named in two documents becomes the bridge between them.

Meet Joey Multi-Hop, who never looks for a single record — he leaps along edges. From the company node he follows the founded_by edge to the founder, then the studied edge to the field. The answer that lived in no document at all lives in the path. This fixed walk is a graph-retrieval toy, not the full Microsoft GraphRAG pipeline (Edge et al., 2024). Local search matches query entities, selects graph context and text units, then invokes an LLM. Global search uses query-time LLM map/reduce over precomputed community reports. Offline indexing does not remove this online cost.

Multi-hop, formally

Make it precise. A knowledge graph is \(\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{R})\): nodes \(\mathcal{E}\) are entities, edges \(\mathcal{R}\) are relations, each edge a triple (head, relation, tail) extracted from a document. A multi-hop answer is not a node you retrieve — it is the result of a walk:

$$ \text{answer} = \text{walk}\big(e_0 \xrightarrow{r_1} e_1 \xrightarrow{r_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{r_h} e_h\big) $$

There are \(h\) hops from the start entity \(e_0\) to the answer entity \(e_h\). Our anchor needs \(h = 2\): Acme Corp \(\xrightarrow{\text{founded\_by}}\) Dana Reyes \(\xrightarrow{\text{studied}}\) computer science. This sits on top of the Oracle's flat \(R_k\): plain retrieval finds the documents; the graph's edges chain facts across them. The lift is structure, not more text.

The question asks for the field the founder of Acme Corp studied — but no single document names both the founder and a field of study. Above sit the three retrieved documents; below, the entities they mention wait as faint nodes. Answering will take more than one lookup.

Single-hop retrieval grabs the one best document and stops. It names the founder, Dana Reyes, but says nothing about a field of study — so the answer is simply not in this chunk. Recall against the question lands at zero: the right fact lives one hop away, in another document.

Instead of treating documents as a flat bag, we extract entities and relations and link them into a graph — starting with the chain that answers the question. From d1 the company links to its founder; from d2 the founder links to what she studied. Acme Corp → Dana Reyes → computer science: the two facts that matter, now connected across two documents.

The rest of the graph fills in too: Acme Corp is headquartered in Portland, Dana Reyes studied at MIT, and MIT is located in Cambridge. These context edges are facts the extractor lifted but the question does not need — the full constellation, with the answer-path already laid through it.

Now we walk the graph. Start at Acme Corp, follow the founded_by edge to Dana Reyes, then her studied edge to computer science — two hops crossing two documents to reach the answer node. The fact a single lookup missed sits at the end of a short path, so recall is now full.

Recall, by hand: 0 → 1

Let's grade both strategies on the anchor question with the simplest possible metric: does the answer node appear in what we retrieved? A binary answer-hit indicator, \(\text{recall} = \mathbb{1}[\text{answer node} \in \text{retrieved}]\), with the answer node being computer science. (This is the one-node case, not L5's Recall@k — the fraction of relevant items retrieved; we reuse the word here.)

Single-hop stops at the best document \(d_1\), which contains \(\{\text{Acme Corp},\ \text{Dana Reyes},\ \text{Portland}\}\) — and not computer science. So \(\text{recall} = \mathbf{0}\). The 2-hop walk crosses two documents along the shared entity Dana Reyes: Acme Corp \(\xrightarrow{\text{founded\_by}}\) Dana Reyes \(\xrightarrow{\text{studied}}\) computer science, touching \(\{d_1, d_2\}\). The answer node is now reached, so \(\text{recall} = \mathbf{1}\).

Recall climbs from \(\mathbf{0}\) to \(\mathbf{1}\) — and notice the corpus did not change. The same two documents were there all along. The entire lift comes from the edges: the same records, now navigable. That is the result of this constructed graph-walk example, not a benchmark of Microsoft GraphRAG.

A real model builds the graph

The toy graph was hand-curated — but is the extraction step realistic? We gave llama3.1:8b an open-extraction prompt over the same three documents and asked only: name the entities and the relations between them. No schema, no toy. The model emitted 7 (head, relation, tail) triples on its own:

From \(d_1\), the company — (Acme Corp, was founded by, Dana Reyes) and (Acme Corp, is headquartered in, Portland): 2 triples, including the founded-by edge we need. From \(d_2\), the founder — (Dana Reyes, studied, computer science), (Dana Reyes, studied at, MIT), (Dana Reyes, founded, a company): 3 triples, including the studied edge to the field. From \(d_3\), the school — (MIT, is a, research university) and (MIT, is located in, Cambridge, Massachusetts): 2 more. Total across the three documents: 7 triples.

Then the same 2-hop traversal runs on the model's own graph: Acme Corp was founded by Dana Reyes, who studied computer science. The model named the entities and relations; the graph chained two documents to the answer. Real open extraction, real traversal — the toy was not cheating. (This is a single illustrative run, \(n=1\), of a local llama3.1:8b; the exact triple count can vary run-to-run — what holds is that the model names the entities and the graph chains the docs.)

Local walk vs global summary

Our 2-hop traversal answered a specific question — but some questions are corpus-wide: "what are the main themes across all these documents?" No single walk reaches them. GraphRAG's answer is to cluster the graph into communities and pre-summarise each one, then route a query to one of two modes.

Local search walks the neighbourhood of the query's entities — exactly our 2-hop traversal. Best for specific questions with a clear starting node. Global search is a map-reduce over the graph: in the map step each community summary independently yields a partial answer and a self-rated relevance score; in the reduce step the low-scored partials are dropped and the survivors are combined into one answer. That per-community LLM pass is the cost global search pays for corpus-wide coverage — which is why it is best for broad "main themes?" questions that touch the whole corpus.

The paper's title says it: From Local to Global. The close walk and the bird's-eye view, from one graph. We showed the local walk in detail; the global summary is the same graph, read top-down (Edge et al., 2024).

Two communities, by hand

The local walk answered a specific question; now let me make the global side concrete by partitioning this graph. We have \(6\) entities — Acme Corp, Dana Reyes, Portland, MIT, Cambridge, computer science — and GraphRAG clusters them into \(\mathbf{2}\) communities of \(3\) each.

Community A (the company & its founder) holds \(\{\texttt{Acme Corp},\ \texttt{Dana Reyes},\ \texttt{Portland}\}\) — size \(3\). Community B (the university & the field) holds \(\{\texttt{MIT},\ \texttt{Cambridge},\ \texttt{computer science}\}\) — size \(3\). Sizes \([3, 3]\) sum to \(3 + 3 = 6 = n_{\text{entities}}\): the partition is exact, every entity in exactly one community. What links the two clusters? Dana Reyes's two study edges — \(\texttt{Dana Reyes} \xrightarrow{\text{studied}} \texttt{computer science}\) and \(\texttt{Dana Reyes} \xrightarrow{\text{studied\_at}} \texttt{MIT}\) — both leave A and land in B. That is \(\mathbf{2}\) cross-community edges.

Now route by question type. A broad question — "what are the main themes across these documents?" — is answered by GLOBAL search: read the \(2\) community summaries ("a company and its founder in Portland", "a university and a field of study in Cambridge") and reduce them into one answer. No single record holds the theme; the two summaries do, and global search map-reduces over exactly those \(2\) cards.

A specific question — "what field did the founder study?" — is answered by LOCAL search: drop into the founder's neighbourhood and walk the \(2\)-hop path \(\texttt{Acme Corp} \to \texttt{Dana Reyes} \to \texttt{computer science}\). Notice the second hop crosses the A→B boundary — it rides one of the \(2\) cross-community edges. That is the deep point: the bridge edges are not noise to be clustered away; they are the only structure that lets a local walk leave A and reach the answer in B. Cluster for the bird's-eye summary, but keep the bridges, or the graph fractures into islands no walk can connect.

When the graph earns its cost

A graph is powerful — and expensive to build and to keep current. Don't reach for one by default. Reach for GraphRAG when facts chain across documents (genuine multi-hop), or when the question is corpus-wide — "what are the main themes?" These are exactly the answers flat retrieval cannot assemble.

Skip it when the answer lives in a single passage. Naive RAG from the Oracle is cheaper, simpler, and already correct — a graph would add cost and buy nothing. The costs are real: corpus-wide extraction means an LLM call over every document, the graph needs storage, and worst of all it goes stale — every corpus update must re-extract and re-link.

The lesson rhymes with the Oracle's applicability rule: start flat; earn the graph. Diagnose the question first. If a single passage answers it, you have over-engineered. If the answer is a path no passage holds, the graph has just paid for itself.

Search beyond text

GraphRAG let the Ship reason across records. The second frontier lets it search records that are not text at all. Half the world's information is a picture — a diagram, a scanned invoice, a chart, a photograph. Text retrieval is blind to all of it.

The fix is a single, audacious idea: train an image encoder and a text encoder to map into the same coordinate system, so a photograph and the sentence that describes it land next to each other. This is CLIP (Radford et al., OpenAI, 2021). Once image and text share one space, cross-modal retrieval is just nearest-neighbour search — the very lanes we built in L13, now reaching across modalities. And the knight who measures the distance is one we already know: Sir Cosine.

CLIP, formally

Two encoders, one space. An image encoder \(f_{\text{img}}\) and a text encoder \(f_{\text{txt}}\) map their inputs into the same vector space, so an image embedding and a caption embedding are directly comparable by cosine. Cross-modal retrieval then has a one-line definition:

$$ \text{retrieve}(\text{image}) = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{j}\ \cos\!\big(\mathbf{e}_{\text{img}},\ \mathbf{e}_{\text{txt}}^{(j)}\big) $$

Embed the image, embed every candidate caption, take the nearest neighbour by cosine. That is the same nearest-neighbour search as L13 — argmax cosine, which at scale runs over L13's ANN index — except one side is now a picture and the other its words. The cleanest way to see it work is a cosine matrix of images against captions — and if the space is any good, the diagonal (each image with its own caption) should win every row. Let's compute it.

Both pictures and their captions are embedded into ONE shared space — the same coordinate system holds image vectors and text vectors at once. Down the rows sit three image concepts; across the columns sit the same three caption concepts. The grid is empty for now: every cell will become the cosine similarity between an image and a caption.

Now fill each cell with the cosine of that image against that caption, shaded by value: pale where they are far apart, deep green where they nearly coincide. A bright band runs down the diagonal — an image and its own caption land almost on top of each other — while the off-diagonal cells stay faint. The structure is already visible before we name it.

Take one image row — the cat — and scan it left to right. Cross-modal retrieval is a single act: pick the cell with the highest cosine, the argmax of the row. The losing cells fade; the brightest one is ringed green — and it is the cat's own caption. That one pick is 'search the captions with a picture' in miniature.

For each image row, the top match is the cell with the highest cosine — and it is the diagonal one, the image's own caption. Highlighted in green, the winning cells form a clean diagonal: every image retrieves the right caption. This argmax-per-row is exactly cross-modal retrieval — searching text with a picture, or pictures with text, inside the shared space.

Why does the diagonal win so cleanly? Average the cosine of the matching pairs and average it for everything else: the matched mean is high and the mismatched mean is low, and the gap between them is wide. These hand-set vectors illustrate the contrastive idea from L7 (Sir Cosine) across modalities. They are not outputs of a CLIP model or a training run.

The diagonal wins

This is an illustrative matrix from manually chosen vectors, not a CLIP forward pass or training run. Three concepts — cat, dog, car — each with an image vector and a caption vector in the shared space. Score every image against every caption by cosine and you get a \(3\times 3\) matrix. The diagonal is each image paired with its own caption; everything off-diagonal is a mismatch.

The cosine matrix \(\cos(\text{img}_i, \text{txt}_j)\) comes out as \(\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0.9974} & 0.6547 & 0.171 \\ 0.6609 & \mathbf{0.991} & 0.2877 \\ 0.1712 & 0.329 & \mathbf{0.9949} \end{bmatrix}\). Take \(\operatorname*{arg\,max}_j\) of each row: the top match is the diagonal every time — cross-modal retrieval is right \(\mathbf{3/3}\). Average the matched (diagonal) cells: \(\overline{\cos}_{\text{matched}} = \tfrac{0.9974 + 0.991 + 0.9949}{3} = \mathbf{0.9944}\). Average the six off-diagonal cells: \(\overline{\cos}_{\text{mismatched}} = \tfrac{2.2745}{6} = \mathbf{0.3791}\). The contrastive gap is \(0.9944 - 0.3791 = \mathbf{0.6153}\).

The gap describes the geometry we constructed. It illustrates the separation contrastive training aims for, but it cannot establish what a trained model would achieve on images, captions or unseen concepts.

Top-1 retrieval, by hand

The matrix told us the diagonal wins on average; now let me retrieve with it, one image at a time, the way a real cross-modal search does. Fix an image, score it against all three captions, sort descending, and read off the top-1. This is just one row of the cosine matrix, ranked.

Take the cat image. Its cosine against each caption is \(\cos(\text{cat}, \texttt{cat}) = \mathbf{0.9974}\), \(\cos(\text{cat}, \texttt{dog}) = 0.6547\), \(\cos(\text{cat}, \texttt{car}) = 0.171\). Sort descending: \(0.9974 \gt 0.6547 \gt 0.171\), so the ranked list is \([\,\texttt{cat},\ \texttt{dog},\ \texttt{car}\,]\) and \(\operatorname{top\text{-}1} = \texttt{cat}\) — its own caption. Repeat for dog: \([\,\texttt{dog}\ (0.991),\ \texttt{cat}\ (0.6609),\ \texttt{car}\ (0.2877)\,]\), top-1 \(= \texttt{dog}\). And car: \([\,\texttt{car}\ (0.9949),\ \texttt{dog}\ (0.329),\ \texttt{cat}\ (0.1712)\,]\), top-1 \(= \texttt{car}\).

Every image's nearest caption is its own caption. Recall@1 counts the fraction of images whose top-1 is correct: \(\tfrac{3}{3} = 1.0\). Three images, three top-1 hits, recall@1 \(= 1.0\) — perfect cross-modal retrieval on this toy.

Read the two beats together and you see the same fact from two angles. The matrix beat measured the contrastive gap — matched cosines near \(1\), mismatched near \(0.38\) — as a property of the whole \(3\times 3\) grid. This beat uses that gap: because matched pairs out-score every mismatch by a wide margin, the sort can't go wrong, so top-1 is the right caption every time. The gap is why recall@1 lands at \(1.0\). A real CLIP space over millions of pairs won't be this clean, but the mechanism is identical: retrieval is a cosine ranking in a space trained so the right pair sits closest.

Sir Cosine, across modalities

Our hand-set matrix illustrates the geometry that contrastive learning encourages. In CLIP, an image and its caption form a positive pair; other batch pairings supply negatives. The objective averages image-to-text and text-to-image cross-entropy. The toy vectors here did not come from a CLIP forward pass or a training run.

The toy matched mean is \(\mathbf{0.9944}\), the mismatched mean is \(\mathbf{0.3791}\), and their difference is \(\mathbf{0.6153}\). These summarize the chosen vectors. Temperature \(\tau\) in \(\exp(\cos/\tau)\) rescales logits: smaller values sharpen the softmax and change gradient weighting, including the penalty for false negatives. They do not change fixed cosines or guarantee a larger cosine gap after training. CLIP learns the logit scale. Sir Cosine still compares angles, now between the image and text encoders' representations.

A real vision model reads the picture

The matrix was a toy. Does a real vision model do this? We ran llava:7b — a genuine vision-language model — on a forced-choice image→caption task over 5 text-free shapes. Text-free is the point: with no letters in the picture, the model cannot cheat by reading; it has to actually see the shape.

For each image, llava is shown the five candidate captions and asked which one fits — "Reply with only the number". It is a generative model, not a CLIP dual-encoder, so there is no image embedding and no cosine here: it reads the picture and the captions and generates its choice \(j^\star\). On all five text-free shapes its pick was the correct caption. Top-1 accuracy \(= 5/5 = \mathbf{1.0}\).

Five out of five, on a real multimodal model, reading pictures rather than letters. The toy CLIP matrix (cosine in a shared space) was the mechanism; here a different kind of model — generative — reaches the same answer by grounding its words in the pixels. Either way, a real vision model genuinely connects images and text. (n=5 is an existence-proof, not a benchmark.)

ColPali: retrieve over the page

The frontier idea pushes further: what if the page itself is the unit of retrieval? The classic document pipeline is OCR → layout parse → chunk → embed — and it is brittle on tables, figures, stamps and scans, where the meaning lives in the layout, not a clean text stream.

ColPali (Faysse et al., 2024) skips all of it. Render each page as an image, embed it with a vision-language model into patch-level vectors, and retrieve with late interaction — a query token attends to its most similar image patch (MaxSim). A figure, a table cell, a stamp — all retrievable without ever transcribing them. And notice the lineage: this is ColBERT's late interaction from L12 (Sir Cosine measuring per-patch), fused with L20's shared image–text space. The two frontiers we built meet in one model. The price comes from that same lineage: late interaction stores one vector per patch (hundreds-to-over-a-thousand per page), so the index is one-to-two orders of magnitude larger than a single CLIP page vector, and MaxSim must score every query-patch × page-patch pair per candidate — the same multi-vector storage and latency tax as ColBERT in L12, now charged per page. That is exactly why, on the menu, ColPali is the most faithful and the most expensive option.

The shared space has edges

One shared space is powerful — and only as good as its training pairs. CLIP unlocks records text never could, but it knows only the concepts it was trained on, and inherits the biases of its image–caption data. Reach for it when the corpus is visual, or when you need text↔image search; reach for ColPali when document layout carries the meaning.

Mind the limits. Fine-grained detail is weak — counting objects, reading exact text, recognising rare concepts. And real cosine matrices are far noisier than our clean \(3/3\) toy; in the wild the diagonal does not always win every row. There is also a modality gap: image and text embeddings occupy offset regions of the shared space, so cross-modal cosines run systematically lower and less calibrated than within-modality ones — tune the retrieval cutoff per modality rather than reusing a text-search threshold (it is also why the toy's matched \(0.9944\) cosines look unrealistically high). The shared space is a map, not the territory.

There is a sharper warning here. A model that can see and answer can also confabulate, and its training pairs carry whatever bias the internet handed them. The more the Ship can do, the more there is to answer for. Power leads straight to responsibility — which is the third and final frontier.

With power, responsibility

The Ship can now reason across records and see beyond text. It is, for the first time, genuinely powerful — and that is exactly the moment to slow down. A system that retrieves and generates does not just answer questions; it shapes what its users come to believe, surfaces whatever its index holds, and speaks with a fluency that hides its uncertainty.

This third frontier has no clever formula and no leaderboard. It is rigorous in a different way: a set of risks you can name precisely, and for each a control you must build. There are four — bias, hallucination harm, privacy, attribution — and they share one spine: ground the answer, cite its sources, and let it abstain. The principle the whole act reduces to is simple and uncomfortable: the captain answers for the Ship.

Hallucination is the harm

Start with the harm that is easiest to demonstrate and hardest to catch. We asked llama3.1:8b about the "Quasar-9 vector database" — a fictional entity that does not exist. There is no right answer; the only honest response is "I don't know."

Closed-book, with nothing retrieved, the model does not abstain. It confabulates concrete features — "a collection of 9000 vectors… large-scale dataset…" — fluent, confident, and entirely invented. To a user, this is indistinguishable from a true answer; that is precisely what makes it dangerous.

Now ground the same question in a retrieved context stating that no such database exists. The model abstains: "I cannot answer this question. The context does not provide any information…" Same model, same question — grounding plus the freedom to refuse turns a confident fabrication into an honest refusal. Hallucination is the harm; grounding plus abstention is the control.

Confabulate vs abstain, by hand

Let's run the safety demo step by step — one model, one fictional entity, two conditions — and read the model's actual output at each step.

Step 0 — the question. \(Q\): "features of the Quasar-9 vector database?" The entity is fictional; the only correct behaviour is to refuse. Step 1 — closed-book. Compute \(\text{answer} = G(q)\) with no context: the model answers from parametric memory alone. Step 2 — measure. \(\text{abstained?} = \mathbf{false}\). It invents features: "a collection of 9000 vectors… large-scale dataset…" — CONFABULATED. Step 3 — grounded. Compute \(\text{answer} = G(q, \text{context: ``no such DB''})\). Step 4 — measure. \(\text{abstained?} = \mathbf{true}\): "I cannot answer this question. The context does not provide any information…" — ABSTAINED.

The two conditions differ in one thing only: whether the model was grounded and allowed to refuse. Same weights, same prompt, opposite outcomes. The safety control is not a smarter model — it is a grounded model, given permission to say "I don't know." (This is a single illustrative run, \(n=1\), of a local llama3.1:8b; abstention can vary run-to-run, but grounding + abstention is the control either way.) One honest caveat: grounding reduces the harm, it does not remove it — a grounded model can still ignore the context, over-claim past it, or mis-cite. That residual unfaithful generation is precisely why L16's faithfulness check runs after grounding, as a separate gate, not instead of it.

Risk 1 — bias

Bias enters a RAG system at two stages and compounds. Retrieval inherits the corpus: a skewed corpus surfaces skewed passages — the recall floor from the Oracle, now an ethical problem. Generation inherits the model: an LLM amplifies whatever its training taught it to say. The retriever picks biased evidence; the generator dresses it in fluent prose.

Multimodal makes it sharper. CLIP's shared space is only as fair as its image–caption training pairs — the limit we just flagged — so a biased pairing distribution becomes a biased retrieval space.

The mitigation is operational, not aspirational: audit the corpora; measure subgroup performance; diversify the sources. Bias you do not measure, you ship. The point is to make fairness a metric you track, the way the Oracle made faithfulness a metric — not a hope you hold.

Risk 2 — privacy / PII

The index is an attack surface. Indexed documents may leak personal or secret data — and once a passage is retrievable, the generator can surface it to anyone who phrases the right query. PII or confidential text swept into the corpus becomes an exfiltration path, and in a multi-tenant system one tenant's query can pull another tenant's records.

Here grounding cuts both ways. The very property that makes RAG trustworthy — it answers from the index rather than from imagination — is exactly what makes a leaky index dangerous: a faithful answer to a forbidden passage is still a leak.

The mitigation: scrub PII; put access control on the index; isolate per tenant. Treat the index with the same care you would the source database it was built from — because, functionally, it now is a queryable copy of it.

Risk 3 — attribution

An answer without sources cannot be verified or contested. The user has no way to tell a grounded claim from a confident guess, and no way to push back. The generator can paraphrase the retrieved context without ever pointing to it — and fluency hides the seam between "the passage said" and "the model decided."

This is the Oracle's citation discipline made ethical. Back in L15 we wrote grounded prompts that demanded citations; attribution is why that was non-negotiable. A claim a user can trace is a claim a user can challenge — and a system that can be challenged is one that can be corrected.

The mitigation: cite the retrieved passages; show provenance; let users inspect. Provenance is not decoration on the answer — it is the answer's receipt, the thing that turns a fluent assertion into a checkable claim.

Risk 4 — the corpus talks back

The four risks so far were about the answer being wrong. The next three are about the answer being turned against you. RAG's defining move — pulling third-party text into the prompt — is also its widest attack surface, because a language model has no type system that separates data from instructions. A retrieved passage that says "ignore your previous instructions and…" can be executed, not quoted. This is indirect prompt injection: the command rides in on the retrieval channel, not from the user, so the attacker needs no access to your system — only for their text to get retrieved.

It composes into something worse. Corpus poisoning plants a handful of documents built to be retrieved — close to a target query for the retriever, persuasive for the generator. Retrieval is argmax, not a vote: to win you only need the top-k for the queries you target, so a niche question is cheap to hijack. And the exfiltration channel cashes it out — an injection tells the model to emit a Markdown image whose URL query-string carries the conversation's secrets; the client auto-loads the image and the attacker's server logs the request. Zero clicks. Poison plants it, injection issues the command, rendering ships the data.

The frame to carry: treat every retrieved passage as untrusted input, and every tool the assistant holds as a loaded weapon. The very grounding that made RAG trustworthy in the last four risks is what makes a hostile corpus dangerous here — the system faithfully does what the text tells it.

The defence stack — in depth, not silver bullets

There is no general solution to prompt injection yet, so the answer is defence in depth — layers stacked so one breach yields neither execution nor exfiltration. Instruction isolation puts retrieved text into a separate, explicitly-marked data block and tells the model never to execute its contents. Input sanitization strips imperative text and hidden prompts at ingestion. A rendering allow-list — images and links only from trusted hosts, plus a Content-Security-Policy — closes the Markdown exfiltration channel. Least-privilege tools — narrow scopes, an action allow-list, a human in the loop for anything irreversible — cap the blast radius. And corpus write control — trust tiers, dedup, ingestion anomaly checks — raises the cost of poisoning.

Every layer has a limit, and naming it is the point. Markers can be spoofed; keyword filters are evaded by paraphrase; the allow-list does nothing about a leak in the visible answer text; tool scoping still permits read-only leaks; a trusted source can itself be compromised. No single control is absolute — which is exactly why you stack them.

So the boundary on this whole act: assume the assistant is fallible and the corpus is hostile, and design so that being right about the content and safe about the channel are two separate guarantees. Grounding buys you faithfulness; it does not buy you security. Those are earned by different layers.

Faithfulness becomes an obligation

These risks share a thread back to the previous lecture. In Judging the Oracle we measured faithfulness — the fraction of an answer's claims that are actually supported by the retrieved context. Here that same metric stops being a score and becomes a guardrail against the hallucination harm.

Keep the two metrics apart. Relevance asks: did we retrieve the right passages? Faithfulness asks: did the answer stay true to them? The Quasar-9 confabulation is a pure faithfulness failure — the answer invented content no passage supported.

So wire it in: run a faithfulness check — an LLM-judge or an NLI model — before the answer is shown, and if it falls below threshold, abstain. That is the whole thread of the last two lectures, tightened: L16 taught us to judge the Oracle; L20 says we are obliged to. Measuring faithfulness was a method; enforcing it is an ethic.

The responsibility framework

Collect the four risks into one framework — each a named failure, each with a control you build, not a hope you hold:

Bias — retrieval and generation inherit corpus and model biases → audit corpora; measure subgroup performance; diversify sources. Hallucination harm — fluent but unsupported claims mislead users → grounding + citations + abstention; faithfulness checks. Privacy / PII — indexed documents may leak personal or secret data → PII scrubbing; access control on the index; per-tenant isolation. Attribution — answers without sources cannot be verified or contested → cite the retrieved passages; show provenance; let users inspect.

Every mitigation is a control you build. And they share one spine: ground the answer, cite its sources, and let it abstain. Faithfulness — whether each claim is supported — is the metric from the Oracle's trial, turned into an ethical obligation. Four risks, four controls, one spine.

The captain answers for the Ship

Here is the misconception the whole act exists to break: "If the model hallucinated, leaked a record, or answered with bias — that's the model's fault. The system designer just wired it up." It is the most comfortable lie in the field, and it is false.

You chose the corpus. You chose the retriever, the prompt, the threshold — and whether the system is even allowed to say "I don't know." Every harm in the framework is a design decision, not an act of God: a leaky index, an unaudited corpus, an answer with no citation, a model never given permission to abstain. The phantom Confabulous fabricates only in the room you left unguarded.

So the principle the whole act reduces to: the captain answers for the Ship. Ground it, cite it, and let it refuse — and when it errs, the responsibility is yours, because the controls were yours to build. This is not a footnote. It is the standard you carry when you take the helm.
