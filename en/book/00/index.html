The Briefing

I'm Serega, and I'll be your guide for the next fourteen weeks. Not your captain — by the end, that's you. Think of me as the slightly-too-enthusiastic co-pilot who's flown this route before and knows where the turbulence is. A word on the cast: I'm the doodle in the embroidered cap who narrates the story. The human actually teaching this course, grading your work, and answering your emails is Albert Nasybullin — his bio and contact live on the Syllabus, and I never speak for him. I'm the mascot; he's the lecturer. Two different jobs.

We're about to cross the Galaxy of Information: an endless ocean of documents, queries, signals and noise, and somewhere in it the one answer a user actually wants. Picture a single user typing four words into a box, hoping that out of a billion candidate documents the machine hands back the one that helps. That gap — between a vague need in someone's head and the right record buried in the dark — is the whole problem we spend fourteen weeks learning to close.

Our job is to build the Ship that finds it — and keeps finding it. Not a one-off lucky hit in a notebook, but a machine that finds the right thing for every user, every query, every day, and doesn't fall over when the corpus grows or the questions get weird. We build that Ship one part per lecture: this week the sensors and archives, next the instruments that measure closeness, then the bridge that ranks and serves answers at scale. By the final defense, I step back and hand you the helm — you take the Ship out alone.

Today is the briefing, so we don't build anything yet. We lay out the galaxy you're about to cross, the four territories that make up the map, the schedule and the rules, and how not to crash. Then we fly.

Search is everywhere

Why care? Because search is the quiet engine under almost everything now. Type into Google and a retrieval system searches an index of a trillion pages in a quarter of a second. Open your phone's photos and search “dog on a beach” — that's retrieval over pixels, not text. Your feed, your shopping recommendations, the autocomplete in your code editor, the support bot that finds the right help article: all of them are search wearing different costumes. Once you learn to see it, you can't stop spotting it.

The newest costume is the one keeping large language models honest. Ask a chatbot a factual question and, left alone, it will sometimes answer with total confidence and zero correctness — it will tell you a horse has eight legs and not blink. The fix is to retrieve real evidence first and let the model answer from that, instead of from its hazy memory — though, as we'll see, the model can still ignore the evidence it was handed, which is why a whole lecture goes to measuring faithfulness. That pattern — retrieve, then generate — is called RAG, and the entire back half of this course builds toward it. A language model without retrieval is a brilliant student who never opens a book; retrieval is the library card.

Here's the catch that makes all of this hard, and it's worth feeling viscerally. A user doesn't hand you their actual need — a rich, fuzzy thing in their head. They hand you a query: a few keywords squeezed through a tiny funnel, most of the meaning lost on the way down. “python” — the snake, or the language? “jaguar” — the cat, the car, or the football team? Your job is to reconstruct the need from the crumbs of the query and find the matching record. Get search right and a billion documents feel like a tidy bookshelf you can pull from in a heartbeat. Get it wrong and the right answer might as well not exist — it's there, it's just unreachable.

The scale of the thing

Two numbers to set the mood — order-of-magnitude figures, not precise counts. The web is on the order of \(10^{12}\) pages — a trillion. And every single day, a large fraction of queries are ones the engine has never seen before — Google has long quoted around 15% — brand-new phrasings, fresh news, typos no one anticipated. Sit with that for a second. You cannot pre-compute the answers, because a meaningful fraction of tomorrow's questions don't exist yet. And you cannot read everything when the user hits enter, because reading a trillion pages takes far longer than the quarter-second a user will wait before deciding your product is broken.

So the naive plans both die immediately. Plan A — “answer every query in advance” — dies on the 15% you've never seen. Plan B — “scan the whole corpus on each query” — dies on the clock: even at a microsecond per document, a trillion documents is over a week of compute for one search — and even sharded across a thousand machines that's still about a thousand seconds — some 15 minutes — per query, far past the budget once you also have to do this for every user at once. Neither brute force works. That's not a footnote; that's the reason this field exists.

That tension — too much data, too little time — is the whole course in one sentence. Almost everything we build is a clever way to dodge it: indexes that let you jump straight to the few relevant documents without touching the rest; cheap models that quickly throw out the obvious junk so expensive models only judge a handful; approximate methods that trade a sliver of accuracy for a thousandfold speedup. Keep that trade-off in your pocket. Every technique in this course is, at heart, a negotiation between how good the answer is and how fast and cheaply you can get it.

Welcome aboard. Here's the whole galaxy we're about to cross — four territories, one route. Every search-and-answer system, ours included, makes these moves.

First territory — The Archives: Get Data. Turn the messy world into something computable — tokens, embeddings, an index. This is where the Ship grows its sensors.

Second — The Instruments: Measure. How close are two meanings? How good is a ranking? Without honest measurement you're flying blind.

Third — The Bridge: Rank. Order the answers and deliver them, at scale, forever — the index, the cascade, the hyperspace lanes that keep the Ship fast.

Fourth — The Oracle: Generate. The Ship stops merely finding and starts answering — retrieve, then speak, grounded in what we found. By Project Defense, you'll be the one at the helm.

The whole machine, on one page

Before the schedule and the deadlines, one picture — because every lecture after today is one part of the same machine, and a part is much easier to learn when you know where it goes. The machine has two rows. The top row encodes: text arrives as a sequence of tokens, a model turns each token into a vector, a stack of layers refines those vectors without changing their shape, and then a step called pooling collapses the whole sequence into a single vector. That collapse is what makes everything else possible — a document stops being a sequence of words and becomes one point you can put on a map.

Now notice that this same encoder gets run in two completely different situations. Run it over your entire collection ahead of time, offline, and what falls out is one point per document: that pile of points is the index. Run it on a user's query, live, and you get one more point. Search, at bottom, is asking which stored point is nearest the new one — and the only reason that is affordable at all is that the expensive half was paid for long before anybody typed anything into a box.

The bottom row is the funnel, and it only ever narrows. The whole index gives way to a shortlist found by retrieval; the shortlist gives way to a handful after a slower, more careful re-scoring; the handful gives way to one answer, written by a language model that was handed exactly those documents to read. Every arrow in that chain costs time and money, and every arrow is a place where you trade how much you find against how long the user waits. Hold both rows in your head, because for the rest of the course the first question about anything new is simply: which box on this drawing does it replace?

What you will be able to do

Course goals are usually written as a list of topics, which is a polite way of promising nothing. So here they are as verbs instead — five things you should be able to do at the end that you cannot do today. Build an end-to-end retrieval-and-answer pipeline, from a raw corpus to a served answer. Argue about classical versus neural retrieval and say, for a concrete case, which one wins and why. Evaluate ranking with the metrics that actually measure ranking, rather than the accuracy score that happens to be easy to compute.

The last two are the ones people underestimate. Design a search system for production — which means holding latency, scale and feedback loops in the same head at the same time, because a model that is right and slow is a model nobody ships. And read a paper in this field and implement it: not admire it, not cite it, implement it. Every technique you will meet started as a paper, and the gap between «I have heard of it» and «I have made it run» is where the whole course lives.

There is a reason the last item on the course is a defence rather than another exam. An exam can check whether you can restate a mechanism. A defence checks the verbs: you show the thing you built, and then you argue for the choices inside it — this chunking, this model, this cut-off — using numbers you measured, against alternatives you actually tried. If you keep that room in mind from week one, every design decision you make on the way there becomes a note to your future self.

Why an index can exist at all

The page before this one said the expensive half is paid in advance, which sounds like a scheduling convenience. It is in fact a hard constraint on the shape of the score. An index can exist only because the similarity score can be torn in two: one half computed from the document while knowing nothing about the query that will arrive, the other computed from the query while knowing nothing about the documents, and at the very end the two halves are merely compared. Separability is not an implementation detail. It is the condition under which an index is allowed to exist at all.

Check it the other way round. Picture a scorer that refuses to split: it reads the query and the document together and emits a single number for the pair (later in the course you meet it under the name cross-encoder). Now nothing about a document can be computed ahead of time, because until a query shows up there is nothing to compute with. Searching a billion documents becomes a billion forward passes of a neural network — per query. Notice exactly what breaks: the index does not get slower, it stops existing, because there is nothing left to put in it.

Out of that one seam comes the whole funnel, and its honest price. A separable scorer is cheap and a little blind: the document was described before anyone knew the question, so the comparison happens between two descriptions that were both compressed in advance. A non-separable scorer is sharp and charges a full pass per pair. The course haggles at exactly this spot, over and over: let the cheap blind scorer run wide and leave a shortlist, then let the expensive sharp one read only the survivors. So about anything new, ask first which side of the seam it stands on.

The flight plan

Fourteen weeks, and the climb is left to right. We start classical: TF-IDF and BM25, inverted indexes, and the ranking metrics (nDCG, MAP, MRR, \(\text{Precision@}k\), \(\text{Recall@}k\)) you'll use to tell a good system from a bad one. This is the baseline and the vocabulary — skip it and the neural stuff is just magic words. Then we go neural: word and contextual embeddings that turn meaning into coordinates, transformers and attention, and bi- and cross-encoders that learn what's relevant instead of just counting matched words. Then we make it survive scale: approximate nearest-neighbor search (HNSW, IVF, PQ), FAISS, vector databases, and the unglamorous production work — quantization, caching, latency — that decides whether your system is usable. Finally we make it answer: RAG, its evaluation (which is harder than it sounds — how do you grade a confident-sounding answer?), and agentic loops that critique and correct themselves.

The week-by-week rhythm is simple. Wednesdays we cover ground in lectures; Fridays we get our hands dirty in labs, building the thing we just discussed. The three assignments chain together into one growing pipeline: A1 is classical IR and metrics (implement BM25, compute nDCG/MAP/MRR, analyze your tokenization choices); A2 is two-stage neural retrieval and learning-to-rank (bi-encoder then cross-encoder, hard-negative mining); A3 is a full RAG system with evaluation and an agentic loop. Each one assumes the last. Fall behind on A1 and A3 will feel like a foreign language.

Now the part to write down. Grading is split 30% continuous and 70% summative. The continuous 30% is three assignments and three labs, 5% each. The summative 70% is the Midterm (20%), the Final (30%), and the Project Defense (20%) — the defense is where you show the system you actually built and argue your design choices with metrics, not vibes. Letter grades: A is \([90, 100]\), B is \([70, 90)\), C is \([50, 70)\), D is \([0, 50)\). The exact dates live on the Syllabus and Schedule pages — bookmark them, because two rules are absolute and I'll say them once: no late submissions (a missed deadline is a zero, not a negotiation), and cheating is a zero-tolerance black hole. Everything else I'll help you through; those two I won't.

Why the weeks come in this order

The order of the schedule is not taste, and it is not history for its own sake — it is a dependency graph. You cannot evaluate a ranking until you have a ranking, so counting-based retrieval and the metrics come first. You will not appreciate what a dense representation buys you until you have personally watched a keyword system fail to connect two sentences that mean the same thing, so the classical half has to hurt a little before the neural half arrives. And you cannot ground a language model in retrieved evidence until you can retrieve, and measure, and tell a good hit from a lucky one — which is why the answer-generating part sits at the far end and not at the fashionable beginning.

Inside a week the rhythm has the same logic. The lecture and the lab sit a couple of days apart on purpose: the idea and the hands belong in the same week, because the gap between hearing a thing and running it is exactly where forgetting lives. The lab is deliberately small enough to finish, and deliberately close enough to the lecture that you can still hear my voice explaining the part you are typing.

Which leads to the one warning worth writing down. Missing a week here is not «being one week behind» — it is standing on the water where the next stone was supposed to be. The first half of the course is the vocabulary the second half speaks in; a term you skipped does not stay politely in its own lecture, it comes back as the word you did not understand in the middle of somebody else's sentence. If a week gets away from you, do not push forward and hope: go back, close it, and then move.

Labs and assignments do different jobs

They look like the same thing — code you write and hand in — and they are not. A lab starts in class, on a short leash: the skeleton is there, the data is there, somebody who knows the answer is in the room, and it is due soon enough that you cannot let it drift. Its job is narrow and honest — to make sure the thing from the lecture has been under your fingers at least once, while the lecture is still warm.

An assignment is the opposite kind of exercise. Longer leash, no skeleton worth the name, nobody standing over your shoulder, and a corpus you did not choose. Its job is to find out whether you can build. And the three of them are not three separate exercises — they are one pipeline growing: counting-based retrieval and its metrics; then two-stage neural retrieval with learning-to-rank on top; then a full retrieval-augmented system with its own evaluation and a self-correcting loop. Each one stands on the one before, which means a lab you skipped is not a small loss — it is the assignment you will later do at half speed.

The practical consequence: treat the lab as the cheapest possible place to be confused. Being confused in a lab costs you an afternoon and a question asked out loud; the exact same confusion, discovered the night before an assignment deadline, costs you the assignment. So run the lab code rather than reading it, break it on purpose, and ask the question in the room while the room is still full.

What each part of the grade is actually measuring

The exact weights live on the Syllabus page, and I would rather you understood the shape than memorised the arithmetic. The shape is this: continuous work — labs and assignments together — is worth less than half the total, and the summative half, the two exams plus the defence, is worth the larger share. That is not a comment on how much the weekly work matters. It is an admission that weekly work is the training, and the training is not what gets measured.

Each instrument is pointed at a different question, and they were chosen so that no single habit passes all four. A lab asks: has this code actually run under your hands? An assignment asks: can you build a working thing on data you did not curate? An exam asks: do you still understand the mechanism with the notebook closed and nothing to copy from? And the defence asks the one question a written exam cannot — can you argue for a design decision, out loud, against a person who will ask why you did not do the other thing, using a number instead of a feeling?

That spread is also the honest answer to «how do I optimise my grade?» You cannot, in any useful sense, because the four instruments disagree about what to reward. Cram and the assignments still have to run; copy and the defence eats you alive; build something beautiful and untested and the exam finds the mechanism you never understood. Any metric that becomes the target stops being a good metric — a lesson you will meet again, formally and painfully, in the lecture on evaluation. The cheapest route through this course really is the honest one.

How to sink (so you don't)

Most people who struggle here do one of two things, and I want to name both so you can dodge them. The first is watching lectures like TV — passive, pleasant, and useless. The slides slide by, everything makes sense in the moment, nothing sticks, and a week later you couldn't rederive a single formula. The cure is to do the work with your hands while it's in front of you: pause, compute the BM25 score yourself on the toy example, predict what the next line of code does before you run it, run the lab instead of reading it. Understanding feels like reading; it is actually doing.

The second is cramming the night before an exam. This fails harder here than in most courses, and there's a specific reason: a search system isn't a fact you memorize, it's a machine you understand. You can't bluff your way through “why does the cross-encoder rerank only the top-k and not the whole corpus?” by having read the slide once at 2am. Either you understand the funnel — cheap-and-broad first, expensive-and-precise last — or you don't, and an exam (and the defense, and any real job) will find out instantly. The knowledge compounds week over week; trying to back-load it into one night is like trying to build the whole Ship the morning of the launch.

Both traps have the same antidote, and it's a single change in the order of your questions. Before you ask “how does this work?”, ask “what problem does this solve?” Every technique in this course is an answer to a specific pain — BM25 answers “how do I rank by relevance cheaply?”, embeddings answer “how do I match couch to sofa?”, ANN answers “how do I search a billion vectors in milliseconds?”. Hold the problem in your head first and the mechanism stops being arbitrary machinery and becomes the obvious, almost inevitable fix. That reframe is worth more than any single algorithm I'll teach you.

What you bring aboard

The prerequisites are short, and I want to say what each of them actually means, because «comfortable with Python» is the kind of phrase everyone nods at and nobody checks. Comfortable means you can read a function somebody else wrote and predict what it returns before running it; you can read a traceback without your pulse changing; and when a tensor turns out to have the wrong shape, your first move is to print the shape rather than to change a number until the error goes away. Not elegance — literacy.

Beyond that: basic machine learning (what training, a loss and overfitting are), a little linear algebra, and a little probability. The linear algebra is the part people brace for and then find anticlimactic — a search engine is, at heart, dot products, matrix products and norms, and if those three words are familiar shapes rather than scary ones you are equipped. The probability shows up mostly as distributions and expectations, and mostly in the ranking metrics.

And the thing you specifically do not need is prior experience with search. Nobody arrives knowing what an inverted index is; that is what the early weeks are for, and they are built assuming you do not. If you are missing one of the pieces above, that is worth a quiet evening in the first week and a question at office hours — it is not worth quietly deciding the course is not for you.

The tool is not the idea

You will spend the term inside a small toolbox: PyTorch for the models, the HuggingFace ecosystem for the pretrained ones, FAISS for searching a pile of vectors quickly, and enough of the Elasticsearch and Solr world to know what a production index looks like from the outside. That list is genuinely useful, and it is also the source of the most common mistake students make about their own learning.

The mistake is to file the course under the tool names — to walk out saying «I know FAISS». FAISS is one implementation of a small number of ideas: how to structure an index so you do not have to look at everything, and how to trade a sliver of correctness for an enormous amount of speed. Those ideas are older than the library and will outlive it; the API you learned will be renamed twice before you retire. Learn the idea and the library is a weekend. Learn the library and the next library is another weekend, forever.

There is an opposite trap, and it catches the more careful students, so let me disarm it too: refusing to touch the tool until you understand the theory completely. That is backwards here. You learn what an approximate index really costs by building one and watching what it loses — the understanding arrives through your hands, not before them. So install the thing on day one, run it badly, and let the theory explain what you already saw go wrong.

How to read this Book

Every lecture in this course exists in three channels, and they are not copies of each other. The slides are the fast channel: one idea per screen, made for a room with a clock in it, and you can reopen them any time. This Book is the slow channel: the same material with the arguments written out at length, the worked examples done by hand instead of gestured at, and the objections answered rather than skipped for time. And the papers at the end of each lecture are the primary source — the place all of it came from.

The Book is written in three languages and it is meant to be poked. Where you see an interactive figure, drag it: the numbers under it are real, they are computed from the same data the slides use, and the fastest way to understand a mechanism is to move a slider until it breaks. Read a chapter before its lecture and the lecture will feel easy; read it after and the lecture will stick. Doing neither is the passive-watching trap under a different name.

One last piece of advice about the reading list, since it is the part everyone skips. The references at the end of a lecture are not decoration and not a flex — they are the shortlist. Read one paper per lecture, badly and incompletely if necessary, and by the defence you will have read the backbone of the field in the order it was built. That habit outlasts every library in the toolbox and every grade in this course.

The shelf this course stands on

The reading list at the end of a lecture is not one pile — it is three different kinds of source, and half of everybody's misery with reading comes from opening the wrong kind. There are practitioner books (Teofili; Turnbull and Berryman; Babushkin and Kravchenko), which answer how do I build this with today's tools. There is a survey (Lin, Nogueira and Yates), which answers what exists in this field, and how do the pieces relate. And there are the primary papers — DPR, SBERT, ColBERT, SPLADE, LambdaMART, HNSW, RAG, Self-RAG, CRAG, RAGAS — which answer what does this method actually claim, and where does it break.

That gives you a rule for which kind to reach for. Stuck on how a thing is built by hand: open a practitioner book. Arriving in a subfield so new you do not even know the names in it: open the survey — it is the cheapest map anyone will ever draw you, and an hour with it saves a week of wandering. Needing to know what a method promises and where it stops working: open the paper, because the honest limits live in its own experiments section and nowhere else. A blog post is a fourth kind: fast, and it inherits the mistakes of whichever of the three it skimmed.

And one reading trick worth carrying separately, because it turns a three-hour paper into a twenty-minute one. Read in three passes: title, abstract and conclusion first, purely to decide whether this is the right kind of source for the right problem; then the figures and tables, because the claim almost always sits in exactly one table; and the method only if you intend to implement it. Inside that table, look at the baseline row before the method row — a result is always a difference, so a paper with a weak baseline tells you nothing, however large its headline number.

Buckle up

That's the briefing. The route is set: Get Data, Measure, Rank, Generate, on repeat, getting deeper each week. You'll cross all four territories, build one part of the Ship at a time, and by the Project Defense you'll do the thing I keep promising — you'll take the helm and fly it yourself, with no co-pilot leaning over your shoulder.

I promise the journey is genuinely fun — and I fully intend to make your life a little miserable along the way, in the way a good coach does. “Miserable” here means stretched, not abandoned: three labs, three assignments, two exams and a defense, all on hard deadlines, with office hours and these materials there to pull you through when it bites. The misery is the training; the find at the end is the point.

So buckle up, crew. First stop: how do we even find one record in a sea of a billion — and then build the machine that keeps finding it forever? See you in The Lost Record.
