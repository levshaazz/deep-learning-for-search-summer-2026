The Lost Record

I'm Serega, and somewhere in a sea of a billion documents there is exactly one record I need. No map, no index card, no helpful librarian. Just me, a query box, and an ocean of bytes. Back in 1982 — before the web even existed — John Naisbitt wrote that we are drowning in information but starved for knowledge. That is the whole problem in one line: the bottleneck was never storing the data, it was finding the one slice that answers the question I have right now. Storage is cheap. Attention is not.

So here is what "finding" actually means, told honestly. In my head I have an information need — a rich, context-soaked want: I'm a second-year student, it's exam week, I want a gentle explanation of how vaccines train the immune system, in English, that I can read in five minutes. Then I squeeze all of that down into the two or three words I type into the box. The average web query is about 2.3–2.9 words long. Everything else — my level, my deadline, my language, my intent — gets thrown away at the door. The system never sees my need. It sees the two words. Ranking is the attempt to reconstruct a rich need from a lossy signal — like guessing the painting from a thumbnail the size of a postage stamp.

And because the need is squeezed through that funnel, the answer can't be a simple yes/no filter. Relevance isn't binary; it's graded. Picture a five-point scale — Perfect, Excellent, Good, Fair, Bad — the same scale human judges use to label search results. A binary filter throws all of that structure away: it can only say "matches / doesn't." A ranking keeps it: the best document goes to position 1, where almost all the attention lives, and the merely-okay ones sink. Users read top-down and rarely past the first few results, so the order is the product. That's the difference between search and a database. A database (SQL) answers "which rows exactly match this predicate?" — the result is a set, order doesn't matter, correctness is exact and verifiable. Search answers "which document best satisfies a need I can only guess at?" — the result is an ordered list, correctness is graded and subjective, and the failure mode is the scary one: it fails silently. A bad ranking still returns ten plausible-looking links. Nothing throws an exception. The user just quietly doesn't find what they came for.

One more reframing before we set sail, because it pays off all term: search and recommendation are almost the same machine. Search is PULL — you have an explicit query, an active need, a loud signal of intent. Recommendation is PUSH — there's no query at all; TikTok's "For You" feed guesses the query you never typed, inferring intent from watch time, dwell, and history. They sit on one continuum, explicit → implicit, and architecturally they converge: both run a candidate-generation → ranking cascade. Recsys is just search where the query is implicit. So everything we learn about ranking transfers to half the internet.

Two missions today, then. First: find it — that's information retrieval (IR). Second, the harder one: build the machine that keeps finding it, for every user, every day, after I've gone to sleep — that's ML system design. Find the needle in the cosmos; then build the thing that keeps finding needles forever.

What an assessor cannot label

The word relevance hides two independent axes, and the second one costs more than the first. Axis one you already have: binary or graded — the five-point judgements that feed \(\text{nDCG}\). Axis two gets named far less often: topical relevance versus situational relevance. Topical is about subject matter — is this document about the thing that was asked? Situational is about fit — does it serve this person right now? Wrong reading level, out of date, behind a paywall, wrong language: a document can land the topic perfectly and still be useless. The axes are independent, and an assessor's grade only ever moves along the first one.

Now push that into your training data, because this is where it bites. An assessor can label the first axis, and only the first. Sitting with a query and a document, a careful person decides how well the document covers the subject; that judgement is repeatable, and the whole assessment guideline is written to keep it repeatable. What that person cannot see is the reader — the deadline, the background, the subscription, the device, the language of this particular minute. So your gold set is not relevance. It is a model of relevance: sharp along the topical axis, mute along the situational one — and every offline number you compute inherits that muteness. Not because assessors are careless: the second axis exists only at the instant one particular person looks at one particular result page.

Two things follow, and both are engineering rather than philosophy. First, two systems can tie on the offline number and separate on live users, because the whole difference can sit on the axis the labels never recorded — the seed of what the second half of this chapter will name outright. Second, whatever the labels cannot say, the system has to carry itself: freshness, language, access rights, reading level and location go in deliberately, as features, filters and business rules, because no amount of judging will put them there. Keep the pair as one question in two halves — how good and good for whom, and when. The first half you measure. The second half you design.

Why you cannot unit-test a ranking

Stand a database next to a search engine and the eye goes to the first difference — a structured predicate against two vague words. The row that actually decides your engineering sits further down: what kind of object comes back. A database returns a set. Order is not part of the answer, so two replies holding the same rows are the same reply, and correctness is a property you can check by comparing. Search returns a ranked list. Permute it and you have a different answer — better or worse — although not one document changed. Order is not presentation. Order is the answer.

Now watch what that costs the moment you try to test the thing. Asserting that the result equals the expected result is a sentence that only parses over sets; for a ranking there is no expected value anyone can write down, because nobody can state the one correct permutation of a thousand documents for a real query. So equality is replaced by two objects standing in for it: a judged set, in which people graded some documents against some queries, and a metric that squeezes an ordering into a single number. And since a metric on one query is noise, the unit of judgement grows again — to a whole query set, read as a distribution. A database test goes green. A search change only ever moves a distribution.

The last difference is the frightening one, and it follows from the first two. A database that cannot answer raises: wrong rows, an error, a stack trace, something loud. A ranking that has gone wrong returns the usual page of plausible links, in the usual layout, at the usual speed — and the user quietly fails to find what they came for. That is silent failure, and it is why the second half of this chapter spends its budget on monitoring rather than on models. In a system whose failures do not announce themselves, the only alternative to measuring is not noticing.

Three products, one retrieval problem

Before we set sail, look at where this machine already runs, because it's the same machine wearing three costumes. Put Google, Ozon, and Yandex Neuro side by side and the surfaces could not look more different — yet underneath each is a query, a corpus, a ranker, in that order.

On Google (web search) the user wants a credible page, the corpus is web documents, and the answer is delivered as ten ordered blue links. On Ozon (e-commerce) the user wants an item to buy, the corpus is product listings, and the answer is a grid of products, ordered. On Yandex Neuro (a generative answer engine) the user wants a direct answer, the corpus is passages pulled from retrieved pages, and the answer is one fluent paragraph with citations. Three wants, three corpora, three presentation layers — but the same pipeline underneath: retrieve candidates, rank them, present the top few. Swap the corpus and the surface; the spine never moves.

Notice the odd one out, because it foreshadows the very end of the course. Yandex Neuro bolts a large language model on top of retrieval. When the retrieval grounding is good, the paragraph is both fluent and correct. When the grounding fails, the model stays fluent and goes confidently wrong — the confident horse with eight legs. An LLM with no retrieval is an eloquent liar; retrieval is what keeps it honest. That coupling — retriever in front of generator — is retrieval-augmented generation, named by Lewis et al. (2020), and it's where this whole expedition lands twelve lectures from now. For today, just hold the shape: every product on this slide is a query, a corpus, and a ranker.

Same problem, different "relevant"

Three products were enough to show that the spine repeats. Now widen the lens, because the word relevant turns out to be a local dialect, and the dialect is what makes each domain hard. On the web, relevant means authoritative and fresh, and the distinctive enemies are spam, scale, and guessing intent. In e-commerce, relevant means will convert — an exquisitely on-topic item that is out of stock is not relevant at all, and the top seller is not automatically the best answer. In enterprise search, relevant means answers a coworker: there is no link graph to lean on for authority, and every candidate must clear an access-control check before it is even allowed to exist for you. In code search, relevant is semantic and literal at once — you want the function that does the thing, and you also want that identifier, spelled exactly. In legal and medical search, relevant means do not miss one, which is why those fields lean on a controlled vocabulary such as MeSH: a term nobody is allowed to invent their own word for.

Now the thing that actually changes the engineering: the cost of error. Web search drifts toward precision, because there is always another decent page and the price of a wrong first result is one wasted click. Legal and medical drift toward recall, because a missed precedent or a missed contraindication is a catastrophe, not a bad session — so the cascade is tuned to let more through and a human does the final filtering. Same pipeline, opposite tuning. Ask this on day one of any search project, before you touch a model: what does a mistake cost here, and in which direction does it hurt more? The answer sets your metrics, your thresholds, and how much latency you are allowed to spend.

What the query buys, and what it costs

The hook put search and recommendation on one continuum — explicit intent at one end, inferred intent at the other — and then walked on. It is worth stopping, because the two ends share an architecture and disagree about almost everything else. Architecturally they converge completely: candidate generation, then ranking, then a short page. What changes is only what stands in the slot where the query stood. Recommendation drops in a user-context embedding — history, dwell, device, hour — and the rest of the machine cannot tell the difference. That is why the cascade, the recall ceiling and the millisecond budget you are about to learn transfer to recommendation without a line of argument.

So price the query honestly, because it is the only real asymmetry. What it buys is enormous. It is a signal of intent that the user declared, dated to this second, and it is the reason an assessor can be asked anything at all: hand a person a query and a document and there is a question with an answer. Take the query away and the assessor's question evaporates — «is this good for this user right now» is not a task you can hand to a stranger, which is why recommendation leans so much harder on behaviour and so much less on judgement. What the query costs is the compression from the hook: a rich need squeezed into two words, with the user doing the squeezing and the system never seeing the original.

Two places where the ends genuinely part, both worth carrying. First, the empty state. Search's hard case is a document nobody has interacted with; recommendation's hard case is a person nobody has observed — and the second is worse, because without history there is no query to construct at all, whereas a query typed by a brand-new user works perfectly. Second, exposure. Search always has the typed words as an anchor outside its own logs; recommendation has nothing but what it chose to show, so everything it learns it learned from its own past decisions. Hold that thought — the loop it creates is the trap this chapter closes on, and the push end of the continuum lives inside it permanently.

The Lexical Gremlin

First obstacle, and he's grinning at me: the Lexical Gremlin. He's a small, smug creature whose entire job is to wedge a brick wall between words that mean the same thing. You search "couch"; the perfect document says "sofa"; the Gremlin makes sure the two never meet. Exact-match search — the kind that just checks whether the document literally contains your query words — loses to a synonym every single time.

This isn't a rare edge case; it's the number-one reason search is hard, and it has a name and a number. Furnas et al. (1987) measured it (the vocabulary problem): ask two people to name the same thing and they pick the same word less than 20% of the time. "Couch" / "sofa" / "settee." "Soda" / "pop" / "fizzy drink." "Joggers" / "sweatpants." The person who wrote the document and the person searching for it almost never agree on the words — so a system that only matches words is wrong by construction most of the time.

The Gremlin attacks from two directions at once, and it helps to keep them straight. Synonymy is the first blade: a relevant document shares zero words with your query, so you never retrieve it — that's lost recall. Search "comfortable couch," the best result reads "plush sofa, sleeps two," overlap is exactly zero words, and yet it's plainly the right answer. Polysemy is the second blade: an irrelevant document shares your query word, so junk floods in — that's lost precision. Search "jaguar" and the word fans out into a cat, a car, and an operating system; search "python" and it's a language or a snake. One word, several meanings, and exact match can't tell them apart.

Which kills a tempting belief students arrive with: "if a document contains all my query words it must be relevant, and if it's missing one it can't be." Both halves are false. Term overlap is neither necessary nor sufficient for relevance. Not sufficient: a page that reads "java java coffee java cup java" contains the query "java" perfectly (every query word present) yet is pure keyword-stuffed spam. Not necessary: the plush-sofa page answers "comfortable couch" with no shared words at all. Term overlap is a weak, biased proxy for relevance — useful, cheap, but leaky.

And the damage is measurable in money, not just theory. On e-commerce sites, 10–20% of on-site searches return zero results — and the usual culprit isn't empty shelves, it's the Gremlin: the shopper typed "joggers" and the catalogue only knows "sweatpants." Run a store with 100k searches a month and a 10% zero-result rate, and that's 10,000 high-intent visitors hitting a wall every month. The fix is almost never more inventory; it's teaching the system that those two words mean the same thing. Hold onto the Gremlin — he's the villain who explains why we eventually have to give words real meaning instead of just matching letters. We'll come back to cage him.

A galaxy too big to read

And the scale is genuinely absurd. The web is on the order of \(10^{12}\) pages — a trillion. You cannot read everything at query time; a single brute-force scan of the corpus per query would melt any machine and blow every deadline. So the first instinct — "just pre-compute the answer to every query" — is dead on arrival, and here's the killer fact that kills it: every day, roughly 15% of the queries a big engine sees are ones it has never seen before. (Google has said this for years — about 25% novel back in 2007, holding near 15% since.) At ~8.5 billion queries a day, 15% novel is on the order of 1.3 billion brand-new queries every single day. You can't have a cached answer for a query that didn't exist this morning.

Why doesn't the novelty ever run out? Because language is Zipfian. Draw query frequency as a beach: a few "head" queries — youtube, weather, facebook login — tower up like sandcastles and soak up enormous volume, while behind them stretches an endless tail of weird, specific, one-off queries that each get searched once and then maybe never again. error code 0x80070643 windows 11 update fix. why does my sourdough smell like acetone. The tail isn't a small leftover — most of the distinct queries, and a huge share of total intent, live out there. You cannot memorise a tail with no end. You must generalise — and that, in one sentence, is the statistical reason search needs machine learning rather than a giant lookup table.

On top of sheer count, the kind of want changes from query to query, which is why one ranking can't be right for all of them. Broder's (2002) classic taxonomy (from AltaVista logs) splits intent three ways. Navigational (~24.5%): you want one specific place — facebook login — and "good" means the single right answer at position 1; anything else is failure. Informational (~39%): how vaccines work — you want to learn, so several good, diverse documents beat one; coverage matters more than a single winner. Transactional (~36%): buy iphone 15 — you want to do something, and "good" means enabling the action. The exact ranking that nails a navigational query is wrong for an informational one. (And note: the percentages wobble between studies, because labelling intent is itself fuzzy — which is exactly why we eventually learn intent from behaviour instead of hand-labelling it.)

So tally up what the system is actually up against: a trillion documents, a billion never-before-seen queries a day, a vocabulary where nobody agrees on words, and a user who told you almost nothing. You cannot pre-compute answers; you cannot read everything at query time; you cannot even rely on the words matching. Something has to give. The way out is not a bigger computer — it's a smarter shape.

The world keeps moving

The Gremlin is the loudest problem, but he is not the only one on board. Start with the query itself, because it arrives in worse shape than any tutorial admits. It is short — a couple of words, almost no context. It is misspelled — typos are not an edge case, they are the weather. And it is ambiguous: python is a language or a snake, and the two audiences want opposite corpora. Each of those is a future engineering project with a name — spell correction, query understanding, query rewriting — and each one is a place where the request can die before retrieval even starts. On-site e-commerce search fails this way constantly: the shopper types a word the catalogue has never heard, and the page comes back empty. That is not missing stock, it is the Gremlin at the door, and the fix is synonyms and typo tolerance, not more inventory.

Now the harder half, because the query is at least standing still and relevance is not. Freshness: after an earthquake, an hour-old report beats a beautifully written page from five years ago, and no static notion of quality can express that. Personalization: python means one thing to a programmer and another to a herpetologist; coffee near me is unanswerable without knowing where you stand. Relevance is a function of the document, the query, the user, and the moment — and only the first two are sitting in your index.

Which sets a trap under your training data, so let's name it now: the label rots. A judgment an assessor wrote last year said "this document is relevant to this query" — and it may simply be wrong today, because the world moved underneath it while the label sat still. Your gold set quietly decays into a museum of past correctness. That is not a data-cleaning chore; it is the same creature we will meet under its systems name in the second half of this chapter, when the world under the model moves on and the model stays where it was trained.

The corpus writes back

One line in the domain table looked like a footnote: on the web, the distinctive enemy is spam. It is not a footnote, it is a property no other machine-learning problem has. A digit classifier's inputs do not care what it predicts. A search engine's inputs are written by people who read its ranking, want to be first, and will edit their documents until they are. That is adversarial retrieval, and the keyword-stuffed page from the exact-match beat was never a curiosity — it was a rational reply to a scoring function that published itself the moment it was deployed.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because it decides which features you are allowed to trust. Your ranking function is a specification, and anything in it that is cheap to fake and heavy in the score becomes a target within weeks. So features split by who controls them. A feature the author writes alone — repeat the term, stuff the title, mint a page per query — decays fastest, because faking it costs a keystroke. A feature that costs someone else something — an inbound link, a purchase, a return visit, a verifiable provenance — survives longer, because forging it means paying a third party. This is the Trickster's law with a second player at the table: not only does your team optimise the proxy, an outsider optimises it too, and against you.

Three consequences to carry, and one honest boundary. Your logs are being poisoned deliberately, not merely by position bias, so a click can be a signal, a mistake, or a purchase. Your evaluation must contain the attack — a judged set of ordinary queries will show none of it, because the interesting documents were built to be found. And the pressure is not uniform across the table: an enterprise corpus written by colleagues has almost no adversary, which is exactly why enterprise search can lean on signals the web cannot. The boundary is that adversarial retrieval is a field of its own and this is not its lecture. Carry the reflex instead, and ask it of every feature you add: who benefits from ranking first here, and what would they have to pay to fake this?

Sixty years of learning to match

Here's the part that surprises people: none of what's coming replaces what came before. Search is an archaeology of good ideas stacked on top of each other, and a modern engine quietly runs almost all of them at once. Walk the timeline and you'll recognise every layer of the cascade we're about to build.

It starts with counting. In 1958 Hans Peter Luhn proposed that a word's importance tracks how often it appears — term frequency, the first statistical signal. In 1972 Spärck Jones added the twin idea that rare words carry more information — inverse document frequency — and \(\text{tf}\cdot\text{idf}\) was born. In 1994 Robertson & Walker tuned that into Okapi BM25, the saturating, length-normalised scorer that — astonishingly — is still the first-stage retriever in most production systems thirty years on, and still the baseline every neural model is measured against.

Then meaning arrived. In 2013 Mikolov et al. shipped word2vec and words became vectors — "king − man + woman ≈ queen" — the first real crack in the Lexical Gremlin's wall. In 2019, on the back of BERT (Devlin et al., 2018), dense retrieval grew up: SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych) and DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) learned to embed a query and a document so close in vector space that synonyms finally meet. Around 2020 the field stopped choosing sides and started fusing lexical and dense rankers together, and then RAG (Lewis et al., 2020) bolted a generator onto the retriever to close the loop.

So the punchline is not "neural beat classical." The punchline is that production runs all of them, cascaded: a 1994 BM25 retriever feeding a 2019 neural re-ranker feeding, sometimes, a 2020 generator — each layer doing the job it's cheapest and best at. The Lexical Gremlin was first measured in 1987 and isn't defeated until the dense-embedding layer of 2019; everything in between is the course you're taking. The timeline is the syllabus.

The full pipeline, station by station

Before we start narrowing billions down to a handful, let's walk the whole machine once, left to right, and name every station — because half of production search lives in the stations nobody puts on a slide. Crawl / ingest: get the documents in at all. Analyze: tokenize, lowercase, strip, stem — decide what a "word" even is. Index: write the analyzed terms into the structure we build in a moment. Then the colour breaks, and the second half begins: query processing (parse, correct, expand the thing the user typed), retrieve (BM25 over the index, or approximate nearest neighbours over vectors), rank and re-rank (the expensive models), and finally present — the results page, the snippets, the layout.

Two of those stations are chronically underrated, and both bite. Analysis is not plumbing: it decides the vocabulary the whole system speaks, and — this is the part that catches people — the same analyzer must run on the query. Stem the documents but not the query and the two halves of your engine are speaking different languages; every mismatch is a document you will never retrieve, and nothing in the logs will tell you. Presentation is not decoration either: a perfect result with a terrible snippet still loses the click. The ranker's job is not finished when the order is right; it is finished when the user recognises the answer on the page.

Two clocks, two worlds

That colour break in the middle of the pipeline is the most important line in the whole architecture, so give it its own name: offline builds the index, online walks it. The offline world crawls, analyzes and indexes. It is latency-insensitive and throughput-bound — the clock here is hours, you may spend days sweeping the corpus, and what you optimise is coverage and cost per document. The online world parses, retrieves, ranks and presents. It is latency-critical — the clock here is milliseconds, you get a couple of hundred of them in total, and what you optimise is tail latency and relevance.

That asymmetry is simultaneously a licence and a cage, and almost every design argument in this course descends from it. The licence: you can afford an obscenely expensive analysis pipeline, because it runs once, on a schedule, off the request path — nobody is waiting. The cage: nothing expensive may touch the request path, because there everything is paid per query, by a human who is watching a spinner. Whenever you are unsure where a computation belongs, ask which clock it should be billed to; the answer is almost always "push it offline".

And the seam between the two worlds is exactly where a whole family of silent bugs lives. The same feature has to be computed the same way in both — offline while training, online while serving — and if the two code paths drift by even a hair, the model trains in one world and serves in another. We will give that failure its proper name in the second half of the chapter, along with the piece of architecture that exists solely to kill it.

The structure that makes search possible

Everything above depends on one classical data structure, and it is the one piece of engineering every search person must know cold. The naive layout is a forward index: document → the terms it contains. It is the obvious way to store text and it is useless for querying, because answering a query means opening every document and looking — \(O(N)\) in the size of the corpus, per query, forever. The inverted index simply turns the arrow around: a dictionary of terms, each carrying its document frequency \(\mathrm{df}\), pointing at a postings list — the sorted IDs of the documents that contain that term, optionally with the term frequency \(\mathrm{tf}\) and the positions inside each document. Now the cost of a query depends on how many documents match, not on how many documents exist. That flip is the reason web-scale search is possible at all.

Build one by hand and it stops being abstract. Take a pair of tiny documents — one about a quick brown fox, one about a lazy fox — and run four steps. Tokenize each document into its words. Emit a (term, docID) pair for every token. Sort and group those pairs, and the postings fall out: brown points at the first document, fox at both, lazy at the second, quick at the first. Now answer the query fox AND quick: look up just those two terms and intersect their postings lists. Only the first document survives, and notice what you never touched — the corpus. You walked two short lists, and the answer was in them.

Two details in that walk pay dividends for the rest of the course. The postings are kept sorted precisely so the intersection is a linear merge rather than a lookup storm — and with more terms you intersect the shortest list first, so the work is bounded by the rarest word in the query, not the commonest. And look at what is already sitting in the index: \(\mathrm{tf}\) in the postings, \(\mathrm{df}\) in the dictionary. Those are exactly the two ingredients a classical relevance score needs, which is why BM25 can be built directly on top of this structure with tf and df already in place.

Narrow before you spend

Here's the central trick of every search system, and once you see it you see it everywhere. You don't run your smartest, most expensive model on a billion documents — you'd melt, and you'd miss every deadline. Instead you build a cascade: a funnel of stages, cheap and broad at the top, expensive and precise at the bottom. Retrieval makes one huge first cut — roughly a billion documents down to ~1000 — and then each later stage spends about 10× more compute per document while handing a ~10×-shorter shortlist to the next. Watch the field narrow.

Run the arithmetic and the funnel stops being a metaphor and becomes a budget. The good re-ranking model is a BERT cross-encoder — it reads the query and a document together and is wonderful at judging relevance, but it costs on the order of tens of milliseconds per query-document pair. Multiply that by \(10^{9}\) documents and you're roughly \(10^{8}\times\) over any reasonable latency budget. It's simply not an option to score everything with the good model. So you don't. You let a cheap model — classic BM25 over an inverted index, or an approximate-nearest-neighbour vector lookup — sweep the whole corpus and keep maybe the top ~1000. Those two are fast because they're shallow. Then a mid-cost ranker (a gradient-boosted tree with hundreds of features) trims ~1000 down to ~100. Only then, on a shortlist small enough to afford it, does the heavy cross-encoder do its careful work and pick the final ~10.

The reason the order matters — cheap first, dear last — is a hard, unforgiving rule: the first stage sets a recall ceiling that nothing downstream can lift. If retrieval drops a relevant document, it is gone for the rest of the pipeline; a perfect re-ranker can only reorder what it was handed. Picture the first stage as a leaky bucket: any star that falls through the floor is lost, and the re-ranker robot standing below simply cannot reach it. So if retrieval finds only 80% of the relevant documents, your best possible end-to-end result is 80% — full stop, no matter how brilliant the re-ranker. This is the single most common production mistake: teams pour months into a fancy re-ranker while 20% of the good documents are being silently dropped at the retrieval stage (Stage 0). Recall is bought early and cheaply; precision is polished late and expensively. Invest in first-stage recall before you tune the re-ranker.

Same shape, two readings. As an IR idea it's "narrow billions down to a handful." As a systems idea it's a latency budget: you spend your milliseconds where they buy the most relevance per millisecond, and the heavy model only ever runs on the tiny set that earns it. Let's watch the cascade narrow, stage by stage, with the real numbers.

Start with the whole corpus — ~10⁹ documents. You can't run an expensive model on all of them. So you build a funnel.

First, a cheap high-recall sweep (BM25 / ANN) keeps ~10³ candidates. Never throw away the answer this early — recall lives here.

A mid-cost ranker scores the survivors down to ~10² with many features.

Finally the expensive cross-encoder re-ranks the last ~10 — precision@k where the user looks. Each stage spends ~10× more compute on ~10× fewer candidates.

Recall multiplies

The funnel just narrowed in front of you, stage by stage. Put a symbol on what each narrowing loses and the ceiling stops being a slogan. Let \(R_i\) be the fraction of the relevant documents that survive stage \(i\) — of those it was handed, how many it passed on. Then the recall of the whole cascade is the product, \(R = \prod_i R_i\). Not the minimum, not an average: a product, because every stage is applied to what is left rather than to the corpus.

Read the product and three design rules fall out of it. It can never exceed its smallest factor — that is the ceiling, restated as arithmetic. It falls whenever any factor falls, so a chain of individually respectable stages is not respectable: many small losses compound quietly, and no dashboard shows the compounding, only the end. And the two halves of quality are not symmetric. Precision is repairable downstream: a later stage can push junk down, and the page recovers. Recall is not repairable at all, because a later stage cannot inject a document it never received. That asymmetry, not tradition, is why the funnel is ordered cheap-first — recall is bought once, at the top, where documents are still cheap to keep, and precision is polished repeatedly, at the bottom, where they are few.

It also tells you exactly what to measure, and this is the part teams skip. Recall has to be measured per stage, each against the same judged set, because an end-to-end number tells you only that the product moved and never which factor moved it. Skip that and you get the classic misdiagnosis: retrieval quietly starts dropping a slice of the good documents, the only metric anybody watches is the one at the end of the pipeline, and the team spends a quarter improving the re-ranker that is faithfully reordering a set the answer already left.

Why every stage costs about the same

Look again at the two columns the funnel handed you: how many documents a stage sees, and what it spends on each one. They move in opposite directions by roughly the same factor — every step down the funnel shows a stage about ten times fewer documents and lets it spend about ten times more on each. Multiply the pair and it barely changes: with \(N_i\) candidates at cost \(c_i\) apiece, the bill of a stage is \(N_i c_i\), and the whole request is \(\sum_i N_i c_i\). Holding those products near each other is a deliberate shape — a balanced cascade, in which every stage draws a comparable slice of the latency budget.

Balance is the right target for a blunt reason. If one stage dominates the sum, it is the only thing you can tune; the others are effectively free, and a free stage earns its keep only if it buys recall for the stages below. If instead a stage's slice is tiny, you are wasting the cheapest compute you will ever be offered — its set is small, so quality there is on sale. And notice the tuning move this hands you: when you want to afford a better model, do not buy hardware, shrink the set it sees. Cost is linear in the number of candidates and relevance is not, which is exactly why halving a shortlist roughly halves the bill while barely moving the order.

Two boundaries, because the rule is a habit and not a law. It assumes a stage's quality rises with what you spend on it, and that rise flattens: past some point a heavier model on the same shortlist buys nothing — and if the shortlist already lost the answer, it buys nothing at any price, which is the recall ceiling arriving from the other side. And the factor of ten is not sacred. It comes from the gap between what a cheap scorer and a heavy one cost per document; change that gap — distil the heavy model, or make the cheap one smarter — and the right number of stages changes with it. Count the stages your ratio implies rather than the stages the diagram had.

A demo is not a system

Great — we can find the record once, in a notebook. The cascade works, the numbers look good, the demo lands. But a notebook isn't a product, and this is the moment the ground shifts under the whole lecture: a model in a notebook is not a system.

Be concrete about how big the gap is. You wrote a model.fit() cell, it scored 0.92 on a held-out set, and it feels like you've solved the problem. You've solved maybe 5% of it. The other 95% is everything the notebook quietly assumed away: where does the data come from, and who keeps it flowing? How is every feature computed identically at training time and at serving time? How do predictions come back in under 200 milliseconds, for thousands of users at once, when the cache is cold right after a deploy? Who notices when results slowly get worse — given that nothing ever throws an error? When and how do you retrain, and how do you roll back the night a release goes bad?

The instant real users arrive, all of that surfaces, and it surfaces as the part of the iceberg below the waterline. The demo was the gleaming tip in the sun. The system is the vast, cold mass underneath that actually keeps search alive — and that's where production search lives or dies. So we pivot: from what is search to how do you ship it and keep it breathing. The second mission of the day starts here.

The Iceberg

Meet the Iceberg. The model is the gleaming tip above the water — and it is small. Below the surface sits everything that actually keeps a search system alive: data collection and verification, feature extraction, configuration, the serving infrastructure, resource management, monitoring, and all the glue holding them together. Sculley et al. (2015) drew exactly this picture at NeurIPS and gave it a memorable caption: only a small fraction of a real-world ML system is ML code. Your course so far has been about the tip. This part is about the iceberg — because every box below the waterline is a place a search system fails silently.

Designing it means thinking in two loops at once. The offline loop runs on a schedule, off the request path: collect query and click logs → run a feature-and-index pipeline → train the ranker → register the model. It's latency-insensitive — you can take hours to crawl and index. The online loop runs per request, on a stopwatch: a query arrives → retrieve → rank → re-rank → serve, all inside a couple hundred milliseconds, then log what happened. Offline builds the index; online walks it. The two clocks could not be more different — hours versus milliseconds — and that asymmetry shapes every design choice that follows. The bridge between them is a single piece of plumbing called a feature/index store: offline writes features and the index there in batch; online reads them at low latency.

That store isn't an architecture diagram flourish — it exists to kill the #1 silent killer, training–serving skew. Skew is when the features your model sees at serving time quietly differ from the ones it trained on — usually because two different code paths compute "the same" feature slightly differently, or because the data drifted between training and serving. The model trained on one distribution, serves on another, and degrades with no error message at all. The architectural fix is exactly the feature store: compute each feature once, write it where both training and serving read it, and parity is guaranteed by construction. (Google's Rules of ML say the same thing twice — "train like you serve," "reuse code between training and serving.")

Sculley's paper catalogues the rest of the debt, and each item has a search face. CACE — Changing Anything Changes Everything: ML systems have no clean module boundaries, so swap the tokenizer or nudge the BM25 floor and the input distribution to your re-ranker silently shifts. Config debt: one stale feature-version pin degrades ranking with no exception thrown — it just quietly rots. Glue and pipeline jungles: code testable only end-to-end, so a break anywhere can hide anywhere. The unifying property, the thing to tattoo on the back of your hand: ML systems fail silently. No stack trace — just slowly worse results. Which is why the last step of the design framework isn't "deploy," it's monitor.

So the whole framework is one disciplined loop, and it's worth memorising as six steps, each producing a concrete artifact. (1) Frame the problem: translate a business goal into an ML objective into a proxy metric — "users find what they want" → learn \(P(\text{relevant}\mid q,d)\) → optimise \(\text{nDCG}@10\). (Google's Rule #1: don't be afraid to launch without ML first; a heuristic often beats a premature model and de-risks the plumbing.) (2) Get the data: logs plus graded 0–4 judgments, with splits chosen to avoid temporal leakage and known biases. (3) Model and baselines: simplest first — BM25 before the neural re-ranker (Rule #4: keep the first model simple, get the infrastructure right). (4) Evaluate: offline metrics that predict online, then the online test itself. (5) Serve: under a p99 latency budget, watching for train/serve skew. (6) Monitor and close the loop: track feature and score distributions, watch for drift, and feed the logs back into the next training cycle. Step 6 loops back to step 2 — the lifecycle is a cycle, not a line. Which brings us to the most dangerous part of that loop: the moment the system starts learning from its own behaviour.

Business goal to proxy metric

Step one of the framework said "frame the problem", which sounds like a warm-up and is in fact the whole game. Framing is a translation across three columns, and you have to be able to write the row out loud. Business goal → ML objective → proxy metric. "Users find what they want" becomes "rank relevant documents at the top" becomes \(\text{nDCG}@10\). "Answers feel fast" becomes "low serving latency" becomes \(p_{99}\). "Content stays fresh" becomes "keep the index up to date" becomes indexing lag. Three columns, no wiggle room, and the arrow only points one way.

Every such row is a contract: it asserts that if the metric moves in the right direction, the goal is served. Which means choosing the metric is the design decision — not a reporting detail, not something you pick after the model works. It is also the one place where a technically flawless project can be wrong from its first day, because nothing downstream will ever notice. And notice the shape of the third column: it is always a proxy. Nobody can measure "the user found what they wanted"; you can only measure a shadow of it that happens to be countable. That gap between the goal and its shadow is not a nuisance to be engineered away — it is a permanent resident, and it is where the Trickster of the last act makes his home.

Three families of metrics

The proxy column is not one number, it is three families with three different jobs, and confusing them is how teams argue past each other for a whole quarter. Offline metrics are computed on logged labels: \(\text{nDCG}\), MAP, MRR, plus the classification proxies AUC and log-loss. They are cheap and instant, which is what makes fast iteration possible at all. Online metrics are measured on live users: click-through rate, conversion, dwell time, session success, time to first click. These are the behaviour you actually care about — the offline family only ever approximates them. Guardrail metrics are the must-not-harm family: \(p_{99}\) latency, error rate, revenue, retention.

The discipline that turns this taxonomy into engineering is one rule: every primary metric ships paired with the guardrails that catch its degenerate direction. Push "more clicks" hard enough and the degenerate optimum is clickbait; push "better ranking" hard enough and the degenerate optimum is a page that takes a second to load. So a treatment that lifts click-through but breaks the latency guardrail does not ship — no argument, no exception, because the guardrail exists precisely to be inconvenient. One more subtlety worth knowing early: when your scores feed a downstream stage — a cascade, an auction, a threshold — calibration matters, and log-loss sees it while AUC does not. AUC only reads the order; the stage below reads the value.

Offline win, online loss

Here is the sentence everybody wants to say at the end of a good week: "our new model wins on \(\text{nDCG}@10\) offline, so it will win with real users." It is the most comfortable false statement in the field. The offline–online gap is real and routine, and its cause is structural, not sloppy: offline metrics are computed in isolation, on historical, biased logs, against labels that were written for a world that has since moved. Blind to how fresh the answer is, how varied the page is, whether the source is trusted, how the result is presented, what the session has already shown — none of which lives in the label file, all of which decides whether a human comes back tomorrow. A model can win offline and lose the A/B test, and this happens often enough that it should be your default expectation, not your surprise.

The response is not to throw offline metrics away — you cannot A/B every idea, and iterating on live users is far too slow to be a research loop. The response is to demote them, precisely: treat your offline metric as a hypothesis about online behaviour, not as a verdict. Then do the work the demotion implies — measure, on your own product, which offline metrics actually correlate with online lift, and trust only those. Keep offline for iteration and let online deliver the judgement. The humbling backdrop, reported across the large experimentation platforms, is that most ideas do not move the metric they were built to move, however good they looked beforehand (Kohavi, Tang & Xu, 2020). Your offline number is a reason to run the experiment. It is not the experiment.

The ladder of risk

If online is where the verdict lives, then how you go online is an engineering discipline of its own, and it is a ladder you climb one rung at a time. Shadow: run the new model in parallel with the old one, serve none of its results, just log them. Zero user risk, and it validates the two things that break most often — the infrastructure and the latency. Canary: start serving, but to a sliver of traffic first, then widen, with automatic rollback the moment any guardrail is breached. A/B: the gold standard for causal lift, because random assignment is what licenses the word caused — but for ranking it is noisy and slow, because the variance between users dwarfs the effect you are hunting.

Ranking has its own superpower for exactly that problem: interleaving. Instead of giving user A one ranker and user B the other, merge both rankings into a single list — team-draft style, each slot claimed by one of the two rankers — and attribute every click back to whoever contributed that result. Now every user sees both systems, so the dominant noise source, between-user variance, simply vanishes. The sensitivity gain is one to two orders of magnitude over the best A/B metric, which turns a weeks of waiting into days. The workflow that falls out: filter many candidates with interleaving, then confirm the survivors with A/B — because interleaving gives you a preference, not an absolute lift, and it will never tell you what happened to revenue or to \(p_{99}\).

One gate before all of this, and it is non-negotiable: sample-ratio mismatch (SRM). You assigned traffic evenly between the arms; now count what actually arrived. If a chi-square test says the observed split is not chance, then something upstream is broken — bot filtering that treats the arms differently, a redirect, lost events — and every number in that experiment is untrustworthy, including the flattering one. So the reading order is fixed: SRM gate, then guardrail gate, then the primary metric, and always with segment cuts, because an aggregate can rise while every single segment falls. Treat a failed SRM like a fever: you do not negotiate with it, you diagnose. And never read out on day one — novelty and primacy effects make treatment effects non-stationary, and the first day flatters whatever is new.

Why an ML system has no modules

The catalogue of technical debt under the waterline usually gets read as a list of bad habits — write less glue, pin your configs, tidy the pipelines. It is one property wearing several faces, and the property is this: in an ML system the interface between two parts is not a signature, it is a distribution. A function contract can be honoured; a distribution contract cannot even be written down. Hence the slogan the field settled on — CACE (changing anything changes everything) — which is not cynicism but an accurate description of a system whose components communicate through data.

Watch it fire in search, where every link in the chain behaves exactly as documented. You improve the tokenizer. The analyzed terms change, so the term statistics change, so the first-stage scores change, so the candidate set handed upward changes — and the re-ranker, untouched and unretrained, is now reading inputs from a distribution it never trained on. Nobody wrote a bug. Two more faces of the same thing: undeclared consumers, where somebody downstream started reading your scores without telling you, so your threshold silently became their API and you cannot change it any more; and configuration debt, where one stale feature-version pin degrades the ranking with no exception thrown, because a slightly wrong number is a perfectly valid number.

Since you cannot get modules back, the discipline is to make the distribution part of the contract. Version and ship the analyzer with the model that trained on it, so the pair moves as one artifact rather than as two independently upgradable ones. Monitor the input distribution of each stage, not just its output metric, because the stage that broke is upstream of the stage that shows it. And treat changing anything as a release with its own experiment rather than as an edit — the same ladder as any model change, for a config value. The unifying property is the one already tattooed on the back of your hand: no stack trace, only slowly worse results, which is why step six of the framework is monitor and not deploy.

Five ways the data rots

Step 6 was monitor, and now we have to be honest about what you're monitoring for — because "the system got worse" is not a diagnosis, it's a symptom, and the cures are mutually exclusive. There are five distinct ways a production search system silently rots, and the whole skill is matching the fix to the failure. Apply a covariate-shift fix to a concept-drift problem and you've solved nothing.

Covariate shift is when the inputs move but the rules don't: \(P(x)\) changes while \(P(y \mid x)\) holds. Holiday traffic floods you with queries your model rarely trained on — the mapping from query to relevance is still valid, you're just seeing a different slice of it. You catch this by watching feature distributions, quantified with KL divergence or PSI (population stability index); when today's histogram drifts from training's, the alarm rings. Concept drift is the nastier twin: the rules themselves move, \(P(y \mid x)\) changes. In 2019 the query "corona" meant a beer; in 2020 it meant a pandemic — same word, same features, totally different right answer. Concept drift is invisible in feature distributions — the inputs look fine — so you only catch it as rising error against fresh labels.

Data leakage is the offline-metric inflator: information from the future sneaks into training, your offline number looks spectacular, and production face-plants. The fix is a discipline — split your data by time, not at random — because a random split on temporal data lets tomorrow teach yesterday. Label delay is subtler: conversions and other slow signals arrive late, so "no conversion yet" is not "no conversion" — treat a missing late label as unknown, not negative, or you'll train the model to distrust everything recent. And Simpson's paradox: an aggregate metric can move up while every single segment moves down, purely because the traffic mix shifted underneath you. The rule that saves you is to always cut by segment and never trust the aggregate alone.

One more failure rides alongside these: cold start — a brand-new user, query, or document with no interaction history at all. You can't lean on behavioural features that don't exist yet, so you fall back on content features (what the document actually says), popularity priors (what's broadly good), and a little exploration to gather the first signals. Tattoo the meta-rule on the back of your hand: the diagnosis determines the cure. Naming the five — covariate shift, concept drift, leakage, label delay, Simpson's — is half the battle, because each one has a different tell and a different fix.

Compute once, read twice

The store between the two clocks is usually drawn as a box, which makes it look like storage. Its job is a proof. Skew appears when the same feature is produced by two code paths — a batch job for training, a service call for serving — because two implementations of one definition drift, and nothing tells you when. The architectural answer does not fix the drift, it removes the second path: compute a feature once and let both worlds read the same values. Parity stops being a promise somebody makes in review and becomes a property of the design.

The subtler half is the one that sinks projects, and it has a name: point-in-time correctness. Training rows are historical — this query, this document, this day — and the feature values joined to them must be the values as they stood at that moment, not the values as they stand now. Join today's popularity onto last month's impressions and you have taught the model with information that did not exist when the decision was made; the offline metric will be magnificent and production will faceplant. That is exactly the leakage from the drift beat, arriving through the join rather than through the split. So a feature store is not a cache of current values. It is a time-indexed log with a current-value view on top, and the log is the half that makes training honest.

Three consequences worth carrying. Features acquire definitions and versions rather than just names, because two teams computing «click rate» differently is the skew you were trying to delete. The online face has to be cheap enough for the online clock, so one definition ends up behind two implementations of storage — batch and low-latency — which is fine, as long as it is one definition. And a feature that genuinely only exists at request time is not illegal, but it must be logged as served, not reconstructed later from whatever the world looks like at training time. The rule is short: log what you served. Reconstruction is where skew comes back in through a door you left open.

Spending the millisecond budget

The online loop runs on a stopwatch, so let's actually read the stopwatch. A typical web-search service holds itself to a \(p_{99} \lt 200\) ms service-level agreement — ninety-nine of every hundred requests must answer in under a fifth of a second. Walk one request through and watch the budget drain. The client's GET /search hits the API: +3 ms. The API asks the retriever for ~1000 candidates: +35 ms. It hands ~100 of them to the ranker: +90 ms. It writes the cache and logs asynchronously off the critical path — so that +2 ms never lands on the user-facing total. It serializes the top-\(k\) back to the client: +4 ms. Total on the critical path: about 132 ms — comfortably under the 200 ms ceiling, with headroom for a bad day.

Read that ledger and one line screams: ranking dominates — 90 of the 132 ms. That is not an accident, it's the whole reason the cascade exists. You cannot afford the heavy model on the corpus, so retrieval cheaply narrows a billion documents to ~1000 in 35 ms, and the expensive ranker only ever touches the ~100 survivors. The cascade is a latency budget: spend your milliseconds where they buy the most relevance per millisecond, and never let the heavy model see more candidates than it has to.

Two rules turn this from a diagram into an engineering discipline. First — and tattoo this one too — alert on \(p_{99}\), not \(p_{50}\). The median is a liar; it's the tail that users actually feel, and a system can have a beautiful 40 ms median while one request in fifty times out. A diagnostic tell: if \(p_{99}\) climbs while \(p_{50}\) falls, your request queue is backing up — some requests sail through while others rot in line. (For scale: YouTube's ranker serves in tens of milliseconds; the budget is real and tight everywhere.) Second, the cascade is a tunable knob, not a fixed shape. Adding a light distilled re-ranker (say MiniLM over the top ~100) costs ~60 ms and buys roughly +5–15 \(\text{nDCG}@10\) — 70–90% of a heavy cross-encoder's gain at 30–50% of its cost. A heavy re-ranker on the top-20 adds ~200 ms to finalise the order. The two dials are candidate-set size and per-stage model complexity, and they trade almost linearly: halving the candidate set roughly halves the cost for a tiny relevance loss. That trade — latency spent versus \(\text{nDCG}\) gained — is the knob you actually turn in production.

Why the tail is the product

«Alert on the tail, not the median» reads like a monitoring preference. It is arithmetic about fan-out. A search request is not one call — it is a fan-out: shards of an index queried in parallel, replicas, a ranking service, a feature read, and the request is not finished until the slowest of them is. Suppose a request touches \(k\) parts and each is fast with probability \(q\), independently. Then the whole request is fast with probability \(q^{k}\), and its tail is worse than the tail of any single part it touched. Tail latency amplifies with fan-out — which is why a system assembled from components that are each almost always quick can be routinely slow.

Two readings follow, and both are counter-intuitive enough to be worth saying. A median improvement is perfectly compatible with a tail regression: make the common path faster, add one more parallel hop, and the typical request gets quicker while the unlucky one gets slower. And the diagnostic tell from the ledger — the tail rising while the median falls — is the signature of a queue: a queue serves some requests immediately and makes the rest wait, so it pushes the two ends of the distribution apart. Then remember who is on the receiving end. One request in a hundred being slow is not one user in a hundred being unhappy; a person issues many requests in a session, so a per-request rarity becomes a per-session near-certainty.

What you actually do about it is design, not monitoring. Give every stage a deadline and a degraded answer to return when it expires — the results retrieval already has, rather than nothing — so a slow component costs you relevance instead of the whole request. Send a second copy of a request that has already waited too long, and take whichever answer comes back first. And notice that the cascade is itself a tail-control device: bounding the candidate set bounds the worst case, whereas an unbounded set has an unbounded tail no matter how good the average is. The rule to keep: a stage without a deadline is a stage without a tail you can promise.

The cache is a ranker you forgot you deployed

Caching looks like the least interesting line in a latency budget, and it is the one that most quietly changes what users see. Why it works at all is the beach from the scale beat: query frequency is Zipfian, so a small head of repeated queries soaks up a large share of the volume, and a cache far smaller than the query space still answers a large share of the traffic. Note what that argument depends on — the shape of the distribution, not the size of the corpus. Give a system uniformly spread requests and the same cache buys nothing at all.

But a cache answers users, and anything that answers users is ranking. A cached list is the output of whichever model was live when the entry was written, so after a deploy some fraction of your traffic is still being served by the previous model — and perversely, the hotter the query, the longer the stale answer survives, because it keeps getting hit and never expires from disuse. That collides head-on with experimentation: if the cache key does not include everything that changes the answer — model version, language, filters, and the experiment arm — then two users in different arms can be handed the same cached list, the treatment leaks into the control, and the effect you measure is smaller than the effect you shipped.

Two costs to price honestly. Freshness: a cached answer is the world as of write time, which is exactly the conflict the hard-realities beat set up, so the time to live of an entry should be set by how fast that corpus moves, not by how much memory happens to be free. And the cold cache: right after a release nothing is warm, so the tail you serve in that window is the uncached tail, and capacity has to be planned for it rather than for the steady state. Which gives the measurement rule: benchmark relevance and latency with the cache cold. A number measured warm describes a system that has already been running long enough to have forgotten your change.

Measured pain, engineered fix

Let's ground all of this in one real ledger, because techniques you adopt as defaults are techniques you don't understand. The task: 371 million short multilingual strings, two languages, return the top-100 matches with a score, under a 1-second SLA, and — the catch — no GPU available for inference. Watch the naive design hit the wall, then watch each fix enter as the answer to a measured pain point, never as a hammer looking for a nail.

Before (naive). Embed all 371M documents with a multilingual model at float64, dimension 768. Now stare at the resource ledger: the vector store has shape \((371\text{M}, 768)\) at 8 bytes each — that's on the order of 2.3 terabytes of raw vectors, which won't sit in memory; there's no GPU to accelerate the math; and a brute-force similarity scan over 371M rows per query blows clean through the 1-second SLA. Three red annotations: huge memory, no GPU, too slow. The design is simply unservable.

After (engineered). Three changes, each targeting one red mark. One: run a cascade with an ANN index (the approximate-nearest-neighbour structures of L13) so you never brute-force — the index jumps to the right neighbourhood instead of scanning everyone, which retires too slow. Two: shrink the embedding from dimension 768 to 24 — via PCA or simply a smaller model — and the store becomes \((371\text{M}, 24)\), roughly a 32× memory cut that retires huge memory and quietly removes the need for the GPU you didn't have. Three: cache the hot queries, since query frequency is Zipfian (remember the beach) and a small head of repeated queries soaks up enormous volume — answer those from cache for free. The result: the aggressive 768→24 compression does cost recall, but it's acceptable — Stage-0 only has to hand the re-ranker a good candidate set (the recall-ceiling logic from Part 02), and the ANN index plus the cache recover the latency/SLA — top-100 in well under a second, at a tiny fraction of the resources. Every red annotation flips to "(no problem)."

The pattern here is the one to carry all term, and it's worth stating bluntly: introduce each technique as the fix to a measured bottleneck, never as a default. Nobody reached for ANN, dimensionality reduction, or caching because they're fashionable; each one entered because a specific number — terabytes, no-GPU, seconds — said it had to. That discipline is the difference between engineering a system and cargo-culting one.

Click logs lie

The system now runs, it is measured, and it logs everything — so here comes the most natural thought any team ever has, and it is a trap. "The first result gets the most clicks, so it must be the most relevant. We have millions of clicks. Let's train the ranker on them." Free labels, at web scale, refreshed daily. What could possibly be wrong? Eye-tracking has the first half of the answer (Granka, Joachims &amp; Gay, 2004): attention concentrates in the upper-left corner of the page — the "golden triangle" — and it does so before the reader has judged anything. The top slot earns its clicks in large part because it is the top slot.

Run the thought experiment that settles it. Make every result on the page equally relevant — same quality, same everything — and then count the clicks. The bars still pile up at the top, and they pile up steeply: a click in the first slot carries nearly three times the weight of a click a couple of positions down, even though the documents are, by construction, indistinguishable. Draw the flat line the clicks should follow if they measured relevance, and the gap between that honest line and the tall bars at the left is not noise. It is position bias, and it is the shape of your training data.

So raw clicks are not labels, they are labels plus the ranking that produced them. Train learning-to-rank naively on that and the model dutifully learns the most available pattern — predict what was already on top — which freezes the incumbents in place and calls it accuracy. The standard fix names the bias out loud: inverse-propensity weighting divides each click by the examination probability of the rank it happened at, so a click that had to be earned from deep in the list counts for more than one that fell into the top slot. It is unbiased only if those propensities are right, and the weight explodes at ranks nobody looks at — so real systems clip the propensities, or learn examination and relevance jointly. Hold on to the shape of that curve: we are about to watch it bite.

A thought experiment: make all 10 results EQUALLY relevant, then count clicks. If clicks meant relevance, the bars would be flat.

They're not. Rank 1 alone takes ~32.3% of clicks — purely because it sits on top. That's position bias.

The top 3 soak up ~60.6%. People click what they see first, not what's best.

Overlay the honest signal: if all 10 are equally relevant, clicks SHOULD be flat at 10% each. The gap between this flat truth and the tall bars IS the position bias.

So if you train on raw clicks — “more clicks = better” — you reward position, not relevance. Goodhart's law: the moment CTR becomes the target, it stops being a good measure.

The flywheel and its evil twin

Position bias would be a curiosity if the logs were read once and thrown away. They are not — they are fed straight back into the next training run, and that loop has a name and a reputation. The data flywheel is the virtuous version, and it is the reason large search systems are hard to catch: more users produce more logs and more labels, more labels produce a better model, a better model produces better results, and better results produce more users. Scale compounds quality. Every quarter the wheel turns, and the gap to a smaller competitor widens without anybody doing anything clever.

Now feed the same wheel naive click data and watch it run backwards. Show a popular item; it gets more exposure; more exposure produces more clicks — many of them purely positional, as we just measured; more clicks push it higher in the ranking; higher ranking produces more exposure. Nothing in that loop consulted quality even once. It is the same machine, turning at the same speed, amplifying whatever bias was already in the logs — rich get richer, the long tail is buried alive, and the catalogue slowly freezes into whatever was popular the day you launched. The flywheel and its evil twin are not two systems. They are one system with two possible diets.

Which is why the biases and their fixes should be memorised as pairs. Position bias — clicks reward rank one for being rank one — is answered by inverse-propensity weighting. Exposure bias — items you never showed generate no feedback at all, so your data is missing not at random — is answered by deliberate exploration: an \(\varepsilon\)-greedy slot, a bandit, some budget spent on finding out rather than on cashing in. Degenerate loops — the model only ever sees feedback on what it itself chose to show — are answered by leaning on context features (time, device, situation) instead of pure interaction history. The meta-lesson outlives all three: your system creates the loop it then learns from, so you must counteract it actively. Leave it alone and tomorrow's training set gets written by today's mistakes — with not a word about it on your dashboard.

The most expensive bug

Ask an engineer where the risk in an ML project lives and the answer is almost always the modelling — pick the wrong architecture and everything falls over. It is a comforting belief, because architecture is the part you know how to argue about. The truth is less flattering: the most expensive bug lives in problem framing, one column to the left of anything you can debug. If the objective is misaligned with the goal, a technically perfect model does not merely fail to help — it makes things actively worse, because it is a flawless optimiser pointed slightly off-target. And you will not notice, because the proxy metric looks magnificent the whole time.

The canonical worked case is a ranker you have already met in this chapter. Train it on \(P(\text{click}\mid\text{shown})\) and it learns exactly what you asked: it prefers thumbnail bait and short clickbait, because those maximise the probability of a click. Click-through goes up; satisfaction goes down; nothing in the pipeline objects. Covington, Adams & Sargin (2016) reframed the target instead of the network: they train a weighted logistic, in which positive examples are weighted by the watch time actually observed and negatives by one, so that the learned odds \( e^{Wx+b} \) approximate \( E[\text{watch time}\mid\text{shown}] \) — a genuinely different quantity from the probability of a click. Same architecture, same logged data, one changed definition of "good" — and the ranker starts preferring videos people finish over videos people merely start.

Sit with what that means. The architecture did not change; the framing of good did, and that reframe was worth more than any architecture change that came after it. This is why the framework spends an entire step on framing before anyone touches a model, why every proxy metric ships handcuffed to a guardrail, and why the law of the creature we will name at the end of this chapter is not a joke about metrics but a design constraint. Swapping the model costs a week. Changing the answer to «what are we optimising at all» costs a year, because that year has already been lived.

What the example weights buy

The previous beat asserted that approximation and walked on. Under it there is exactly one line, and it is worth reading. In a weighted logistic regression the odds the model learns are the total weight of the positive rows over the total weight of the negative ones. Give every clicked impression the weight \(T_i\) — the watch time actually observed — and every unclicked one the weight one, and the odds come out as \( e^{Wx+b}=\frac{\sum_i T_i}{N-k} \), where \(N\) impressions were logged and \(k\) of them were clicked. Not one decision about the network appears here. These are example weights — numbers attached to rows of the training table, nothing more.

Now read the right-hand side slowly. Clicks are rare, so \(k \ll N\) and the denominator barely shrinks: the unclicked rows weigh one apiece and they are nearly all of them. What is left is \( \frac{\sum_i T_i}{N-k}\approx\frac{k}{N}\,\mathbb{E}[T\mid c{=}1]=\mathbb{E}[T] \), and the last equality comes for free, because an impression without a click contributes zero watch time. Same sigmoid, same logs, a different quantity estimated: not «will this be clicked» but «how much watching should I expect».

Sit with where that reframe lives. Not in the architecture, not in the features, not in a preamble — inside the loss, as a weight on a row. That is why reading the model code never reveals that the framing is wrong, why the training curves look healthy throughout, and why the bug survives every review that asks about the network. The same line hands you the boundary: the approximation rests on clicks being rare, and if the clicked rows ever became the majority, the denominator would stop being nearly \(N\) and the odds would stop meaning what you assume they mean.

Where the recipe runs out

Everything this chapter has handed you so far arrived in pairs: a failure and its cure. Position bias, and re-weighting. The poisoned flywheel, and exploration. The wrong framing, and a guardrail. Draw the line honestly now, because three of those cures are not settled results — they are working practice that every team re-checks on its own product. That is what a frontier is: not the part you have not read yet, but the part nobody knows.

Direction one: an offline metric you can trust. You already know that an offline win need not survive the A/B test. The open part is sharper — no metric is known to predict online lift in general, so the best the field can do is fit a metric to one product and confirm the correlation by measuring it right there. And having to re-derive that on every product is not your team's overhead; it is the state of the art. Direction two: learning from logs you contaminated yourself. Inverse-propensity weighting is unbiased under two conditions — the propensities are right, and the variance is controlled — and the first cannot be checked against the very logs whose bias produced it. So the edge here is to learn examination and relevance jointly, and to price exploration honestly: at exactly what showing an unproven document costs the product, not at a constant somebody once picked. Direction three: the cascade as one system. You met the recall ceiling while we were taking the funnel apart, and the latency budget while we were counting milliseconds, and the two were never joined: the constraint is shared end to end, yet stages are still tuned apart, each against its own metric. Tuning the whole cascade against one budget, with early recall and late precision traded explicitly, has no standard recipe.

And one honest gap beside those three. Concept drift moves the rule, not the inputs, so it is invisible in feature distributions and surfaces only against fresh labels — which arrive late. You learn that the model has aged from a metric that has already fallen, and there is no general way to catch it earlier. Put all four together and notice what is missing from the list: not one of them is about the model. They all sit under the water — this is the iceberg, not its tip. Past this point the recipe stops, and you measure for yourself.

Where this chapter lands on the map

Step back far enough to see the whole route, because this chapter was one stop on it. The course runs along four: get the data, measure, rank, generate. The Lost Record anchored us at rank — find the one record among billions — while ML system design wrapped the entire loop around that stop, which is why a single chapter had to hold both a cascade and a monitoring plan.

Every week after this one fills a cell in the blueprint you have just drawn, and it helps to know which. Classical IR — the inverted index and the ranking formulas built straight on top of it — is get the candidates. Embeddings — tokenisation and vector representations — is represent. Neural retrieval over dense representations is measure similarity. Approximate nearest neighbours and vector stores are rank fast enough to serve. Retrieval-augmented generation is the payoff, the last stop, where retrieval grounds a generator. No cell brings a new machine; each one swaps a component inside the same pipeline.

So hold two views of one thing. IR tells you what to build and how to measure whether it worked; system design keeps the built thing alive — under a latency budget, against drift, inside a feedback loop that will poison itself if you let it. Every cell on the map carries the same lifecycle you learned here: frame it, get the data, model it, evaluate it, serve it, monitor it — and feed what you learn back into the framing. Learn a technique on its own and you own a demo. Learn where it sits on this map and you own a system.

The machine that keeps finding

You just saw the most dangerous trap in the whole system, so let's name the creature who springs it: Goodhart the Trickster. His law, in the crisp phrasing Strathern (1997) gave Goodhart's (1975) original: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Train your ranker to maximise click-through rate and Goodhart grins and yanks the CTR line up — with clickbait, outrage, ambiguous thumbnails, and accidental taps. The proxy rises while the thing you actually wanted — a satisfied user — quietly sinks.

It bites so hard because the feedback you collect is contaminated by the very ranking that produced it. Position bias means result #1 earns ~32% of clicks largely because it is #1, not because it is best — the peer-reviewed eye-tracking evidence for that rank-1 share is Granka, Joachims & Gay (2004) (the "golden triangle" popularized by the Enquiro 2005 industry report). The position-based (examination) click model from the comparison in Craswell et al. (2008) supplies the form that makes the decay precise (\(p_r \propto 1/r^{\gamma}\)), and Joachims (2002) first warned that raw clickthrough is implicit, biased feedback rather than ground-truth relevance. Train naively on those raw clicks and the model learns "predict what was already on top," freezing the incumbents and burying the long tail — the same data flywheel that should compound quality instead compounds bias, rich-get-richer, every lap. The fixes are real: pair every proxy with a guardrail (dwell time, quick-back rate); reweight clicks by how likely each result was to be examined — inverse-propensity weighting (Joachims et al., 2017); explore a little so unshown items can earn feedback too. The meta-lesson: you must actively fight the loop your own system creates, or it slowly poisons its own training data.

And there's a quieter, equally costly trap right next door — believing your offline number. "Our new model wins on \(\text{nDCG}@10\) offline, so it'll win with real users." It often won't. Offline metrics are computed on historical, biased logs and miss freshness, diversity, trust, presentation, and session dynamics. Per Kohavi, Tang & Xu (2020), at Microsoft's Bing only about one in three ideas that look promising offline actually produce a positive online A/B effect; the other two-thirds are flat or worse, and comparable rates show up at Netflix and Booking. So offline is a hypothesis, not a verdict: use it to iterate fast, then confirm online — and the most expensive bug of all isn't a wrong architecture, it's a wrong frame. A technically perfect CTR-maximiser that quietly tanks retention is worse than no model, because it fails silently while the proxy looks fantastic. Covington et al. (2016) is the canonical fix: YouTube trains its ranker not on \(P(\text{click})\) but on a watch-time-weighted logistic whose odds approximate \(E[\text{watch time}]\) — same architecture, same logs, but now it prefers videos people finish over videos they merely start. You can always swap the model; you rarely notice you optimised the wrong thing until the business metric has already moved against you.

So that's the shape of the whole thing, and it's worth holding as one triad: IR gives you the problem and the metrics; deep learning fills the pipeline; ML system design keeps it alive. A cascade to find the record fast, wrapped in a system that learns from its own behaviour without being fooled by it, watched closely enough to catch the silent failures. We can find the Lost Record — and keep finding it, for every user, every day, after I've gone to sleep. The needle in the cosmos, found; the machine that keeps finding needles, sketched.

But the Gremlin is still out there, smirking. For all our systems machinery, exact match still can't tell "couch" from "sofa" — every box in that beautiful blueprint sits on a retrieval stage that's blind to meaning. To beat him for good we have to stop matching letters and start giving words meaning: coordinates in a space where synonyms sit close together and "couch" and "sofa" are practically neighbours. How do we even represent a record as something a machine can compare? That's where we sail next — and it's where we finally start building the weapon that cages the Lexical Gremlin.
