Judging the Oracle

Last lecture the Oracle learned to speak: retrieve the right passages, stuff them into the prompt, and generate a fluent, sourced answer. The pipeline lit green end to end. But it closed on a question that no amount of green could answer — is it telling the truth? Confabulous proved that a confident lie costs the model nothing, and from the outside a confident-and-wrong answer looks exactly like a confident-and-right one.

So today we put the Oracle on trial. How do you grade an Oracle that sounds confident whether it is right or wrong? The metrics from the search era — precision, recall, MAP, nDCG — all assumed a gold answer to compare against. Generation has no gold key: there are a thousand ways to phrase a correct answer and a thousand more to phrase a fluent wrong one. This lecture builds the courtroom: RAGAS (reference-free RAG metrics), the LLM-as-judge (a model grading a model), and the agentic loop that lets the Ship grade and correct itself. And it is here that Goodhart, the villain who haunts every measure, makes his strongest return.

No gold answer, and fluent is not correct

Two facts break every classical metric at once. First: there is no gold answer. In ranking we held a labelled relevance judgment for each document and scored the system against it. A generated paragraph has no single correct string — paraphrase, ordering, level of detail, and citation style all vary while the answer stays correct. You cannot diff the model's output against a key that does not exist.

Second: fluent is not correct. A language model is, above all, a fluency engine — it will produce smooth, grammatical, confident prose for a true claim and a false one alike. The surface signal you would naively trust (does it read well? does it sound sure?) is exactly the signal the model optimises regardless of truth. So the two things we actually care about — is the answer grounded in real evidence? and does it address the question? — are invisible to any metric that only looks at the words.

The way out is to stop demanding one gold string and instead decompose the answer into checkable pieces and grade each against the evidence we do have: the retrieved context, and the question itself. That is the idea behind RAGAS — and behind every honest courtroom: you do not ask the witness "are you telling the truth?"; you cross-examine the claims one by one.

RAGAS: grade without a gold key

RAGAS (Es, James, Espinosa-Anke & Schockaert, arXiv:2309.15217, 2023) — Retrieval-Augmented Generation Assessment — is the first witness. Its trick is to grade a RAG answer using only three things every RAG system already has on hand: the question, the retrieved context, and the generated answer. No human-written gold answer required. That is what reference-free means, and it is what makes RAGAS cheap enough to run on every answer, not just a hand-labelled test set.

The genius is the decomposition. Instead of one fuzzy score for "is this a good answer?", RAGAS splits the question into four sharp, separately-checkable metrics — two about the answer and two about the context — each of which a small judging model can verify by entailment, not opinion. Break the answer into atomic claims and check each against the evidence; the whole thing reduces to counting supported claims and dividing. Cross-examination, automated.

Grading with no key

Before the four metrics arrive one by one, look at the shape they all share, because reference-free grading needs an argument before it needs a formula. A classic metric takes two things — the answer and a key — and the key is exactly what a live RAG system does not have. Two moves rescue it, and the first is decomposition: send the answer through \( a\mapsto\{c_1,\dots,c_n\} \), splitting it into atomic claims. A paragraph has no truth value you can count; a single claim does. And phrasing varies wildly while the claim underneath does not, which is the «there are a hundred right answers» objection, answered on the spot.

The second move replaces comparison with a relation. Instead of asking whether the answer matches a string, ask whether the retrieved context supports each claim, and average the verdicts: \( m(a\mid q,C)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}\big[\,C\models c_i\,\big] \), where \(\models\) is entailment and no gold reference appears anywhere in the line. Nothing external is needed because the question and the context are themselves a partial key: they never say what the answer is, but they do say what it may assert and what it must address. What comes out is a counting fraction — checkable by hand, and diagnostic in a way a distance never is, because it names the claim that failed instead of returning a number about the whole.

Hold on to the shape rather than the instance. Swap what stands on the two sides of \(\models\) — answer against context, context against question, context against known facts — and the same fraction gives you a different axis of quality each time; that is why the next beat can define four metrics and still feel like one idea. And say the caveat now rather than later: \(\models\) is decided by a model, not by a proof, so this score is a proxy from its very first line. That is precisely the seam Goodhart will pry open.

The four metrics, formally

Write the four metrics down once, precisely, before we hand-compute them. Two grade the answer against the context; two grade the context against the question. Faithfulness, context precision and context recall lie in \([0,1]\); answer relevance averages raw cosine similarities and can be negative. Together they compose into a single profile rather than a single number — and the profile is the point: a RAG system can score high on one and low on another, and which one fails tells you which part of the pipeline to fix.

Faithfulness \(= \dfrac{\#\text{claims supported by context}}{\#\text{claims in the answer}}\) — does the answer invent anything the context never said? This is the direct hallucination detector. Answer relevance \(= \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \cos(q, \tilde q_i)\), where each \(\tilde q_i\) is a question reverse-engineered from the answer — does the answer actually address the question, or wander off-topic?

Context precision \(= \dfrac{1}{\#\text{rel}}\sum_{k:\,\text{rel}_k}\text{precision@}k\) — are the relevant retrieved chunks ranked high, or buried under noise? (This is MAP from Lecture 1, now applied to the retrieved context.) Context recall \(= \dfrac{\#\text{ground-truth claims present in context}}{\#\text{ground-truth claims}}\) — did retrieval bring back all the facts the answer needed? The first two audit the generator; the last two audit the retriever. Together they localise blame.

No gold answer here — only the question, the retrieved contexts, and a generated answer broken into atomic claims. Three are supported by the contexts; one (“William Harvey, 1628”) is fluent but unsupported — a hallucination. RAGAS scores generation without a reference answer.

Faithfulness = supported claims / total claims = 3/4 = 0.75. It catches the hallucinated claim directly: one unsupported sentence drops the score a full quarter. This is the metric that grades “did the answer stay grounded in what we retrieved?”

Answer relevance reverse-engineers questions from the answer and measures their mean cosine to the original question (mean[0.92, 0.88, 0.31] = 0.7033). The drifting third question — born of the hallucinated claim — drags the mean down. It grades “does the answer actually address the question?”

Context precision asks whether the relevant chunks sit near the top of what was retrieved: Σ precision@k over the relevant ranks, divided by the relevant count = (1 + 2/3)/2 = 0.8333. Relevant items at ranks 1 and 3, with noise between, cost some precision. It grades the retriever's ranking.

Context recall asks the opposite: of the ground-truth claims, how many are actually present in the retrieved context? 2 of 3 = 0.6667 — the third truth (“diastole refills the heart”) was never retrieved. Together the four metrics decompose RAG quality into generation faithfulness, relevance, and both sides of retrieval.

Faithfulness, by hand

Take a real answer to "How does the heart pump blood?" and break it into atomic claims. Faithfulness asks, of each claim: is it entailed by a retrieved passage? Count the supported claims, divide by the total. One hallucinated sentence — however fluent — drops the score by a full quarter.

The answer makes \(4\) claims. Three are supported by the context: the heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood \(\checkmark\), it contracts rhythmically \(\checkmark\), ventricular systole ejects blood into the arteries \(\checkmark\). The fourth — "William Harvey discovered this circulation in 1628" — is fluent, plausible, and nowhere in the retrieved context \(\times\). So faithfulness \(= \dfrac{3}{4} = \mathbf{0.75}\).

The Harvey sentence is the planted hallucination: a true-sounding fact the model added from its own parameters, ungrounded in anything we retrieved. Faithfulness catches it directly — it is the metric that puts a number on grounding. Notice it says nothing about whether the answer is on-topic or complete; it only asks whether every claim has a source. That is its whole job, and it does it with arithmetic a student can check.

Answer relevance, by hand

Faithfulness asks is it grounded? — but a grounded answer can still wander off-topic. Answer relevance asks the complementary question: does the answer address what was asked? The clever trick: from the generated answer, reverse-engineer the questions it seems to be answering, embed them, and measure their cosine similarity to the original question. An on-topic answer yields reverse-questions that hug the original; a rambling one yields reverse-questions that drift. Note what this never reads: the retrieved context. Answer relevance sees only the question and the answer, so it is blind to grounding — the exact mirror of faithfulness, which sees only answer and context. (RAGAS also penalises noncommittal answers: an evasive "I don't know" yields off-topic reverse-questions and scores low.)

Generate \(3\) reverse-questions from the answer and measure each one's cosine to the original question \(q\): \([\,0.92,\ 0.88,\ 0.31\,]\). The first two stay on-topic; the third — born of the hallucinated Harvey claim — drifts. Answer relevance \(= \operatorname{mean}[0.92, 0.88, 0.31] = \dfrac{2.11}{3} = \mathbf{0.7033}\).

The third reverse-question, the one at \(0.31\), is the answer's tangent: because the model invented a historical claim about William Harvey, one of the questions the answer seems to answer is "who discovered circulation?" — which has little to do with how the heart pumps. So in this example the hallucination happens to show up twice: once in faithfulness (an unsupported claim) and again here in relevance (a drifting reverse-question). But do not over-read that coincidence — it is one defect that happened to dent both gauges, not evidence that the gauges move together. Relevance and faithfulness are in fact orthogonal: an answer can be perfectly on-topic and entirely fabricated (high relevance, low faithfulness — Confabulous's fluent lie), or perfectly grounded and useless (high faithfulness, low relevance). The full cross-product of these off-diagonal cases is exactly what we lay out a few beats from now; for here, just hold that the two numbers answer different questions, and you need both.

Context precision, by hand

Now turn from the answer to the context the retriever brought back. Context precision asks: are the relevant chunks ranked high, or are they buried under noise? It is precision@k averaged over the relevant ranks — the same machinery as MAP from Lecture 1, now grading the retrieved context instead of a search results page. Relevant-but-low-ranked passages cost precision, because the generator reads the top of the list first.

Four retrieved chunks, ranked: \(c_1\) relevant, \(c_2\) noise, \(c_3\) relevant, \(c_4\) noise — \(2\) relevant in total. Walk the list and at each relevant rank take precision@k: at rank \(1\), \(\text{p@1} = \tfrac{1}{1} = 1\); at rank \(3\), \(\text{p@3} = \tfrac{2}{3}\). Average over the relevant items: context precision \(= \dfrac{1 + \tfrac{2}{3}}{2} = \mathbf{0.8333}\).

The first relevant chunk sits at rank \(1\) — perfect. But the second relevant chunk is stranded at rank \(3\), with a noise chunk \((c_2)\) wedged above it, so its precision@k is only \(2/3\). That single misplacement is what drags the score from a perfect \(1.0\) down to \(0.8333\). Context precision grades the retriever's ordering: it rewards putting the good passages first, exactly because a tight context window (Lecture 15's budget) means the generator may never read the chunks at the bottom.

Context recall, by hand

Context precision asked whether the relevant chunks are ranked high; context recall asks the mirror question — did retrieval bring back all the facts the answer needed at all? Take each ground-truth claim the ideal answer requires and check whether any retrieved chunk supports it. A truth never retrieved is a truth the generator could not possibly have used.

The ideal answer rests on \(3\) ground-truth claims. Two are present in the retrieved context: the heart pumps blood through the body \(\checkmark\) and ventricular systole ejects blood into the arteries \(\checkmark\). The third — "diastole then refills the heart chambers" — was never retrieved \(\times\). So context recall \(= \dfrac{2}{3} = \mathbf{0.6667}\).

The missing diastole fact is the recall floor from Lectures 1 and 9, now at the context level: if retrieval never surfaces a fact, no amount of generation skill can recover it. Faithfulness and answer relevance can both be high while context recall quietly caps the best possible answer — the Oracle will never mention diastole because nothing it read said so. This is why context recall is the metric that points the finger upstream: a low score here means the fix is in retrieval (better chunking, query rewriting, a wider net), not in the generator.

The four-metric quad

Lay the four side by side and the design clicks into place. They form a two-by-two: answer vs. context crossed with what we have vs. what we needed. Faithfulness and answer relevance grade the generator (given this context, did it write a grounded, on-topic answer?); context precision and context recall grade the retriever (did it surface the right passages, ranked well, with nothing missing?).

The diagnostic power is in the combinations. Low faithfulness, high everything else → the retriever did its job but the generator hallucinated; fix the prompt or the model. High faithfulness, low context recall → the generator was honest about thin evidence, but retrieval missed facts; fix chunking or query rewriting. High answer relevance, low faithfulness → a confident, on-topic, fabricated answer — the most dangerous failure, and exactly Confabulous's signature. A single scalar would blur all of these into one mushy "6.5/10"; the quad keeps them apart so you know where to operate.

This is why reference-free evaluation is not a compromise but an upgrade: it does not just tell you whether the system is good, it tells you which component to blame. That decomposition is the whole reason RAGAS earned its place as the first witness — but, as the next beat warns, the witness has limits of its own.

Where RAGAS stops

RAGAS is automated, reference-free, and decomposed — but it is not magic, and its limits matter before we lean on it. It runs on a judging model. Faithfulness and answer relevance are computed by prompting an LLM to break claims into pieces and check entailment. That means RAGAS inherits the judge's own blind spots — a weak judge mis-counts supported claims, and the score is only as trustworthy as the model computing it. We will measure exactly this in the LLM-judge section.

It assumes the question is answerable from the context. Context recall needs ground-truth claims to check against; for open-ended or subjective questions ("write a moving poem about the sea") the whole framing collapses — there is no factual claim to ground. RAGAS is built for factual, retrieval-grounded question answering, which is most of search-adjacent RAG but not all of generation.

And it grades pieces, not the whole. Decomposition is RAGAS's strength, but a stack of high per-claim scores does not guarantee a coherent answer — the metrics say nothing about flow, tone, or whether the pieces fit together into something a human would call good writing. For that holistic judgment we need a different witness, one that reads the whole answer and renders an opinion. Enter the LLM-as-judge — and with it, the trickster who has been waiting since Lecture 1.

The LLM-as-judge

If you cannot diff against a gold answer and you want a holistic verdict, the modern move is disarmingly direct: ask another language model to grade it. The LLM-as-judge (Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2306.05685) hands the answer — and a written rubric — to a capable model and asks it to score, or to pick a winner between two candidates. It is fast, cheap, and correlates surprisingly well with human preference on clear-cut cases.

The appeal is obvious: a strong judge model reads the whole answer the way a human grader would, weighing coherence and tone alongside correctness — exactly the holistic judgment RAGAS cannot give. The setup is just a prompt: here is the question, here is the answer, here is the rubric; return a score per criterion. No labels, no training, runs at inference time on any answer you can produce.

But the moment you make a model the judge, you have created a metric to optimise — and the instant a metric becomes a target, the trickster who has shadowed every measure since Lecture 1 sits up. The LLM-judge is where Goodhart finally gets his courtroom. First, though, let us see the judge working honestly.

The rubric, formally

A judge with no rubric is just vibes. To make the verdict reproducible, pin down a rubric: a fixed set of named criteria, each scored on a fixed integer scale, and a single aggregation rule. Here the rubric has three criteria — relevance, grounding, completeness — each scored \(1\) to \(5\), and the answer's score is the mean across criteria.

Score two candidate answers. Answer A earns \([5, 4, 3]\) → mean \(= \dfrac{5+4+3}{3} = \mathbf{4.0}\). Answer B earns \([4, 2, 2]\) → mean \(= \dfrac{4+2+2}{3} = \mathbf{2.6667}\). So \(A \gt B\): the pairwise winner is A.

Three things make this rubric trustworthy as far as it goes. The criteria are named, so the judge cannot hide a vague preference behind a single number — it must defend relevance, grounding, and completeness separately. The scale is fixed, so scores are comparable across answers and runs. And the aggregation is explicit — a mean here, but it could be a weighted sum, a minimum, or a veto on any criterion below threshold. Hold that last clause in mind: the aggregation is a knob. Goodhart is about to grab it.

Instead of a single label, we hand the judge a rubric: relevance, grounding, completeness. It scores each answer on every criterion. Answer A reads strong across the board; answer B is weaker on grounding and completeness. This is pointwise scoring — each answer judged alone, criterion by criterion.

Average the criteria and each answer gets one pointwise number. A clears the rubric comfortably; B lags well behind. The means look clean and decisive — which is exactly why a single rubric score feels trustworthy. Hold that thought: the same judge can be steered.

Pairwise comparison asks a cleaner question: not “what score,” but “which is better, A or B?” The judge picks A. Comparing two answers side by side is usually more reliable than asking for an absolute number — there is no shared scale to drift. So far the judge agrees with us.

Now Goodhart strikes. An honest judge prefers the truly better answer over a longer, padded one. But tweak the judge to over-weight length, and the verdict flips: the worse, verbose answer now wins. The moment a score becomes a target — “write longer to score higher” — it stops measuring quality and starts measuring length.

These biases are not hypothetical — they are measured on a real, frozen judge model. When quality is clearly different, it judges perfectly. But on a genuine tie it anchors to whichever answer sits in the first slot, and across the verbosity tests it picks the longer answer every single time. Use a judge — but audit it for exactly these biases.

Pointwise vs. pairwise

There are two ways to put the judge to work, and the choice shapes what you can trust. Pointwise: hand the judge one answer and ask for an absolute score on the rubric — "rate this answer 1–5 on grounding." It gives you a number you can track over time and average across a test set. But absolute scores from an LLM drift: the same answer can earn \(4\) today and \(3\) next week, because the model has no fixed internal yardstick for "what a 4 means."

Pairwise: hand the judge two answers and ask only "which is better?" This sidesteps the calibration problem — relative judgments are far more stable than absolute ones, the same reason human raters find ranking easier than scoring. It is how Chatbot Arena builds its leaderboards: thousands of pairwise battles aggregated into an Elo rating. The cost is that you get an ordering, not a level — pairwise tells you A beats B, never that both are terrible.

Most serious eval pipelines use both: pointwise scores for tracking absolute quality and catching regressions, pairwise battles for ranking candidate systems against each other. But pairwise has a hidden vulnerability that pointwise mostly avoids — position. When you show the judge two answers, you have to put one first, and as the next beats measure, that ordering alone can swing the verdict.

Reference-free vs. reference-based

Both RAGAS and the LLM-judge can run in two modes, and the distinction is worth a beat. Reference-based judging gives the judge a human-written gold answer to compare against — "here is the answer; here is the ideal answer; how close is it?" When you have a gold answer this is the most reliable mode, because the judge is anchored to a fixed target instead of its own shifting taste. The catch is the one this whole lecture opened on: gold answers are expensive, and for open generation they often do not exist.

Reference-free judging gives the judge only the question, the context, and the answer — and asks it to reason about quality directly. This is the default for RAG at scale, precisely because it needs no labels and runs on every answer. It is what makes RAGAS's four metrics and the bare LLM-judge usable in production. The price is that the judge has no external anchor; it is grading on its own internalised sense of "good," which is exactly the surface a bias can exploit.

The practical rule: use reference-based wherever you can afford gold answers — on a small, curated regression set you check before every release — and reference-free everywhere else, for the long tail of live traffic. And whenever you go reference-free, remember the anchor is gone, so the judge's own biases are now load-bearing. Which brings us, at last, to the trickster.

Goodhart returns for the judge

Here he is, in his strongest scene: Goodhart, the trickster who first appeared in Lecture 1 with the line "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," and returned in Lecture 5 to game ranking metrics. He has been waiting for this moment, because the LLM-judge is the most gameable target we have ever built. The instant you optimise your generator against a judge — say, by reinforcement learning on the judge's score — the generator stops learning to be better and starts learning to score higher. Those are not the same thing, and the gap between them is Goodhart's whole domain.

The mechanism is brutally simple. An LLM-judge is itself a language model, with all of a language model's superficial preferences — and a generator optimised against it will discover those preferences and exploit them, regardless of whether they track real quality. The judge likes longer answers? The generator pads. The judge anchors to the first answer it sees? The generator games position. The judge over-weights one rubric criterion? The generator inflates exactly that criterion. None of this makes the answer truer; all of it makes the score go up.

This is not a hypothetical. In the next two beats we freeze a real judge — llama3.1:8b at temperature 0, seed 42 — and measure two of its biases directly, and the numbers are damning. But first, the arithmetic flip: how re-weighting a single rubric criterion can invert the winner without changing a word of either answer.

The Goodhart flip, by hand

Goodhart in three lines of arithmetic. We bring in a fresh pair of candidates here — not the A/B from the rubric example, but two new answers — A, the honest one (scoring \([5, 5, 3]\)), and C, the padded one — scored on the same three-criterion rubric. Under honest judging, the better answer wins. Now over-weight a single criterion as a stand-in for rewarding length, and watch the winner invert without a single word of either answer changing.

Honest mean: \(A = \operatorname{mean}[5, 5, 3] = \mathbf{4.3333}\) and \(C = \operatorname{mean}[4, 3, 5] = \mathbf{4.0}\), so \(A \gt C\) — winner A. Now over-weight completeness \(\times 2\) (a proxy for length): \(A = \dfrac{5 + 5 + 2\cdot 3}{4} = \mathbf{4.0}\) and \(C = \dfrac{4 + 3 + 2\cdot 5}{4} = \mathbf{4.25}\), so now \(C \gt A\) — winner C.

Read what just happened. Honest judging picks A (\(4.3333 \gt 4.0\)). Then we changed nothing about either answer — we only changed what the score rewards, doubling the weight on "completeness," the criterion a longer answer trivially wins. And the padded answer C takes the crown (\(4.25 \gt 4.0\)). Nothing about C got truer. The metric became a target, and the target moved the winner. This is Goodhart's law in three lines: optimise the proxy, and the proxy detaches from the thing it was supposed to proxy.

And the most chilling part: "completeness ×2" was not even a malicious choice. A well-meaning engineer who simply believes thorough answers are better would set exactly this weight — and unknowingly train their generator to pad. Goodhart does not require a villain; he only requires a target.

Position bias, measured

Now stop theorising and measure. (Position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias were named and measured by Zheng et al. (MT-Bench, 2023); what follows re-measures them on a small open judge.) We froze a real judge — llama3.1:8b, temperature \(0\), seed \(42\) so the run is reproducible — and fed it pairwise battles. First, the good news. When one answer is clearly better, the judge is right every time.

On the \(6\) cases where quality is clear, accuracy \(= \dfrac{6}{6} = \mathbf{1.0}\). But on the \(6\) genuine tie cases — two answers of equal quality — the judge anchored to whichever answer sat in slot \(1\): position-follow rate on ties \(= \dfrac{4}{6} = \mathbf{0.6667}\).

Read the two numbers together and the danger sharpens. The judge is perfect \((1.0)\) when the gap is wide — so a casual benchmark on easy cases would declare it reliable and move on. But on the close calls, where you most need a judge, it becomes a coin that lands heads because of where the answer was printed, not what it said: two times out of three it just picks slot \(1\). The bias hides exactly where it does the most damage — the ties, the A/B tests that are nearly even, the leaderboard battles that decide a release.

This is why pairwise's hidden vulnerability is real, not theoretical. If your A/B comparison happens to print the new system first, it inherits a \(0.6667\) tailwind that has nothing to do with quality. The mitigation — swap the order and average — comes a few beats from now; for the moment, sit with how cheaply a "rigorous" automated eval can be fooled by something as trivial as print order.

Verbosity bias — the longer answer wins, every time

Position bias was bad. Verbosity bias is worse — it is the killer finding of this whole lecture. Same frozen judge (llama3.1:8b, temp \(0\), seed \(42\)). We held content quality fixed and varied only length: pair a concise correct answer against a padded version of the same correct answer, and ask the judge to pick.

Across \(5\) paired prompts \(\times\) \(2\) orderings \(= 10\) length-matched trials, the verbosity-preference rate \(= \dfrac{\#\{\text{picks the longer answer}\}}{\#\{\text{trials}\}} = \dfrac{10}{10} = \mathbf{1.0}\). The longer answer won every single time.

Read that number again: a perfect \(1.0\). Not a tendency, not a lean — the longer answer won in \(10\) of \(10\) trials, with content quality held constant. This is Goodhart made measurable. Optimise your generator against this judge and it learns exactly one lesson: write more. Padding is a free win. And it is the single most dangerous bias for search, because a good search answer is the opposite of verbose — it is the shortest grounded answer that resolves the query. A judge that rewards length is a judge that actively trains your system away from the thing that makes search useful.

Note the literature already named this: Saito, Wachi, Wataoka & Akimoto (Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models, arXiv:2310.10076, 2023). We did not discover verbosity bias; we re-measured it on a small open model and watched it hit \(1.0\). Which is the whole lesson — these biases are not exotic edge cases; they are the default behaviour of the cheap judges most teams actually deploy.

Fighting the biases

The biases are real and measured — but they are not unbeatable, and a serious eval pipeline layers several defences. Swap and average kills position bias directly: run every pairwise battle twice, once with each ordering, and only count a win if the same answer wins both ways. An answer that only wins because it sat in slot \(1\) loses when the slots flip, so the swap-averaged verdict discards the position component the \(0.6667\) tie-anchor measured.

Control for length blunts verbosity bias: normalise or cap answer length before judging, or add an explicit rubric instruction — "do not reward length; a concise correct answer is preferred over a verbose one" — and, better, measure the length-preference rate (we got \(1.0\)) as a standing diagnostic so you know when your judge is gaming you. Stronger judges help too: a larger, better-aligned judge model exhibits these biases less, though never zero, so the cheapest judge is rarely the right one for high-stakes decisions.

Calibration and ensembles finish the defence. Anchor the judge with a few reference-based examples so its scale stops drifting; use multiple judges and require agreement; and — the oldest defence of all — keep a small human-graded set as ground truth to validate the automated judge against. The deeper point is structural: you cannot fully eliminate the biases of an LLM-judge, because the judge is an LLM. You can only measure them, correct for the ones you have measured, and never optimise hard against an unaudited judge. Which is exactly Goodhart's law, restated as engineering practice.

Goodhart's law, resolved

Step back and watch the arc close. Goodhart arrived in Lecture 1 as an aphorism — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — a warning attached to precision and recall. He returned in Lecture 5 to game ranking metrics: optimise nDCG directly and the ranker learns to satisfy the metric rather than the user. And here, in Lecture 16, he gets his definitive scene, because the LLM-judge is the purest possible instance of the law: a measure (the judge's score) that we are explicitly tempted to make a target (train the generator to maximise it).

What makes this the strongest return is that the gap is now measurable, not merely cautionary. The verbosity rate of \(1.0\) is Goodhart's law with a number on it: the judge's score and true answer quality have visibly come apart, and we can watch a generator that optimises the score drift away from the goal. The villain is no longer a parable — he is a reproducible experiment.

And the resolution is not "stop measuring." You cannot run a search system without metrics, and you cannot evaluate generation without a judge. The resolution is the discipline this lecture has been building toward: measure the metric's own failure modes, keep a human-anchored ground truth, and never optimise blindly against a proxy you have not audited. A good engineer treats every metric as a target in waiting and stays one step ahead of the gaming. That posture — vigilance toward your own measures — is the lasting lesson Goodhart leaves the spine. Now we turn it on the Ship itself: can the Oracle audit its own answers?

The Ship critiques itself

So far the judge has been external — a separate model grading the Oracle from outside. The agentic turn folds that judgement back inside the loop: instead of generating once and being scored afterward, the Ship retrieves, grades its own retrieval, decides whether it has enough, and — if not — retrieves again, rewrites, or reaches outside the corpus. Evaluation stops being a verdict delivered at the end and becomes a control signal that steers generation as it happens.

This is the same self-correcting family Lecture 15 previewed with CRAG and Self-RAG, now seen through the lens of evaluation: every one of these methods is, at heart, a generator that judges itself mid-stream. The four-metric quad and the LLM-judge gave us the vocabulary — relevance, grounding, is-this-retrieval-good — and the agentic loop wires that vocabulary into the model's own decision-making, turn by turn.

Three methods build the loop, in increasing depth. ReAct interleaves reasoning and acting, retrieving on demand across multiple hops. Self-RAG trains the model to critique its own retrieval and output with reflection tokens. CRAG adds an explicit retrieval-grader that branches on confidence. And Adaptive-RAG asks the prior question — whether to retrieve at all. Start with ReAct, because it is the one that turns a passive answerer into an active agent.

The self-correcting loop, formally

Write the loop down once. A plain RAG pipeline is a straight line: \(q \to \text{retrieve} \to \text{augment} \to \text{generate} \to a\), no edge ever pointing backward. The agentic loop adds the missing feedback edge — a grade-and-branch step between generation and the final answer:

At each turn \(t\): retrieve context \(R_t\) for the current query \(q_t\); compute a grade \(g_t = \text{judge}(q_t, R_t, a_t) \in [0,1]\). If \(g_t \ge \tau\) (good enough), finish and emit \(a_t\). Otherwise act: rewrite the query \(q_{t+1} = \text{rewrite}(q_t, R_t)\), or reach outside (web search), and loop — up to a step budget \(t \lt T_{\max}\) so the loop always halts.

Three knobs define a member of the family. What gets graded — the retrieval (CRAG), each generated segment (Self-RAG), or the whole trajectory (ReAct's implicit success check). Who grades — an external evaluator (CRAG), the model itself via learned reflection tokens (Self-RAG), or the reasoning trace deciding it has enough (ReAct). And what the branch does — finish, retrieve again, rewrite, or escalate to web search. Every method in this lecture is a particular setting of these three knobs; the shared skeleton is the feedback edge, the thing a straight-line pipeline structurally cannot have.

Notice this is the LLM-judge from the middle act, but inlined: the grade \(g_t\) is exactly a reference-free judgment, and everything we learned about its biases applies — a self-grading loop can learn to game its own grade, which is why the step budget \(T_{\max}\) and an occasional external check are not optional. The Ship judging itself inherits all of Goodhart's warnings; the loop is powerful precisely because, and only as long as, its self-grade stays honest.

A two-hop question can't be answered from one retrieval — the answer lives behind a fact we don't have yet. So instead of retrieving once, the model runs a loop: Thought, then Action, then Observation, repeated. recall@1 starts at zero — nothing relevant has been found.

First iteration. The Thought names what's missing; the Action is a lookup that searches for the first entity; the Observation returns the founder's name. We're one hop in, but we still don't have what was actually asked — so recall@1 is still zero.

Second iteration — this is the hop that matters. The new Thought feeds the founder's name back into a second lookup, and this Observation finally returns the missing fact. The chain closed: recall@1 jumps to one and the Observation lane turns green.

With both hops landed, the model stops looping and emits a finish action with the answer — the loop is solved. And this isn't only a toy: a real, small open model ran exactly this ReAct loop end to end on a different two-hop question and got it right, shown in the badge below.

Free-form ReAct improvises its loop in natural language. Its structured cousins make the same control flow explicit: Self-RAG emits reflection tokens to decide when to retrieve and to critique its own output, while CRAG grades each retrieved doc and branches on the grade. Both are the L10 self-correcting loop, now tokenized.

ReAct, by hand

ReAct (Yao, Zhao, Yu, Du, Shafran, Narasimhan & Cao, ICLR 2023, arXiv:2210.03629) interleaves Reasoning and Acting: the model emits a thought, takes an action (a retrieval), reads the observation, and loops — reasoning over what it just learned to decide the next action. This is what makes multi-hop questions answerable: questions whose answer requires chaining facts no single retrieval holds.

Take a two-hop question — "What did the founder of Acme Corp study?" — and track recall@1, whether the top-1 retrieval at each step already contains the final answer.

Step 0: thought "I need the founder of Acme Corp", action lookup[Acme Corp], observation "founded by Dana Reyes" — useful, but not the answer, so recall@1 \(= 0\). Step 1: thought "now find what Dana Reyes studied", action lookup[Dana Reyes], observation "studied computer science at MIT" — the bridging fact arrives, recall@1 \(= 1\). Step 2: finish[computer science at MIT], recall@1 \(= 1\). The recall-by-step sequence is \([\,0,\ 1,\ 1\,]\).

The sequence \([0, 1, 1]\) is the whole argument for the loop. A single-shot retriever fires once at step 0, gets the founder's name, and stops at recall \(= 0\) — it can never reach "computer science at MIT" because that fact is two hops away, reachable only after feeding the first observation back in. The loop closes the chain: the output of hop one becomes the query of hop two. Reasoning is not decoration here; it is the mechanism that turns one observation into the next query, and a passive retriever into an agent that can follow a trail.

And this is not a toy claim. The next beat shows a real small model running exactly this loop and solving a genuine two-hop question — structure, not scale, is what made it an agent.

A real model solves a 2-hop question

The toy trace argued the loop should work; here is a real one that did. We froze llama3.1:8b (temperature \(0\), seed \(42\)) and gave it a ReAct loop over a small entity list, then asked a genuine two-hop question: "What publishing company did an author of the book 'The Pragmatic Programmer' co-found?" — book \(\to\) author \(\to\) publisher, a chain no single lookup can resolve.

Step 0 lookup[the pragmatic programmer] → observation: "written by Andrew Hunt and David Thomas." Step 1 lookup[andrew hunt] → observation: "Andrew Hunt… co-founded the publisher Pragmatic Bookshelf." Step 2 finish → answer: Pragmatic Bookshelf. Solved in \(3\) steps.

The model never saw "Pragmatic Bookshelf" and "The Pragmatic Programmer" linked in a single passage; it had to follow the chain itself: look up the book to get the authors, pick one author, look that author up to get the publisher, then finish. Each hop's observation became the next hop's query — the \([0,1,1]\) recall pattern from the toy, playing out on a real model.

And here is the lesson that matters for small models everywhere: structure, not scale, made it an agent. An 8-billion-parameter model is not large, and asked the question cold it would likely confabulate. But given a tool (a lookup over a clean entity list) and a loop (ReAct's reason-act-observe cadence), the same small model became a capable multi-hop agent. You do not always need a frontier model; you often need to give a modest model the right scaffolding. That is the agentic payoff — and it sets up the deeper self-correcting methods next.

Self-RAG: the model critiques itself

ReAct made the model an agent; Self-RAG (Asai, Wu, Wang, Sil & Hajishirzi, ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.11511) makes the agent its own judge — moving the evaluation inside the model. It trains the language model to emit special reflection tokens as it generates, so the same model that writes the answer also decides when to retrieve and critiques what it retrieved. This is the LLM-judge from the middle act, internalised: the judge is no longer a separate call but a learned behaviour woven into generation.

Four reflection tokens carry the self-awareness, and each maps onto a metric we already defined. Retrieve — emitted before a segment, it decides whether retrieval is even needed here (a segment the model already knows skips it; one needing grounding triggers it). IsRel — is this passage relevant? — exactly context relevance, but self-reported. IsSup — is this claim supported by the passage? — exactly faithfulness, checked claim by claim as it writes. IsUse — is the overall response useful? — a final answer-relevance gate on the whole.

The crucial token is the first. A plain RAG loop retrieves unconditionally — even "what is two plus two?" triggers a retrieval that only adds noise and latency. Self-RAG's Retrieve token lets the model retrieve on demand: ground the parts that need grounding, answer the rest from its own parameters. Retrieval becomes a decision, not a reflex — which is precisely the question the next method, Adaptive-RAG, lifts to the level of the whole query.

But look at the loop it creates and the warning from the middle act snaps back into focus: the model is now judging itself, and we measured what happens when a model grades — verbosity \(1.0\), position \(0.6667\). A model that grades its own faithfulness can also learn to inflate its own grade. Self-RAG's reflection tokens are powerful exactly when they stay honest; an unaudited self-grader is Goodhart wearing the Ship's own face.

Naive RAG retrieves once and trusts whatever comes back. Self-correcting RAG adds a gate. CRAG first retrieves, then runs a lightweight retrieval evaluator that GRADES each document — a decision diamond with toy score thresholds.

The grade falls into three buckets by score: correct (≥ 0.7), ambiguous (0.3 .. 0.7), or wrong (< 0.3). Each is a labelled branch out of the diamond — the same retrieval, three possible verdicts.

Now act on the verdict. correct → use the retrieved context as-is. ambiguous → combine it with a corrective web search. wrong → discard it and fall back to web search or a query rewrite. The pipeline repairs its own bad retrievals.

Self-RAG goes further: it trains the model to emit reflection tokens as it generates. Retrieve? decides whether retrieval is even needed; IsRel asks if a retrieved passage is relevant; IsSup asks if the claim is supported by it. Each is a gate in the chain.

The last gate, IsUse, scores how useful the overall response is (1..5). Only a response that clears every reflection gate becomes the grounded answer — the model critiques itself before it commits, instead of trusting one retrieval blindly.

CRAG: grade the retrieval, then branch

Self-RAG put the judgement inside the model; CRAG — Corrective RAG (Yan, Gu, Zhu & Ling, arXiv:2401.15884, 2024) — keeps it explicit and external: a lightweight retrieval evaluator that grades the retrieved context before the generator ever sees it, and branches on the grade. It is the cleanest illustration of the self-correcting loop, which is why the control-flow widget above traces its decision graph.

The evaluator sorts each retrieval into one of three grades, and each grade fires a different action. Correct — confidence is high: the context is good, so refine then use it. (Correct does not mean trust verbatim — CRAG still runs its decompose-then-recompose knowledge-strip filter, splitting each document into fine-grained strips and discarding the irrelevant ones before grounding.) Ambiguous — confidence is middling: the context is partially useful but thin, so combine it with a corrective web search rather than trust it alone. Wrong — confidence is low: the context is off-base, so discard it and rewrite the query or fall back to web search, instead of grounding on noise.

That third branch is the load-bearing one, and it is exactly the missing defence against Lecture 15's catch — garbage in, fluent garbage out. A plain RAG loop has no way to say "I don't have good evidence for this"; it grounds on whatever came back, however irrelevant, and the generator confidently summarises noise. CRAG gives the loop an off-ramp: when the corpus fails, reach outside it or re-ask, rather than confidently fabricate. It is the feedback edge from the formal loop, with the grade computed on retrieval and the branch reaching for web search when retrieval comes up empty.

Adaptive-RAG: should we retrieve at all?

Self-RAG let the model skip retrieval segment by segment; Adaptive-RAG (Jeong, Baek, Cho, Hwang & Park, NAACL 2024, arXiv:2403.14403) lifts that decision to the whole query and asks the prior question: how hard is this question, and how much machinery does it deserve? It routes each query by estimated complexity before committing any retrieval at all.

Three routes. A simple factual question the model already knows — "what is the capital of France?" — needs no retrieval; answer it straight from parameters, fast and cheap. A single-hop question — one fact lookup away — gets one retrieval and a single generation pass. A multi-hop question gets the paper's IRCoT backend, interleaving chain-of-thought reasoning and retrieval until it answers or reaches the step limit. A similar router could run ReAct, but that would be an engineering adaptation, not the published setup. Match the cost of the method to the difficulty of the question.

This is the efficiency counterpart to everything before it. The earlier methods made the loop smarter; Adaptive-RAG makes it cheaper by not paying for machinery a question does not need — no retrieval latency on trivia, no multi-hop loop on a one-hop lookup. It closes the agentic menu with a routing decision that sits above retrieve-augment-generate, deciding how much of the pipeline to even run. And notice the routing classifier is itself a small judge — so the whole agentic toolkit, top to bottom, is built from the evaluation primitives this lecture established.

The agentic menu

Four methods, one loop, four settings of its knobs — lay them on a menu so you know which to reach for. ReAct: interleave reasoning and acting; the agent retrieves on demand across hops, with success judged by the reasoning trace itself. Reach for it on multi-hop questions where one retrieval cannot hold the answer. Self-RAG: the model emits reflection tokens to gate retrieval and critique its own output. Reach for it when you can train or fine-tune the generator and want the judgement learned, not bolted on.

CRAG: an external evaluator grades the retrieval and branches — use, augment, or discard-and-rewrite. Reach for it when retrieval quality is unreliable and you need an off-ramp to web search; the original method fine-tunes a T5-large retrieval evaluator, while the generator can stay fixed. A ready-made LLM grader is a CRAG-inspired variant. Adaptive-RAG: route by question complexity — no retrieval, single retrieval, or full loop. Reach for it when cost and latency matter and your traffic spans easy and hard questions.

They compose. A production system might route with Adaptive-RAG, run ReAct on the hard route, grade each retrieval with a CRAG-style evaluator, and have a Self-RAG-trained model critique its own segments — and then evaluate the whole thing with RAGAS and a swap-averaged LLM-judge. The menu is not four rival options; it is four layers of self-awareness you can stack on the same retrieve-augment-generate spine. Each one moves a little more of the judgement from outside the loop to inside it — which is exactly the arc that lets the Ship, finally, grade and correct itself.

RAGAS vs. judge vs. human

Three witnesses, three trade-offs — pick by what you can afford and what you need to know. RAGAS is cheap, fast, and decomposed: four interpretable metrics that localise blame to the generator or the retriever. Its weakness is that it needs a factual, retrieval-grounded question and runs on a judging model whose blind spots it inherits. Use it for continuous monitoring — every answer, every day — and for diagnosis when something breaks.

The LLM-judge is also cheap and fast but holistic: it reads the whole answer and renders an overall verdict, catching coherence and tone that RAGAS's per-claim view misses. Its weakness is the one this lecture spent a whole act on — position bias \((0.6667)\), verbosity bias \((1.0)\), and gameability. Use it for ranking candidate systems (pairwise, swap-averaged) and as a holistic gate, but never as the sole optimisation target.

Human evaluation is the gold standard: it captures nuance, intent, and quality no automated metric can, and it is the ground truth the other two are validated against. Its weakness is obvious — it is slow, expensive, and does not scale to live traffic. Use it for the small curated set you check before every release, and as the anchor that keeps your automated judges honest. The practical pipeline is all three in concert: RAGAS for breadth, the LLM-judge for ranking, humans for the anchor — the cheap metrics watching production, the expensive truth keeping the cheap metrics from drifting into Goodhart's trap.

What the human sample buys

The previous beat called humans the anchor that keeps the cheap proxies honest. Time to say what an anchor mechanically does, or it stays a pretty word. Write any proxy's score as a sum of three terms — \( \hat{s}=s+b+\varepsilon \) — where \(\hat{s}\) is what you see, \(s\) the true quality you cannot reach, \(b\) the judge's systematic offset, and \(\varepsilon\) its run-to-run scatter. One unpleasant consequence follows immediately: running the judge over more questions and averaging shrinks \(\varepsilon\) and does not move \(b\) by a hair. A bigger test set never fixes a biased judge; it only reports the biased number more precisely. Which is why an absolute proxy score is unreadable on its own — an unknown term sits inside it.

Now subtract one system from another under one and the same judge: \( \hat{s}_A-\hat{s}_B=(s_A-s_B)+(b_A-b_B)+\varepsilon' \). If the offset is shared — pressing on A and on B alike — it leaves in the difference, and what remains is what you wanted: the gap in true quality. That is the algebraic reason pairwise comparison is safer than absolute scoring; the pointwise-versus-pairwise beat named it in words, here it is visible in the line. And that is why, of all the biases in the act on judge biases, verbosity is the poisonous one: answers differ in length, so \(b\) depends on the answer itself, so it stops being shared and no longer cancels in the difference. Position bias you can at least average away by swapping the order; a bias tied to the content of the answer you cannot.

One name is left to write in the same letters: Goodhart. Optimise \(\hat{s}\) and the system drifts toward wherever \(b\) is larger, because adding to \(b\) comes cheaper than adding to \(s\); the number climbs while true quality stands still — exactly the flip computed by hand in that act. And now it is clear what a small human-labelled sample buys: not truth — it is small and will never cover live traffic — but an estimate of \(b\). That is calibration: you are not measuring the system's quality, you are measuring the judge's offset. Three rules follow that until now sounded like etiquette and are now derived — keep the sample held out and frozen (train against it and you lose your only estimate of \(b\)), compare under one judge, and report the difference, not the absolute.

Faithfulness vs. relevance, one more time

Of the four RAGAS metrics, two deserve a final, sharper contrast, because confusing them is the most common evaluation mistake — and the most dangerous. Faithfulness asks: is every claim grounded in the retrieved context? Answer relevance asks: does the answer address the question? They sound similar; they are orthogonal, and the four cells of their cross-product are four very different failure modes.

High faithfulness, high relevance — the answer is grounded and on-topic; this is the goal. Low faithfulness, low relevance — grounded in nothing and off-topic; obviously bad, and easy to catch. The dangerous cells are the diagonals. High relevance, low faithfulness is the worst case in all of search: an answer that is perfectly on-topic and confidently fabricated — Confabulous's exact signature, the fluent lie. High faithfulness, low relevance is the quieter failure: every claim is true and sourced, but the answer wanders away from what was actually asked — correct, grounded, and useless.

The lesson the spine has been driving toward: a single quality score collapses these into one number and hides exactly the distinction that matters. Search engineering lives in the off-diagonal — the confident fabrication that a naive judge rewards for sounding good (and a verbosity-biased judge rewards more for being long), and the dutiful, grounded answer to a question nobody asked. You need both numbers, separately, every time. That is the entire reason RAGAS decomposes rather than averages, and it is the discipline that lets the Ship trust its own self-grade.

When to reach for what

Pull the whole lecture into a decision. Building a RAG system? Instrument it with RAGAS from day one — the four metrics turn "it feels worse this week" into "context recall dropped, so retrieval regressed," and that localisation is worth more than any single quality score. Comparing two systems or prompts? Run a pairwise LLM-judge, but always swap-and-average to defuse position bias, and keep verbosity in check by capping length or measuring the preference rate.

Shipping to production? Keep a small human-graded set as the anchor you validate the automated judges against before every release — the cheap metrics watch the long tail, the expensive truth keeps them honest. Optimising the generator? This is where the danger peaks: the moment you train against a judge, you have made it a target, so audit the judge's biases first, and never optimise hard against a metric you have not stress-tested. Goodhart is always one gradient step behind you.

Multi-hop or tool-using questions? Reach for the agentic loop — ReAct for reasoning across hops, CRAG when retrieval is unreliable, Self-RAG when you can train the judgement in, Adaptive-RAG when cost matters. The unifying rule across all of it: every metric is a target in waiting. Measure honestly, decompose rather than average, keep a human anchor, and treat your own evaluation as something to be evaluated. That posture is the whole of judging the Oracle.

The judge is not ground truth

Before the payoff, one last warning that the whole lecture earns the right to make: the judge is not ground truth. It is tempting to treat a RAGAS score or an LLM-judge verdict as the answer to "is this good?" — a clean number you can chase. But every witness we called is itself a model, with measured biases (verbosity \(1.0\), position \(0.6667\)) and inherited blind spots. A high score is evidence of quality, not proof of it.

This is the deepest form of the catch. RAGAS runs on a judging LLM, so it inherits that LLM's failures; the LLM-judge games on length and position; even the agentic loop's self-grade is just an inlined judge that can learn to flatter itself. Stack them all and you have a system that grades itself — powerful, but only as trustworthy as the honesty of the grade. Optimise hard enough against any of these and Goodhart's gap opens: the score climbs while the true quality stalls or falls.

So the discipline is permanent, not a phase you graduate out of. Keep a human-anchored ground truth, however small. Treat automated judges as fast, cheap proxies you continuously validate — never as the truth itself. The Ship can grade and correct itself, and that is a genuine triumph — but a self-grading system that forgets its grade is fallible is just Goodhart with extra steps. The next beat is the payoff; this beat is the asterisk that keeps the payoff honest.

The Ship grades and corrects itself

Put the whole lecture together and the Oracle is transformed. It no longer just speaks — it judges and corrects itself. RAGAS decomposes every answer into faithfulness, relevance, context precision, and context recall, so quality is a profile, not a guess. The LLM-judge renders holistic verdicts, swap-averaged against position bias and watched for verbosity. And the agentic loop folds that judgement inside: the Ship retrieves, grades its own retrieval, and — when the grade is low — retrieves again, rewrites, or reaches outside the corpus, before it ever commits to an answer.

That is the spine's return to Measure, completed for the generative era. We began ten lectures ago measuring how well the Ship found records; we now measure how well it answers, and we taught it to run that measurement on itself, turn by turn. Judging the Oracle is no longer a question we ask from the outside — it is a loop the Ship runs from the inside. The trial is over, and the defendant has become its own honest examiner.

And Goodhart? He does not vanish — he never does. But he has been named, measured, and out-engineered: we know his moves (length, position, the re-weighted criterion), we have numbers on them, and we keep a human anchor he cannot game. The villain who has shadowed every measure since Lecture 1 is finally a known quantity, defeated not by ignoring the metric but by auditing it. The Ship grades itself — honestly — and that hard-won honesty is what lets it sail on.

Bridge to the frontier

The Ship can now find, rank, answer, and judge — and judge itself. Across eleven lectures the spine has grown from a single leg (Rank) to four (Get Data, Rank, Generate, Measure), and the Measure leg, the one that began this whole journey with precision and recall, has finally caught up to the generative era. The Oracle speaks, and it grades what it speaks. The courtroom we built today is the last piece of machinery the standard pipeline needs.

What is left is the frontier — the open edge where the standard pipeline runs out and the live research begins. Longer context windows that threaten to swallow retrieval whole; multimodal search over images, audio, and code; reasoning models that plan their own retrieval; the agents that turn search into something closer to an autonomous colleague. The next lecture (L20) sails past the edge of the map, into the questions the field has not yet settled — carrying everything the Ship has learned: index, rank, generate, and the hard-won discipline of measuring honestly. Judging the Oracle was the last courtroom; next, the deep field.
