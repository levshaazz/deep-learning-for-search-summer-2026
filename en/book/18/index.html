The page that forgot who it was about

At my reading desk a visitor leans in and asks about a certain city — how many people live there? The answer is already in the book on my lap: a single line, Its 3.85 million residents..., written plainly on page forty. The line is right there. Yet send the question to the archive and the retriever walks straight past it, as if it were invisible.

Here is why, and it stings. Before I shelved this book I did what everyone does — I tore the pages out and read each one alone. But the answer page never says Berlin; that name sat three pages back, and I tore it away. Now the line reads Its 3.85 million residents... — whose? The page has forgotten who it is about, so a question about Berlin sails right over the orphan that holds its answer.

That is the whole ache of this chapter, and the whole fix. In L17 we spent an entire forge on the question, rewriting it until the archive could read it. Here the question is fine. The damage was done to the documents long before it arrived — at the moment I cut them. I don't need a smarter model or a bigger index — I need to stop tearing first. Read the whole book, then lay the ruler across the spine and mark the chunks. We change when you pool, not the model.

You can't fit a whole book on one index card

At my reading desk I once tried a foolish thing: to press an entire book onto a single index card. One embedding, one vector \(v\), meant to hold every chapter at once. It does not hold. Ask it about the harbour and it mumbles about the mountains too; every specific fact dissolves into a grey average of the whole. The longer the text, the flatter the card — the model calls this length-induced collapse, and I call it a book you can no longer read.

So I stopped cramming. I cut the book into small pages and gave each its own card. Now the card about the harbour smells only of salt and rope; when a reader asks for the harbour, that one card lights up and the mountains stay quiet. Small chunks keep a single clear scent, and retrieval walks straight to the right page instead of to the blurred whole.

This is why we chunk at all — not for tidiness, but because small segments simply retrieve better. And so the standard move became almost a reflex: cut the book into chunks first, then embed each one. Chunk-then-embed. Chunk Norris sharpens his blade at the very first line... and that eager cut-first order, as we shall see, is exactly where the trouble begins.

A referent is named once and then carried by pronouns. Take a typical distance of g = 40 tokens between the naming and the mention that needs it. Nothing has been cut yet.

Cut at s = 32 tokens. The chunk is shorter than the gap itself, so a seam falls between the pair no matter where the grid starts: min(1, 40/32) = 1. Every single mention is orphaned.

Double it to s = 64 and part of the pairs collapse safely inside one chunk: 40/64 = 0.625. Still nearly two mentions in three do not know who they are about.

s = 128: 40/128 = 0.3125. About one mention in three is still severed from its referent. Notice the curve is a hyperbola, not a straight line — the first doublings buy the most.

s = 256 — the paper's fixed-size setting. 40/256 = 0.1563: roughly one chunk with a pronoun in six still does not know its subject. That is the share late chunking hands back.

s = 512: 40/512 = 0.0781. The curve has visibly settled — big chunks mostly hold their own referent, which is why the naive pipeline catches up there and the late-chunking gain shrinks.

Do not cut at all and nobody is orphaned — but length collapse returns and the answer is averaged away. Now the test: the law predicts smaller chunks → bigger gain, and the three published strategies obey it, three out of three. And the counterexample: on Needle-8192 the neighbours are pure filler, Δ is negative, and min(1, g/s) says nothing about that case.

Where the gap law comes from

You have just swept a curve; here is where it comes from, in two lines you can redo on a napkin. A mention leans on a referent named \(g\) tokens earlier. The chunker lays a grid of boundaries across the text with pitch \(s\), and it lays it down wherever the document happens to begin — so relative to any particular mention, the grid's offset is arbitrary. Ask one question: does a boundary fall inside the stretch between the referent and the mention? That stretch is \(g\) long and the grid repeats every \(s\), so a seam lands inside it with probability \(g/s\), and once \(g\) reaches \(s\) it lands there for certain. Survivors \(1-g/s\); orphans \(\min(1,\,g/s)\).

Be clear about the status of that line, because it was not read off anybody's table — it is a model, and mine. It assumes a grid of one pitch at an arbitrary offset, one referent per mention, and a gap that does not care where the boundaries happen to be. Real chunkers bend all three: sentence boundaries are not evenly spaced, a chunk may hold several mentions, and a splitter that cuts at paragraph breaks cuts precisely where dependencies are least likely to straddle. So do not read magnitudes off it. Read its shape — a hyperbola, not a straight line, which is why the first doublings of the chunk buy far more than the last ones, and why "tune the chunk size" changes character the moment you can picture that curve.

A model earns its keep by predicting something it was never fitted to, and this one predicts an ordering. The smaller the chunks a strategy produces, the more mentions its blade severs, so the gain from repairing them should be largest for the strategy with the smallest chunks and smallest for the one with the largest. Line the published chunking strategies up by the size of the pieces they make, and the measured gains fall in exactly that order. That is a far weaker claim than getting the magnitudes right — and a far cheaper one to check, which is the honest reason to carry the law through the rest of this chapter rather than merely admire it.

"Just use bigger chunks"

The law hands everyone the same idea at the same moment, and it is wrong in an instructive way. If \(\min(1,\,g/s)\) falls as \(s\) grows, then grow \(s\): make the chunks big enough to swallow the referent, and the orphans go away. The arithmetic even agrees with you. The trouble is that \(s\) is not a free parameter lying around waiting to be turned — it is the same knob you already turned, hard, in the opposite direction, and you turned it for a reason.

The reason was the opening page of this chapter. A card that covers more text points less sharply, and past some width a long chunk retrieves worse than the small ones you would have cut it into. So growing \(s\) buys back exactly the loss that made you chunk in the first place. Two effects, one knob, pulling opposite ways — which is why chunk size has an interior best value on every corpus, why that value is different on every corpus, and why nobody has ever published a size that is right everywhere.

And the second half of the objection is the half that settles it: the seam never leaves. Whatever pitch you choose, the grid still cuts somewhere, and every boundary still severs whatever dependency happened to straddle it. Bigger chunks make the cuts rarer, not gentler; the referent that used to sit a page away now sits a chapter away and is still on the other side of a blade. The size knob moves the seam; it cannot remove it. The knob that removes it is a different one entirely — not where you cut, but when you pool — and that is the whole of the rest of this chapter.

Tear out the page, and the sentence forgets whose it is

Let me show you the old way — the one I mean to cure you of. I tear the pages out of the book first, then hand each loose page to a clerk and tell him: read only yours. One clerk holds a page that begins, "Its 3.85 million residents…" He looks up, baffled — whose residents? The word that said Berlin was on a page I already tore away.

That amnesia costs us at retrieval time. Ask about Berlin, and the chunk that literally names Berlin answers loudest — reported cosine \(0.8486\). But the chunk that actually holds the fact, "Its 3.85 million residents…", scores only \(0.7084\); "The city is also a state" reaches just \(0.7535\). The sentences that carry the answer rank below the one that merely says the name.

This is the coreference gap, and it is the whole trouble in a single line. It, its, the city — each such word is a promise that its subject lives nearby. Chunk-then-embed tears that neighbour away, then asks the orphaned chunk to speak for itself. It can't — so retrieval walks right past the very sentence that holds your answer.

The pronoun is only the loudest one

Every write-up on this problem shows the same example — a pronoun with nothing to point at — and the reader walks away thinking the fault is pronouns. It is not. Sit at the desk with a torn page and read what else has quietly stopped meaning anything. A definite description: the city — which city? An acronym that was expanded once, in a heading you cut away: three capital letters and no expansion. A unit that lives in a table header: a bare number in a cell, and the column that said what it counted is on another page. Ellipsis, where the subject was dropped because the previous sentence had already supplied it: …and declined to comment — who did? A condition or a negation carried over from the paragraph before: in that case the threshold does not apply — in which case?

Line them up and they are not six problems. They are one break, filed under six grammatical addresses. Write the chunk's text as a substring \(c_i \subset D\) and its meaning as a function of what a reader can see; for every one of those five plus the pronoun, the argument the function needs sits at a position outside \([\mathrm{start}_i,\mathrm{end}_i]\). The grammar differs, the failure is identical: the chunk's meaning rests on words beyond its own boundary, and the encoder is handed only what is inside.

That reframing decides which repair is worth building. A fix aimed at pronouns — run coreference resolution over the document and substitute the antecedents in — repairs one row of the table and leaves the other five standing, and it costs a parser pass over the whole corpus. A fix aimed at the boundary repairs the whole column at once, because it never removes the outside words from the encoder's view in the first place. Before you accept that argument, do the cheap thing: take a hundred of your own chunks, read them cold as if you had never seen the document, and tally which of the six bites you. The mix tells you how much this chapter is worth on your corpus — and it is a different mix for contracts, for support tickets, and for scientific abstracts.

Chunk Norris cuts a beat too soon

You've met Chunk Norris — my disciplined friend from the tenth lecture, who roundhouse-kicks a long scroll into clean, equal passages and fusses over the overlap so nothing slips between the slices. It is beautiful footwork, and I have leaned on it for years. But this time, as his heel came around, I raised a hand: wait.

His trouble was never where the blade lands — it was when. He cuts before anyone has read the book. The instant the scroll flies apart, the page reading "Its 3.85 million residents…" is left on its own, and the name it belonged to — Berlin, two pages upstream — has landed in a different pile. The slice is tidy and orphaned: it no longer knows who it is about, so retrieval strolls right past the answer it is holding.

So I am not asking him to kick softer or aim truer. I am asking him to kick later. Read the whole book first, let every page soak up its subject, and only then lay the ruler across the spine and mark the chunks. Late chunking is exactly that one correction — the same clean slices, but the cut falls after the reading, never before it.

Every splitter turns the same knob

You already own a shelf of splitters from the chunking chapter: cut every fixed number of tokens, cut at sentence ends, walk recursively down a list of separators, or hunt for the point where the topic turns. Read all four through the law and they collapse into one family. Fixed-size holds the pitch constant and lets the offset fall where it falls. Sentence splitting makes the pitch smaller, so it manufactures more orphans, not fewer. Semantic splitting makes it larger and moves the seams toward places where a cut is less likely to matter. Every one of them is an argument about \(s\), and about where the offset lands. Not one of them can put inside a chunk a word that is not inside it.

Overlap is the same story in a disguise. Letting each chunk borrow a margin of its neighbour genuinely repairs the short-range case — a referent a line or two back now falls inside the borrowed strip, and the chunk stops being an orphan. But the margin is measured in tens of tokens, while the referent in a long article was named in its opening paragraph, thousands of tokens upstream. Overlap is a neighbour-scale patch on a document-scale wound, and widening it until it reaches costs you a second copy of most of the document.

Which is why the correction in this chapter is not a fifth splitter. Keep every boundary rule you already have — they remain the right tools for the job they actually do, which is deciding what a retrievable unit is, and that job has nothing to do with context. Change the moment instead. Nothing on that shelf is thrown away by pooling late: the boundaries stay exactly where the splitter put them, the chunk count is unchanged, and the only thing that moves is how much the encoder had read by the time those boundaries were applied.

Read the Whole Book First — Mark the Chunks Last

Here is the whole correction, and it is smaller than you'd fear. I stop tearing the pages out. I leave the book bound and I read it cover to cover, so that every page hears every other — the chapter that names Berlin still whispering to the page that only says its 3.85 million residents. That is nothing but ordinary full-document reading — the same self-attention I'd run on any long text — carried out before a single cut is made.

Only then do I lay a ruler across the spine and mark the boundaries — this stretch a chunk, that stretch the next — and gather each stretch into one tidy index-card by averaging the pages it spans. The page about its residents was never orphaned: it did its reading while still bound to the chapter that named the city, so Berlin-ness is already written into it. One card per chunk, same size as before, ready for the same shelf.

Look closely and you'll see I invented nothing. There are only ever three moves — cut, attend, pool. The eager way does them cut → attend → pool: chop first, then let each lonely page attend to itself and pool into a card. My way does attend → cut → pool: read the bound whole, then cut, then pool. Same three moves, one swapped order — and that is why we call it late chunking. The chunking simply happens last.

The one line of difference, written in symbols

I keep saying the difference is one line, so let me put that line on the desk. The torn-page way: \(e_i = \operatorname{pool}\big(\mathrm{Enc}(c_i)\big)\). My way: \(e_i = \operatorname{pool}_{[\mathrm{start}_i,\,\mathrm{end}_i]}\big(\mathrm{Enc}(D)\big)\). Look at what is identical: \(\mathrm{Enc}\) is the same encoder with the same weights in both lines, and \(\operatorname{pool}\) is the same averaging. Only one argument moved — what I handed to the reading, a single chunk or the whole bound book.

That gives the method a name borrowed from algebra: the composition does not commute, \(\mathrm{Enc}\circ\mathrm{cut} \neq \mathrm{cut}\circ\mathrm{Enc}\). Cutting first is not a harmless reshuffle of work. It erases everything past the seam from the encoder's input, before attention ever gets the chance to look there. Nothing downstream can put it back, however good the model is: the missing pages were simply never on the desk.

Carry the line as a test you can run on any claim of the shape it is only a reordering. Ask which operator got the whole document. Here both lines hand back one card per chunk, of the same width, at the same cost when a reader walks up — so no, nobody smuggled a bigger model in under a new name. Same reading, same averaging, a different argument.

Mean pooling: lay the ruler, take the average

Let me show you the one operation at the heart of all this, and how plain it is. Once I have read the whole book, every word on the page carries its own little vector — a contextual token vector. To turn a chunk into a single index-card, I do not summarise it or rewrite it. I just take the average of the word-vectors that fall between my two ruler marks. That average, \(e_i=\tfrac1n\sum_j \vartheta_j\), is the chunk's embedding — the center of mass of its tokens. That is mean pooling, and that is the whole trick.

Now here is the only thing that separates late chunking from an ordinary document embedding. Normally you pool every token into one vector for the whole document. Late chunking pools segments of that same token stream — one continuous read, but I lay the ruler down and average each stretch between marks on its own. Naive chunking averages each torn-out page in isolation; I average slices of the bound book. Same arithmetic — a world of difference in what each average remembers.

That freedom asks for one thing: the right tool. I need a model that reads the whole document in a single pass and hands me the token vectors, not just one finished summary — a long-context, mean-pooling embedder like Jina v2 or v3 (8192 tokens) or Nomic. A model that returns only one sealed vector leaves me nothing to slice. And when the book runs longer than the window, I read it in overlapping macro-chapters — long late chunking — so no seam ever falls cold.

Four tokens, one chunk boundary after the second. Only «Berlin» and «residents» carry meaning — (4,0) and (0,4) on the two axes. A wall stands in the boundary: nothing may read across it.

Naive: chunk B is encoded ALONE. Each of its tokens can only average what it reads inside the chunk, so both come out (0,2) and the pooled chunk vector points straight up — cos to the query is exactly 0. The chunk is about residents in general.

Now encode the whole document instead. The wall falls, and the attention arcs from «Its» and «residents» reach across the boundary to «Berlin». Nothing about the cut has changed — only what the encoder was allowed to see.

One rule, applied twice: a token's vector is the average of what it may read. Reading all four tokens, both chunk-B tokens become (1,1) — «Berlin-ness» has been written into the pronoun before anyone pooled anything.

Pool the SAME segment — and the chunk vector has turned 45° onto the query ray: cos 0 → 0.7071. Note it also got shorter, from 2 to √2: reading more spreads the average thinner. Hold that thought for the Needle test.

Both vectors at once. Same document, same boundary, same model, the same one vector out — the only thing that moved is WHEN the average was taken. That is the whole method.

Twenty thousand tokens of document, and a model that can hold eight thousand one hundred and ninety-two of them at once. The window covers the first two fifths — and the answer-chunk sits far outside it, with its referent named on the very first page.

Truncate, and the tail goes dark. Late chunking is not weakened here, it is absent: the answer-chunk never reaches the index at all. Chunking a long document was worth +24,47 % relative nDCG — this throws that away wholesale.

Cut it into three touching macro-chunks instead. Every token is now indexed — but look at the arc: the dependency that crosses a seam is severed exactly as it was in ordinary naive chunking, only at a coarser scale.

Long late chunking: overlap the macro-chunks by ω tokens, so stride = l_max − ω = 7 680, and three chunks start at 0, 7 680 and 15 360. A token sitting on a seam is now read once inside a window that also contains its context — the arc holds.

And now the line everybody skips. The overlapping tokens were encoded twice, but line 14 KEEPS ONLY ONE COPY — the overlap exists so a token can see its context, not so it can enter the index twice. The bill: 21 024 tokens encoded, 1 024 of them extra, exactly ω per seam, about five per cent at index time and nothing at all at query time.

The ruler measures characters; the model counts tokens

Between the ruler and the book there is a small piece of bookkeeping nobody warns you about. The chunker hands me boundaries measured in characters — that is what a splitter knows how to count. The encoder hands me one vector per token. To average a stretch I need neither of those: I need the index of its first and its last token. Call that pair the cue — two integers that tell the pooling where to begin and where to stop.

Building the cue takes two lists and no dictionary at all: the chunk lengths in characters \(|c_1|,|c_2|,\dots\) from the chunker, and the token lengths in characters \(o_1,o_2,\dots\) from the tokenizer. Open a chunk: reset a counter \(o_{\text{chunk}}\) and remember the first token not yet handed out. Walk right, adding each token's length, \(o_{\text{chunk}}\mathrel{+}= o_i\); while the running sum trails \(|c_j|\), the token belongs to the chunk you have open. The moment the sum catches up, close it: that index is the end of the cue, the counter resets, and the ruler moves to the next mark.

In practice you do not write that loop — you ask the tokenizer for the character span of every token (return_offsets_mapping=True) and read the cues straight off it. But do write the loop once in your head, because it tells you something the library hides: a ruler mark can land in the middle of a token, and the counter resolves it in whole tokens. The boundary the model pools at is the chunker's boundary rounded to the nearest token seam — close enough to ignore, never exactly what you asked for.

The chunk that quietly disappears

Now look at where that counter closes the cue, because the published pseudocode closes it inside the condition — the assignment to the end index fires only when the running sum has caught up with the chunk length. Read the loop with a hostile eye and you can see the hole from here: what happens if the tokens run out first?

Nothing happens. That is the whole problem. The condition never fires, the cue is never completed, and the last stretch of the book — the short tail that did not fill its quota of characters — is silently dropped. No exception, no warning, no empty vector to notice: the index simply ends up one card short, and whatever answer lived on the final page has become unreachable by any query you will ever write. A bug that throws costs you an afternoon; a bug that returns a slightly smaller index costs you a fraction of your recall and never tells you.

The repair is one line — close the cue after the loop as well as inside it — but the habit is worth more than the repair. Count your cards. Assert that the number of pooled vectors equals the number of chunks the splitter returned, and assert it on a document whose last chunk is deliberately a stub. This is the general shape of index-side defects: they do not break the pipeline, they quietly shrink what the pipeline can find.

Normalise after the mean, never before

Every recipe for this method carries one instruction that looks like a detail and is not: normalise after the averaging. Write the two orders side by side and the difference stops being a matter of taste. \(\mu_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_j \vartheta_j\) averages the token vectors as they came and shortens the result to unit length afterwards. \(\tilde\mu_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_j \vartheta_j/\lVert\vartheta_j\rVert\) shortens each token first and averages the stubs. Two different vectors, and only the first one is what I have been calling a card.

The names tell you what changed. The first is a centre of mass: a long token vector pulls the average harder, so tokens the reading made confident about get more say. The second is a mean of directions: every token votes with equal weight, the loud ones and the empty ones alike. They coincide exactly when all the norms \(\lVert\vartheta_j\rVert\) are equal — which is precisely what contextual vectors are not, since the whole point of reading the bound book was to make some of them longer than others.

And there is a second, sneakier loss. The length of a pooled card is itself a reading: it records how tightly the chunk points one way, and it drops when the average is spread thin across several topics. You have felt this by hand — when the little chunk vector turned toward the query, it also came out shorter than the tokens it was made from. Normalise before the mean and you throw that quantity away before it is ever measured, which costs you the one instrument that will later show, on a page of pure filler, that the method overreached.

The whole algorithm, in four moves

Let me put the published procedure on the desk, because it is shorter than its reputation. One: tokenize the whole document — not the chunks, the document — into one long sequence. Two: run the transformer over that sequence once, and keep the contextual token vectors \(\vartheta_1,\dots,\vartheta_n\) instead of collapsing them. Three: translate the chunker's boundaries, which are counted in characters, into token spans \([\mathrm{start}_i,\mathrm{end}_i]\). Four: average each span into its own \(e_i\). Compared with making a plain document embedding, exactly one line changed: pool per segment instead of over the whole sequence. No weight is touched anywhere — the method is training-free.

Step three is the only one that is not obvious, and it is where the interesting object lives. The paper carries, per token, a quantity \(o_i\) that people persistently misread as an offset. It is not a position; it is the length of token \(i\) in characters. With it, the translation is a running total and nothing more: open a chunk, set the counter to zero, walk right adding \(o_i\), and the moment the counter reaches the chunk's own character length \(|c_j|\), close the chunk and start the next. That accumulator is the entire bridge between a chunker that thinks in characters and a model that thinks in tokens — no alignment table, no shared lexicon, no second tokenizer.

Then note the fencepost in the averaging, because it decides whether your reimplementation matches the paper's: the span is inclusive at both ends, so the divisor is \((\mathrm{cue}_{end}+1)-\mathrm{cue}_{start}\), not the difference of the cues. Off by one there and every chunk near the boundary is averaged over a slightly wrong set — a bug that never crashes, never warns, and shows up only as a ranking that is mysteriously a little worse than the paper's. Read the four moves once more and notice what is absent from all of them: any new parameter, any new model, any new call at query time. That absence is the whole commercial argument of the method.

The tokens you never asked for

Write the four moves out in real code and a nuisance appears that no formula mentions. Your tokenizer does not hand back only your words: it adds service tokens at the ends — the opening classification token, the closing separator — and, on the models this method actually runs on, a task prefix telling the encoder whether it is reading a query or a document. None of those exist in the character stream your chunker measured, so the running total that maps characters to tokens knows nothing about them.

Now the trap. A tokenizer that reports character spans gives those service tokens the empty span \((0, 0)\) — start equals end. If you select a chunk's tokens with a test like the token starts before the chunk ends, the trailing separator passes it, every single time, because its start is zero and zero is before everything. Do that and each chunk quietly pools all the way to the end of the document: every vector drifts toward the same document average, the ranking degrades a little everywhere, and nothing in the run logs says a word. Guard against it by requiring the span to be non-empty, then put the service tokens back deliberately — the opening token and the task prefix belong to the pooling of the first chunk, the separator to the last.

The second nuisance is not a bug but a relapse. If the model call sits inside the loop over chunks, you have written the torn-page method again, with extra ceremony: one forward pass per chunk means attention never crosses a boundary, and the vectors you get are the naive ones no matter how carefully you pooled them. There is a cheap test that catches both faults at once — embed a document, then embed it again with the chunk boundaries shifted by a few characters. Under a correct implementation the vectors move a little; under a relapse into one-pass-per-chunk they move exactly as much as re-encoding different texts, and under the \((0, 0)\) trap they barely move at all, because every chunk was reading the same thing.

And you can train for it, too

I have told you four times that this trick is training-free, and every one of those times was true. But I owe you the rest of the sentence: the same paper that proposes late chunking also proposes training for it, and if I leave that out you will walk away thinking training-free is what the method IS, rather than where it starts.

The trained variant is called span pooling, and the recipe is one you already know. Take triples — a query, a document, and a pair of offsets \(\langle \text{start}, \text{end}\rangle\) marking the span that answers it — and train the model to emit a vector for that span directly, instead of averaging it after the fact. The loss is InfoNCE: the very same objective you built a batch for in the lecture on negatives. The data is FEVER and TriviaQA, about four hundred and seventy thousand pairs, batches of 512, five hundred steps. Nothing exotic.

And what does it buy? Very little, honestly. The best cell in their table gains under a point of nDCG, and three cells go slightly backwards. Which is exactly why I am telling you: the gap between "average the span" and "learn the span" is small, so the free version is not a poor cousin of the trained one. It is most of the trained one. Training-free is the floor of this method — and the ceiling turns out to be within arm's reach of it.

By the time I pick up the ruler, every 'it' already knows it means Berlin

Here is the whole trick, and it is quieter than you'd expect. When I read the book cover to cover before I cut, full-document attention lets the word Berlin reach forward across every page and press a little of itself into each later its, each the city. By the time I pick up the ruler, those token vectors are no longer orphans — Berlin is already written inside them.

So the chunk "Its 3.85 million residents…" is not a naked pronoun waiting for a subject that got torn away; it carries its subject in its bones. That is coreference resolution — the thing we used to beg a large model to do by rewriting the text — and here it happens for free, as a side effect of attention. No LLM is asked a single question. The referent-free chunks that scored a dull 0.71 and 0.75 quietly climb to 0.82 and 0.85 (reported).

The deeper shift: every chunk-card is now conditional, not i.i.d. Naive chunking embeds each piece in isolation — independent, and identically blind to its neighbours. Late chunking makes each vector a \( \text{chunk} \mid \text{document} \), aware of what came before it. Same model, same dimension, same index; the only thing I changed is that I let each card read the room before I filed it.

Both directions at once — and why a GPT cannot do this

There is a sentence about late chunking that sounds right, is printed in the paper's own figure caption, repeated in its README, and is nonetheless wrong: that a chunk becomes conditioned on the chunks before it — \(v_k \sim Q(c_k \mid c_1,\dots,c_{k-1})\). It reads plausibly because reading is something we do left to right, and because we have all been trained by chat models to imagine text arriving one token at a time.

The truth is simpler and stronger: \(v_k \sim Q(c_k \mid D)\). The conditioning goes both ways. An encoder's attention matrix is fully connected — every token sees every other token, in front of it and behind it, in the same pass. When I read the whole book before I mark the pages, the last chapter shapes the first exactly as much as the first shapes the last. A referent introduced on page two hundred can reach back and light up a pronoun on page three.

That is not a decorative detail; it decides which models can do this at all. An autoregressive decoder wears a triangular mask by construction: token \(j\) may look backwards and nowhere else. Run late chunking on such a model and the first half of your document never learns that the second half exists — you would get half a repair and pay full price for it. So when someone offers you a fashionable decoder-based embedder for this job, the question is not how big its context window is. The question is whether it is allowed to look forward.

Independent chunks, or conditional ones

There is a clean statistical name for what changed, and having it makes the difference arguable instead of merely felt. Under the torn-page order each chunk is encoded in isolation, so the vectors are independent and identically distributed — draw \(e_i\) knowing nothing about \(e_{i-1}\) and you have lost nothing, because \(e_i\) never depended on it. Under the read-first order every vector is conditional on the whole document: \(e_i = f_i(D)\), a function of the entire text, of which the chunk's own span is one argument among many.

Say what that costs and what it buys, in that order. It costs independence, and independence is a convenience you were quietly enjoying: with i.i.d. chunks you may re-embed one paragraph after an edit and leave the rest of the document's cards untouched. Conditional chunks do not grant that. Edit a sentence in the opening paragraph and, strictly, every card in that document is stale, because every one of them was computed from a text that no longer exists. In practice the drift is small far from the edit, but the correct invalidation unit stopped being the chunk and became the document — and that is an indexing-pipeline fact, not a modelling detail.

What it buys is the only thing the chapter has been after: the vector of a chunk that says the city now carries the city's name, because the name was in the conditioning set when attention ran. Notice this also explains a boundary you will meet again — the repair reaches exactly as far as the conditioning does. Everything inside one reading is conditioned on everything else inside it; nothing is conditioned on what the encoder never saw. So the honest way to describe the method is not chunks now have context but chunks are now conditioned on one reading of the document, and every limit of the method is a limit on how much fits in a reading.

Not a small weight — a missing column

Let me write out one row of that fully connected matrix, because the whole chapter hides inside its index. For one head of one layer, the vector a token ends up carrying is \(\vartheta_j=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\alpha_{jk}\,V_k\), with the weights \(\alpha_{jk}=\dfrac{\exp\!\big(Q_j^{\top}K_k/\sqrt{d}\big)}{\sum_{m\in\mathcal{K}}\exp\!\big(Q_j^{\top}K_m/\sqrt{d}\big)}\). The \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\) here are attention's own query, key and value from the transformer lecture — not the reader's question at my desk. Everything I am about to say is about one symbol: \(\mathcal{K}\), the set the sum runs over.

Read the whole book first and \(\mathcal{K}=D\): the token Its has a Berlin column, and that column can take as much weight as the model thinks it deserves. Tear the pages out first and \(\mathcal{K}=c_i\): the sum runs over the orphan chunk alone, and there is no Berlin column at all. This is the sentence to keep. The naive order does not give the referent a small weight — it leaves out the term. A weight can be nudged by training, by a better prompt, by a longer sentence; a term that is not in the sum cannot be nudged by anything.

That distinction changes how you debug. If you catch yourself saying the model underweights the subject, you are describing a failure the arithmetic does not have. Cut-first does not misjudge the referent, it deletes it from the input; and the only repair for a deleted input is to stop deleting it. Which is exactly the one line we swapped — and it is why the repair is total inside a reading, rather than a matter of degree.

And the denominator moved too

There is a second consequence sitting in that same row, and it is the one people walk past. The softmax normalises over the same set the sum runs over — the denominator is \(\sum_{m\in\mathcal{K}}\). Shrink \(\mathcal{K}\) to a single torn page and the weights of the surviving neighbours grow, although not one logit has changed. So cutting first does two things at once: it removes the referent, and it redistributes the referent's weight among whoever happened to land on the same scrap. The orphan chunk does not merely forget Berlin; it becomes disproportionately certain about the words it was torn out with.

Now read the same denominator in the other direction, because this is where the chapter's happy ending gets its price tag. Give the reading the whole bound book and \(\mathcal{K}\) grows, so every individual neighbour's share falls. On a document that really is about one thing that is exactly right — the referent claims its share and the card comes home. On a book of eight thousand pages of filler, the identical line of algebra spreads a card thin over words that had nothing to do with it. One expression, two outcomes: the lift you have just measured and the failure waiting at the end of this chapter are the same denominator, read at two different documents.

The lift lands where the tear was

Now let me lay two rulers side by side and read the numbers off them. Before, when I tore the pages out and read each alone, the two orphan chunks — the one that said "Its 3.85 million residents…" and the one that said "The city is also a state" — scored a thin \(0.7084\) and \(0.7535\) against the name they had lost. They were about Berlin, but nothing on the torn page remembered it.

Read the whole book first, then run the ruler down the spine, and the same two chunks jump: \(0.7084 \to 0.8249\) and \(0.7535 \to 0.8498\). The lift lands exactly on the wounds. Full-document attention had already written "Berlin"-ness into the Its and the city tokens before I ever pooled them — so the orphans came home.

And the chunk that never lost its subject? The page still saying "Berlin, the capital of Germany" out loud barely stirs: \(0.8486 \to 0.8495\), a rounding-error nudge. That is the whole shape of the method in one row of a table — it heals precisely where the tear was, and leaves untouched what was already whole. That chunk never needed the help.

Where the lift comes from: the shift, split in two

The column of gains you have just read is not three unrelated facts — it is one expression evaluated three times, and the expression is worth having. Give the change a name: \(\delta_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_j\big(\vartheta_j^{\,\mathrm{late}}-\vartheta_j^{\,\mathrm{naive}}\big)\), the chunk-mean shift of the token vectors when the reading is given the whole book. To first order in that shift, with the reader's question scaled to unit length, \(\Delta\cos \approx \dfrac{\langle q,\delta_i\rangle}{\lVert e_i\rVert} - \cos(q,e_i)\,\dfrac{\langle e_i,\delta_i\rangle}{\lVert e_i\rVert^{2}}\).

Two terms, and they are the two halves of the story. The first is the turn toward the query: the part of the shift that points where the reader is looking, and the only thing that lifts a score. The second is the length penalty: a shift along the card's own direction makes it longer without making it point better, and cosine refuses to pay for length, so that part is subtracted back out. Now the column reads itself. The chunk that already said Berlin had the name in its input all along, so \(\delta_i\) is near zero and both terms are near zero — it barely stirs. The chunk built on a pronoun gets a shift aimed straight at its referent, and the question was about that referent, so the first term is large and the card climbs.

The same two terms also predict a failure you have not met yet, which is what makes the expansion worth carrying rather than admiring. Let the surrounding text be irrelevant to the question. The shift is still there — attention still mixes the neighbourhood in — but now it points somewhere the reader is not looking, so the first term dies while the second does not. The gain does not go to zero. It goes negative. Nothing broke; the vector simply moved, and the direction it moved in was nobody's answer.

A cosine that rises is not yet a result

Three cosines moved and it is tempting to call that the finding. It is not, and the reason is structural: retrieval is ordinal. The reader never sees a similarity, they see a list. Add the same constant to every score in the index and not one ranking changes; scale them all and nothing changes either. So a lift in one chunk's cosine is evidence of nothing at all until you say what it moved past.

Which is why the shape of that little table matters more than its column of gains. The chunk that already named its subject barely stirred; the two that leaned on a pronoun climbed. The lift was differential — it landed on some cards and not on others — and only a differential lift can reorder anything. Had every card in the corpus risen by the same amount, the method would have produced a very satisfying column of positive numbers and precisely zero improvement in what anybody retrieves.

So carry the cosine deltas as a mechanism check, never as a result. They tell you the repair happened where you predicted it would, which is exactly what a worked example is for, and it is why the next thing on the desk has to be a case where two chunks trade places. The scoreboard from the metrics chapter measures orders rather than distances for this reason — and an evaluation reported in similarities instead of a ranking metric has skipped the step at which the claim becomes something anybody could disprove.

The answer that couldn't prove it was about ACME

Come to my reading desk. I lay out one tiny memo about a company, ACME, and the captain hands me his query: acme revenue growth. I do it the naive way first — tear the memo into chunks, then score each torn page alone. The page that actually holds the answer reads it highlighted 3% growth. But it is a pronoun, and the name ACME sat two sentences up, on a page I already tore away. Cosine to the query: a limp \(0.5164\).

Meanwhile a bland decoy — a chunk that literally says revenue growth about nobody in particular — scores \(0.5774\) and takes rank 1. My gold answer sits at rank 2, out-ranked by a distractor that shares the query's surface words but none of its meaning. The answer is lying right there on the desk, and retrieval walks straight past it, because the gold chunk cannot prove it is about ACME.

Now read the whole memo first, then lay the ruler across the spine and pool. Full-document attention has already bled ACME-ness into that lonely it before I mark a single boundary. The gold chunk's cosine climbs to \(0.7071\) and steps into rank 1; the decoy drops behind it. Same text, same query, same index — the ordering simply inverts. That one flip is the whole lecture, measured.

A small win, paid for with nothing

Now the honest part. I lay the benchmark on my desk — BeIR, scored by nDCG@10 (the ranking metric from L5, the same ruler we grade any retriever with), averaged over three embedding models and four datasets — and I read the two columns side by side. Sentence chunks: \( 52.4 \to 54.3 \). Fixed 256-token chunks: \( 52.2 \to 54.0 \). Late chunking wins in every column, but it wins by a whisker — call it a point and a half.

Where does that whisker come from? Look at the largest cell: NFCorpus, a corpus thick with the-city-style back-references, climbs \( 23.5 \to 30.0 \). That is where reading-the-whole-book-first truly bites — where chunks lean on a subject named pages away. On flatter, self-contained corpora the lift is smaller, sometimes barely there. I will not sell you a revolution.

But read the price tag, not just the gain. That point and a half costs nothing: no new training, no bigger vectors, no extra call when a query arrives — you changed only when you pool, not which model you run. A small win, handed to you for free, is still a win worth taking.

One more thing, because averages are polite liars. Thirty-six cells sit behind those three rows — three chunking strategies, three models, four datasets — and the honest score is thirty-three wins, two ties and one loss. The loss is a tenth of a point, on one model and one dataset, and it matters not because it is large but because it exists: a deck that tells you 'late never loses' and then, fourteen slides later, that late loses on Needle tests, is arguing with itself. And while you are down there in the table, notice that the paper's own prose rounds a gain to one and a half points while its own twelve semantic cells give \( +1.41 \) — that is the semantic row, not the fixed-size one. Read the table, not the abstract.

Then someone else ran it

Everything I have shown you so far was measured by the people who invented the method. That is not an accusation — it is how a new idea always arrives — but it is a fact about the evidence, and you should hold it in your hand while you read the numbers. So let me put a second hand on the table. In 2025 an independent group ran late chunking across other models and other corpora, published the code under MIT, and got a rougher picture.

On one benchmark with the BGE-M3 encoder, switching from early to late pooling did not merely fail to help — the score fell to under a third of what it had been. That is not a shrug, that is a rout. On passage-length MS MARCO, early pooling won outright: the documents are short, there is no distant referent to rescue, and pooling late only spreads the average thinner. And head-to-head against Anthropic's contextual retrieval, late chunking lost on every metric they reported — narrowly, and standing alone against a three-part pipeline (contextualisation + BM25 rank fusion + a reranker), but it lost.

Their conclusion, word for word, is that late chunking does not consistently outperform the early approach across all models and datasets. I want you to hear that flatly and not flinch. It does not undo the Berlin example or the BeIR table; those are still real. What it does is turn my advice from a rule into a condition: this trick pays when your encoder is bidirectional, long-context and pools by mean, and when your documents are long and actually about one thing. Outside that fence, the outcome ranges from nothing to a disaster — and checking the fence takes an afternoon, while rebuilding an index takes a week.

A point and a percentage are different animals

You have just read two kinds of number in quick succession: a gain measured in the metric's own units, and a share of something removed. Line them up in one sentence and you will be comparing quantities that do not belong on the same shelf. Write both down. The absolute move is \(\Delta_{\mathrm{abs}}=M_{\mathrm{late}}-M_{\mathrm{naive}}\). The relative one is \(r=\dfrac{f_{\mathrm{naive}}-f_{\mathrm{late}}}{f_{\mathrm{naive}}}\), the fraction of failures removed, where \(f=1-R\) is the share of queries the system missed.

One line of substitution turns the second into \(r=\dfrac{R_{\mathrm{late}}-R_{\mathrm{naive}}}{1-R_{\mathrm{naive}}}\), and now you can see the trap. The numerator is exactly the absolute gain — the point. The denominator is the headroom: how much was left to fix before anyone started. For a fixed absolute gain, a stronger baseline has fewer remaining failures in the denominator, so its relative failure reduction is larger. This is the opposite of relative metric growth, which divides the gain by the metric's starting value. Always distinguish the two denominators.

So make the question a reflex, and ask it of your own reports as sharply as of a vendor's. Not by what percentage, but a percentage of what — and measured on which collection. That is also why a relative drop in failures on one shop's internal mixture and an absolute gain in the metric on a public benchmark cannot be added, averaged, or ranked against each other. They are not two readings of one instrument. They are two instruments.

Buy the pooling before you buy the chunker

Here is the comparison that reorders a backlog. Take the most expensive way to place boundaries — embed every sentence, hunt for the points where the topic turns, cut there — and combine it with pooling done the old way. Then take the cheapest way there is — cut every so many tokens and stop thinking — and combine it with pooling done late. On the paper's own averages the second beats the first. The dumbest boundaries with the better order beat the cleverest boundaries with the worse one.

That is not a paradox once you separate the two jobs. Clever boundaries decide where to cut; late pooling decides what a chunk knows. Both are aimed at the same hole — a chunk that leans on words outside itself — but one tries to move the hole out of the way while the other makes it stop mattering. And because they patch the same hole from two sides, their gains do not add up cleanly: buy both and you will not get the sum of the two improvements you measured separately, which is exactly the kind of arithmetic that makes a roadmap slip.

So take the ordering as an engineering rule, not a slogan. If your backlog holds add a semantic chunker and move the pooling, do the second first: it is a smaller change, it costs no extra model at index time and none at all at query time, and by this table it is worth more. Then measure again before you buy the chunker — you may find the remaining gap does not justify a second embedding pass over the corpus. Semantic chunking stays a real tool; it simply stopped being the first purchase.

Read that table by column, not by row

Overlapping chunks — let each one borrow a little of its neighbour's text — is the oldest folk remedy for the torn page, and the appendix of the paper measures it properly. The temptation is to read that table across, dataset by dataset, and announce which configuration won. Read it down instead, one column against its twin, because the question is not which row is biggest: it is whether overlap changes anything, separately for each of the two orders.

Do that and the two columns answer differently. For the torn-page order, adding overlap helps on most of the datasets tested — small, but consistently in one direction. That is what a real crutch looks like. For the read-first order it helps on a minority and hurts on the rest, which is the signature of noise: the context already arrived through attention, so there is nothing left to borrow from next door. The paper's own wording is milder than the folklore — no clear advantage — and it says that of both methods, which is a fair reading of a small table and a better argument for the mechanism than an overstatement would be.

Two habits to keep from this. First, when a claim is about whether a knob does anything, the comparison is a column, and any reading that ranks rows is answering a different question. Second, look at the tables even when the prose seems clear: the same paper states in its method section that no chunks overlap in any experiment, while one of its headline rows matches the with overlap column of this appendix digit for digit. Published text can contradict itself; published numbers usually cannot, because they are all still sitting there to be compared.

What "supported" turns out to mean

The folklore says this is built into everything by now. Check the documentation rather than the blog post and the picture is thinner. One embedding vendor exposes it as a first-class flag on its API. One chunking library ships it as a class, for locally-loaded models only. Several vector databases have a recipe in their blog, which is a different thing from native support, and worth saying plainly because the two are constantly confused. And any service that returns a single finished vector per input cannot do it at all — there is nothing left to pool.

Then the trap, which costs a working day the first time you meet it. Turn the flag on and the service's token budget stops applying per string and starts applying to the whole array you sent. It cannot be otherwise: to pool late the service has to concatenate your inputs into one text and run one pass over it. Two consequences follow immediately, and neither is in the migration guide. A batch that fit yesterday now trips the limit. And the order of the strings in your array becomes semantically meaningful, because neighbouring entries now attend to one another.

Which yields a rule you should write on the wall: never put chunks from different documents in one call. Attention is obedient, not wise — hand it two unrelated documents in one sequence and it will dutifully bind them, and you will get chunk vectors quietly flavoured by a document they have nothing to do with. The general lesson is the one this act keeps repeating: when a method changes what the model reads at once, every batching convenience you built on the old assumption of independence has to be re-examined.

Two 'lates', mirror-image bills

Two of the tricks in this book wear the very same word — late — and I have watched clever people trip over it. Both earn the name honestly: each waits for the full contextual reading before it does any arithmetic. But the bills they hand you are mirror images.

ColBERT's late-interaction — we met it back in L12 — keeps every token's own vector, a fat fistful of per-word sticky-notes for each document, its index swollen to hold them all. The comparison itself, that many-armed MaxSim handshake between your query's words and the document's, waits until query time. So you store a great deal, and you pay all over again at every single question, right there at the door.

Late-chunking keeps the opposite books. It too waits for full-document attention — but then it pools back down to one tidy index-card per chunk. The whole cost is spent once, in the back room, at indexing; query time is a single cosine, free as any plain embedding. Same word, opposite bill: late-interaction pays at the door, late-chunking pays once at the filing cabinet and hands you a clean card.

And let me kill a folk belief while I have the ledger open, because I used to repeat it myself: 'a ColBERT index is enormous.' It was. ColBERT v1 stored a full float vector per token and its MS MARCO index ran to a hundred and fifty-four gigabytes. Then ColBERTv2 replaced each vector with a nearest-centroid id plus a quantised remainder, and the same index came down to twenty-five gigabytes at its default setting — which is, to the digit, what an ordinary one-vector-per-passage index costs on the same corpus. Parity. So the real difference between the two lates was never the shelf space. It is the counter: MaxSim is charged to every single query, forever, while pooling is charged once, at filing time.

What defers for free

Those mirror-image bills have one cause, and it is not the size of the index. It is linearity. Averaging passes straight through the inner product: \( \big\langle q,\ \tfrac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j\in c_i}\vartheta_j \big\rangle = \tfrac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j\in c_i}\langle q,\vartheta_j\rangle \). The right-hand side needs the query in hand; the left-hand side does not. So I collapse the sum before a single question has been asked, file one card per chunk, and the reader at the desk pays one cosine.

Now hold MaxSim up to the same light: \(\max_{j\in d}\langle q_t,\vartheta_j\rangle\). Which of the document's tokens wins is decided by the query token itself, \(j^{*}=j^{*}(q_t)\) — and there is nothing to file in its place, because no summary of the pile can tell me in advance which sheet the next visitor will pull out of it. Every token vector has to survive until the question arrives, and every question pays for the whole pile. Same shelf, opposite counter.

That is the rule worth carrying out of this act: a deferral is free exactly when the deferred operator commutes with scoring. Note the commutation here is a different one from before — that one was about the encoder and the cut, and it said that cutting first erases information; this one is about the operator and the score, and it says nothing about quality, only about which bill you are handed. And notice where the fork is not: both lates start from the very same contextual token vectors of one encoder pass. The encoder is shared; only the postponed operator differs. Ask it of the rest of the pipeline too — which other query-time operator commutes with scoring, and what would postponing it buy?

When an architecture turns into a setting

All act long I have been presenting the two deferrals as a fork you have to choose at build time: either one card per chunk, cheap to file and cheap to consult, or one card per token, expensive on both counts and unbeatable at matching. That framing is honest about the bills. It is less honest about how long a fork stays a fork. Recent embedding models emit both outputs from a single encoder pass — a single vector for the whole span, and a per-token multi-vector, from the same reading, with a window long enough for the documents this chapter cares about.

If that holds up, the choice stops being an architectural commitment and becomes a query-time parameter. Sweep the corpus with the cheap single-vector mode, then spend the expensive per-token comparison only on the survivors — which is exactly the cascade the reranking chapter built out of two separate models, now folded inside one. Note what does not change: the bills are still mirror images, the storage for the multi-vector mode is still the larger one, and nothing here repeals the cost argument. What changes is when you have to decide.

Treat the specifics as perishable and the habit as durable. Whether any given release also supports late chunking on top of that is worth verifying on the day you read this, because vendor documentation on exactly this point has contradicted itself before — check the model card, not the announcement. The habit: when two techniques are separated by architecture, ask how long that separation is load-bearing. Often the next release simply emits both, the argument you were having turns into a configuration line, and the people who understood the mechanism can use the setting while the people who memorised the fork are still defending it.

The scribe you never had to hire

There is another way to put the name back, and it comes from Anthropic: Contextual Retrieval. Before a torn page goes into the index, you hire a small, clever scribe — an LLM — to write one preface line at the top: "This passage is from ACME's quarterly report, about revenue." Then you embed page-plus-preface. It works honestly — the referent is restored and the relevant chunk climbs to a reported cosine near \(0.859\), right where you want it.

But look at the bill. That scribe writes a fresh preface for every chunk and charges an LLM call each time — once for every passage in the library, and again for every new document you ever add. It is a real fix with a meter that never stops ticking.

Late chunking buys the same shelf for nothing. At my reading desk the very same ACME chunk lands at a reported \(0.852\) — a hair from the scribe's \(0.859\), the same answer for any practical purpose — while naïve chunking still buries it at \(0.634\), beneath a generic header sitting at \(0.851\). No preface, no coin: the long-context encoder had already written ACME into the word it while it read the whole book. Late chunking just harvests what attention already knew — the binding was free all along.

Three repairs, sorted by when they happen

Put the competing fixes on one axis and the whole argument becomes readable, because the axis is not how clever but when. Before the embedding: hire a scribe to rewrite the text, prepending a sentence of context to each passage — the referent is restored in the words themselves. During: read the whole document once and pool per segment — the referent is restored in the vector, and only in the vector. After: defer the comparison to query time and match token against token — nothing is restored in advance at all, because nothing was thrown away.

The column that decides most real designs is not the cost column, it is what physically changes. The scribe changes the text, and the text is not only an index entry: it is what the generator will later be handed. Grow every passage by a paragraph of preamble and, on a model with a hard input limit, the tail of the passage can be silently cut off at generation time — the failure lands far from its cause, in the answer, on a Friday. The read-first order changes only the vector; the generator still receives the original passage, byte for byte. The deferred comparison changes nothing until a question arrives.

Two honest notes to carry out of the comparison. First, the scribe is a real fix, not a straw man — it works, it is measurable, and its meter runs once per passage in your library and again for every document you add forever. Second, the same source that sells the scribe also gives the advice a vector-database vendor never will: if your entire corpus is small enough to fit in a prompt, do not build an index at all. Knowing when the whole chapter is unnecessary is part of knowing the chapter.

Same drawer, a better card

For weeks I braced for the bill. Surely a new way of writing the index cards means a new drawer to hold them, a new desk to read them from, a heavier reach at query time. So I re-embedded a whole corpus the late way, walked to the front of the library — and nothing downstream had moved. The card was the same size. It slid into the very same slot.

That is the quiet gift of late chunking: it changes nothing past the binding room. Same dimensionality — the vector is exactly as wide as before, so it drops into your HNSW or IVF index untouched, no re-tuning, no reshaped schema. Same query-time cost — the reader at the desk pulls one tidy card per chunk and is gone, free, just as fast as with naive chunks. I didn't hand them a fistful of per-word sticky-notes to sort through; I handed them one card that simply remembers who it's about.

So everything I fixed, I fixed upstream — offline, once, at index time. Re-embed the corpus the late way and the vector DB, the ANN index, the reranker, the whole query path never learn that anything changed. That is why this ships on a Tuesday and not next quarter: it's a pure indexing-side upgrade, not a rebuild.

The gain is a product, and the law owns only one factor

Everything measured in this chapter folds into one shape, and it is worth writing down before the boundaries arrive: \(\Delta \sim \min\!\left(1,\ g/s\right)\times\rho\). The first factor is the gap law you swept at the start — with \(s\) the length of a chunk and \(g\) the distance from a mention back to the thing it names, \(\min(1, g/s)\) is the share of mentions the blade severs from their referent. It grows as the chunks get smaller and as referents sit further upstream, and it is the only factor the law knows how to count.

The second factor \(\rho\) is the one the law is silent about: whether the context you restored has anything to do with what the reader asked. You have already met it in symbols — it is the share of the shift that points toward the query, the first term of the decomposition. And it is genuinely independent of the first. A chunk can be maximally orphaned, its referent a hundred pages upstream, and the repair still be worth nothing, because what came home is not what the question was about. The law measures the loss; only \(\rho\) measures the use.

Read as a product, every row you have seen explains itself. The lift lands where both factors are large — small chunks, distant referents, questions about exactly that referent. It fades to nothing where either one is small, and it fades for two entirely different reasons that no single number can tell apart. That is why the fence around this trick has two rails, and why a checklist with only one of them — are my chunks small? — will sell you an index rebuild you did not need.

Two ways to lose, and only one of them is a zero

Zero out the first factor and nothing dramatic happens. Make the chunks large enough that few mentions are cut away from their referents and \(\min(1, g/s)\) falls toward nothing; the gain decays with it, along the curve you watched while sweeping the chunk size. Read that as a clean verdict rather than a disappointment: there was no wound, so there was nothing to heal. The pages you filed were already whole.

Zero out the second factor and something worse than nothing happens, which is the part the law never warned you about. Let the neighbourhood be irrelevant — filler, or a hidden needle in a haystack of it — and \(\rho\) goes to nothing while the mixing does not. Attention still spreads the surroundings into every token; the denominator still grew; the card is still averaged over a chunk that has absorbed pages of noise. The turn toward the query dies, the dilution stays, and a vector pushed somewhere the reader is not looking scores below the torn page it replaced. That is why the needle case is not a shrug — it is a loss.

So keep the two diagnoses apart, because they call for opposite actions. No gain means your chunks were whole already: stop paying the index-time bill, you were buying a repair for an intact page. A loss means the context you mixed in was noise: here the old, discredited move — tear the page out and read it alone — is the correct one. The method is not good or bad. It is a bet on the neighbourhood being about the same thing as the page, and there are documents where that bet is simply wrong.

The haystack you built yourself

The needle case reads like a laboratory curiosity — who indexes a book of filler with one secret line buried in it? — and then you meet it in production, wearing ordinary clothes. A support export in which unrelated question-and-answer pairs about different products sit concatenated in one file. A scraped page carrying an article and, beneath it, the navigation, the comments and a row of unrelated teasers. A meeting transcript where several topics take turns. Nobody built any of these to be adversarial. Each of them is a haystack with several needles in it.

Run the read-first order over such a file and attention does exactly what it is supposed to do, which is the problem. It is obedient, not wise: told that these tokens form one document, it binds them, and a chunk about one product comes out flavoured by the two that happen to sit beside it. Every card from that file drifts toward the file's own average — the average of something that is about nothing in particular — so a query about one product must now pick its answer out of a set of cards that all smell faintly of the whole export.

Which sets the correct repair, and it is not to switch the pooling back off. It is to make the document boundary mean something again: split the export into one document per entry before anything is embedded, strip the boilerplate off the scraped page, cut the transcript where the topic changes. This chapter's bet is that a chunk's neighbours are about the same thing as the chunk. That bet is not a property of the method — it is a property of your corpus, and unlike the method, the corpus is something you control.

Half an index is worse than either half

Drop-in is true about the shape of the card and false about the migration. The vector is the same width and slides into the same slot, so nothing in the schema objects when you re-embed part of a corpus the new way and leave the rest — and that silence is the failure. The old cards and the new ones are not two versions of one measurement. They are the outputs of two different functions, and your index is now scoring every query against a mixture of them.

The mixture is not neutral, and you have already been told why. Pooling over a document's contextual tokens turns a card toward whatever the document is about, and changes its length as well as its direction. Compare a query against a corpus where some cards carry that shift and some do not, and the ranking is partly deciding which pipeline produced the document rather than which document answers the question. There is no reason for that effect to be small, and none for it to be constant across your collection. Nothing throws. The list is simply a little wrong, everywhere, in a way no unit test is watching for.

So the migration rule is blunt: re-embed the whole corpus in one pass, or stand the new index up beside the old one and cut over in a single move. Two habits fall out of it. Stamp every vector with the pipeline that produced it, so that a mixture is detectable rather than merely possible. And remember what the beat on conditional chunks already established — the unit you invalidate stopped being the chunk and became the document, so an edit anywhere in a file makes every card of that file stale. A pure indexing-side change is still a change to every entry in the index.

When you should have torn the page out

Every method has a room it should never enter, and mine is the needle in a haystack. Picture a book of eight thousand pages of pure nonsense-scribbles with a single secret passphrase hidden on one of them — the Passkey and Needle tests. The answer is that one line; everything around it is filler that has nothing to do with it.

This is exactly where late chunking turns against itself. Its whole gift is that full-document attention smears the surrounding context into every token before I pool — but when the surroundings are gibberish, mixing them in only muddies the needle. Here I'd have been better off doing the very thing I warned you against: tear the one page out and read it alone. Late chunking pays off when the neighbours are the referent — 'Berlin', the missing subject — not when they're noise.

And two plain costs, so you carry no illusions. It needs a long-context, mean-pooling model that hands you token-level vectors — an ordinary 512-token encoder can't read the whole book at once. And reading it whole is not free: full-document attention is roughly \(O(n^2)\), so you pay more at index time. Cheap at query time, yes — but you moved the bill, you didn't cancel it.

There is a cleaner version of the same lesson, and it needs no experiment at all. On Quora the documents average about sixty characters — shorter than a single chunk. So naive chunking, late chunking and not chunking at all produce the same number, 87.19, digit for digit. Nothing was severed, so there is nothing to restore; the gain is zero by construction, not by misfortune. And at the other end of the ruler stands the method's ceiling: the longer the text you push through an encoder in one pass, the more its embeddings collapse toward one another — self-attention is a low-pass filter, and detail is the high frequency it removes. You already watched that happen by hand: when our little chunk vector turned toward the query, it also got shorter. Late chunking buys distance and pays in sharpness, and on a long enough document the two cancel.

What the seam costs, counted

You have moved the bill, not cancelled it, so let us actually read the bill. When the book is longer than the window, long late chunking reads it in overlapping macro-chapters: pieces of at most \(l_{\max}\) tokens, each starting an overlap \(\omega\) before the previous one ended. The first thing to notice is what the piece count divides by. It is not the window but the stride, \(l_{\max}-\omega\): the number of pieces is \(m=\left\lceil (L-\omega)/(l_{\max}-\omega)\right\rceil\). Widen the overlap for safety and the stride shortens under you, so the passes multiply faster than the width you added.

Now the tokens actually pushed through the encoder: \(T = m\,l_{\max} = L + (m-1)\,\omega\) — exactly, when the last piece comes out full. Read the right-hand form aloud, because it is the promise made honest. You pay the document once, plus one overlap per seam, and there is always one seam fewer than there are pieces. The surcharge is set by how many joins you made, not by how many times longer than the window your book is. That is why a doubled window is cheap and a doubled overlap is not.

And the reason you tolerate seams at all sits in the same line. Attention is quadratic in the length of its input: read \(L\) tokens in one go and you pay about \(L^{2}\), read them as \(m\) pieces of a fixed \(l_{\max}\) and you pay about \(m\,l_{\max}^{2}\), which grows only linearly in \(L\). The window is not merely a limitation the model imposes on you. It is also the thing that keeps reading the whole book from costing more than the shelf it earns you.

The ceiling: long readings blur what they read

Every method should be able to say where its own gain stops growing, and this one can. There is a documented effect called length-induced collapse: as the input to an encoder gets longer, the output embeddings crowd into a narrower and narrower subspace, until different long texts resemble each other more than each resembles its own short passages. The mechanism is not mysterious. Self-attention averages — each layer replaces a token's vector with a weighted mean over the sequence — and a mean taken over more and more terms behaves like a low-pass filter: the slow, shared, document-level component survives, and the sharp local detail is what decays.

Read that against what we just built and the tension is exact. The read-first order deliberately pushes more text through the encoder in one pass — that is the entire trick — which is precisely the regime in which the collapse bites hardest. So long-range context and local sharpness are bought with the same coin. You already watched a miniature of this by hand: pooling turned the chunk vector toward the query and, in the same motion, made it shorter. Turning toward the document is the gain; getting shorter is the blur.

Hence the shape of the method's return curve, which is the honest thing to tell a stakeholder. On short documents there is little cross-boundary dependency to repair, so the gain is small. On documents long enough to carry referents across pages, the gain is real. On documents long enough for the collapse to dominate, the two effects pull against each other and the gain fades again. And notice where that leaves us: the reason to chunk small in the first place — a small card is a sharp card — was never repealed by this chapter. It was only made cheaper to satisfy.

Three ways past the window, and their bills

A document longer than the encoder's window leaves you exactly three moves, and it is worth laying them side by side with what each one loses and what each one costs. Truncate at the window: costs nothing, loses the entire tail — those pages never enter the index at all, so no query can ever reach them. Cut the document into window-sized pieces and read each separately: costs nothing, loses every dependency that crosses one of those cuts — which is the torn page again, merely at a coarser grain. Overlap the pieces and discard the duplicated margins: costs a margin's worth of tokens at every seam, and loses only the links that reach further than the margin.

Read the first row against something you already know and its price stops being abstract. Chunking a long document at all, rather than embedding it whole, is worth a large relative gain — that is the measurement act one opened with. Truncation throws away exactly that value from the other end: not by pooling badly, but by refusing to index the text. It is the cheapest row on the page and almost always the most expensive mistake on it.

The third row is the one people call late chunking for long documents, and it deserves its honest name: a sliding window with a per-seam price. The overhead is one margin per seam, which is linear in document length, not a second pass over the document — a distinction worth defending in a design review. But no setting of the margin makes context travel further than one window, so on a genuinely long document a name introduced in the opening chapter will not reach the closing one. That is not a defect to be tuned away. It is what a finite window means, and saying it out loud is the difference between understanding the algorithm and repeating its brochure.

The bill you pay in memory, not in time

The cost of reading the whole book is usually quoted as attention growing with the square of the input length, and that is the compute half of the bill. There is a second half, and it is the one that decides whether your indexing job runs at all. To pool late you must keep the contextual token vectors until the ruler comes down: every token of the window, each a full-width vector, all resident at once. An ordinary document embedding collapses them the instant they exist. This method cannot — collapsing them early is precisely the thing it refuses to do.

Read the two costs as different resources with different cures. The compute grows with the square of the window and is paid in seconds; you buy it down by shortening the window, which is exactly what reading the book in overlapping macro-chapters does. The storage grows with the length of the window multiplied by the width of a vector, and it is paid against a hard ceiling; the same shortening helps, but so does processing fewer documents at once — the index-time batch size is a knob the torn-page order never made you touch. And notice the symptom, because it is not the one you would expect. The first sign of getting this wrong is not a slow job. It is a job that dies on the longest document in the corpus, which is also the document this method was most likely to help.

One footnote, because you will meet it in the source. The published description calls this memory growth exponential. It is not: it grows the way attention does, with the square of the window, and the difference is not pedantry when you are sizing a machine — double the window under a square and the bill is four times as large, while doubling it under a true exponential produces a number you would not quote out loud. Quote the paper with the correction. It is a small slip in an otherwise careful text, and the habit it teaches — check a growth rate against the mechanism that produces it — will save you far more than this one sentence.

The column that disappeared

This chapter has compared the read-first order against the tear-first order all the way through, and that is one comparison short. There is a third pipeline, and it is the one nobody bothers to run: do not chunk at all. Embed each document whole, one card per file, and let retrieval work at document granularity. The paper's first version carries that column — and on a minority of the collections it tested, plain no-chunking wins. Not against naive chunking. Against late chunking.

Sit with that instead of explaining it away, because it is exactly what the ceiling predicts. Chunking small was worth a great deal, because a small card is a sharp card. Reading the whole document before pooling was worth a little more, because it puts the missing context back. But if a document is short enough, or coherent enough, that one card over the whole of it is already sharp, then the boundaries were never buying anything, and pooling late is repairing a wound nobody inflicted. The three pipelines converge from one end — a document shorter than a chunk makes all three the identical arithmetic — and from the other end they can cross.

The research habit here is worth more than the finding. That column is present in the paper's first version and in the repository's own README, and absent from the versions people actually cite. Nothing dishonest need have happened: columns get cut for space, for a reviewer, for a cleaner story. But the pattern is reliable enough to make a rule of — when you want to know what a result does not claim, read the earliest version, and read the code repository beside it. The most informative table is often the one that stopped being printed.

The trick works inside the window, not over it

The same arithmetic reads the other way, and this is the honest half. Two tokens sitting in different macro-chapters have no attention column on each other — none, unless one of them fell inside the overlap. So a referent further than \(\omega\) past a seam is exactly as orphaned as it was under the old cut-first order. Late chunking does not abolish the seams; it moves them, from between every pair of chunks to between every pair of windows, and it makes them far rarer. Rarer is not never.

That gives the repair its true shape: total inside a reading, nil across one. Every token in a window sees every other token in that window, which is why the Berlin page came home; no token sees anything in the next window, which is why a book whose subject is named in its opening chapter and needed in its last will still hand you an orphan. The overlap is a crutch, and it is squeezed from both ends — widen it and the previous beat's stride shortens, so you pay in passes; narrow it and long-range coreference stays broken. Choose the width by measuring how far your own documents actually carry their referents, not by taste and not by the default in a config file.

And take the general reflex with you, because it outlives this method. Whenever something promises you the whole document, go and find the window it really operates in — a context length, a retrieval depth, a batch, a time range. Chunk Norris at least cut where you could see him. A window cuts silently, in a place nobody chose, and the only way to know where it fell is to go and look.

Three questions, then rebuild the index

Before you re-index a corpus, interrogate the model, in this order — the order is chosen so the cheapest question rules out the most candidates. One: does it hand you token-level vectors, or only a finished embedding? If only the finished one, the method cannot run at all; there is nothing left to pool, and no amount of prompt engineering changes that. Two: is the window long enough for your documents, and is the attention bidirectional? Under a causal mask the first half of a document never sees the second, so the very dependency you came to repair is unreachable by construction. Three: is the pooling head a token mean? If it is something else, your vector stops matching what the model was trained to produce.

Be precise about where each requirement comes from, because they are not equally well established. The first three properties — long context, mean pooling, token-level outputs — are stated in the paper itself. That certain other pooling heads are outright incompatible is asserted in the vendor's blog and nowhere in the literature. And whether a model that pools on its last token behaves well here is, as far as anyone has published, simply unknown: nobody has measured it. Say unknown out loud rather than guessing; a stated hole is a research question, a guessed answer is folklore in the making.

What makes the checklist worth two minutes is the size of the alternative. The independent replication found a model whose retrieval quality did not merely fail to improve under the new order — it fell to a small fraction of what it had been, on a corpus where the method's gain is otherwise largest. The plausible culprit is an incompatible pooling head, and calling that a hypothesis rather than a diagnosis is the correct level of confidence: nobody has published the mechanism. Either way the lesson holds — three questions cost minutes, a wrong re-index costs a rebuild of your whole corpus.

The whole book, in one glance

Step back from the reading desk with me. The whole idea fits on one card. Naïve chunking tears the pages out first — each chunk read alone, so "Its 3.85 million residents" forgets it was ever about Berlin, and retrieval walks right past the answer. Late chunking reads the whole book first, then lays the ruler across the spine. Same model, same embedding — you change only when you pool: run full-document attention once, let it write "Berlin"-ness into the "Its" and "the city" tokens, and only then mean-pool each chunk. The coreference resolves itself, no LLM in the loop.

That is the one lever worth remembering: pool late. The payoff is honest — a point or two of nDCG on average, biggest where the chunks lean hardest on context — but it is free at query time and drop-in: same dimensionality, same HNSW index, same cost, and it matches an LLM that pays to prepend a context paragraph to every chunk. Hold on to the two lates, too. ColBERT's late interaction (L12) keeps every token as its own vector and pays at query time — a fistful of sticky-notes for token-level precision. Late chunking pools back to one tidy card per chunk and pays once, at indexing. Different bills, same instinct: don't decide too early.

And know where it breaks: bury a lone needle in 8,000 pages of filler and reading the whole book only smears it — sometimes the torn page really is better. But for ordinary documents whose chunks lean on what came before, this is the cheapest upgrade you will find. Take the shape home and drop it straight into the RAG index we built back in L15 — change when you pool, not the model. And carry it into L19, where the same pipeline meets a language that fights back one level lower — inside the word itself. Thank you for reading the whole book with me.
