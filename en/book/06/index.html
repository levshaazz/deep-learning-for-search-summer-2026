The Map of Meaning

I'm Serega, and I want to take you back to First Contact — the alien on the screen, the translator spitting out symbols, the moment we couldn't tell whether two of its phrases meant the same thing. We solved half of it: we turned the alien's text into countable units and we built cosine, a way to measure when two vectors point the same way. But there was a crack we papered over, and it has been widening ever since.

Here's the crack. The whole classical machine — the inverted index, BM25, the bag-of-words vectors — treats a word as a symbol: a unique ID, a card in a catalog, a slot in a vector. And symbols have no sense of nearness in meaning. To that machine "couch" and "sofa" are as unrelated as "couch" and "crocodile" — different IDs, full stop. The alien could hand us a perfect synonym for a word in our index and we'd score it exactly zero.

So today we do the thing the alien needed all along: we give every word coordinates. Not an arbitrary ID, but a position in a space where the geometry means something — where "couch" and "sofa" land as neighbours, where the direction from "king" to "queen" is the same arrow as from "man" to "woman", and where near finally means related. We're going to draw the Map of Meaning. Then, because that map lives in three hundred dimensions and you have two eyes, we'll learn to fold it small enough to see.

Symbols have no “near”

Let's make the failure concrete, because it's worse than "a bit blind" — it's total. The classical way to hand a word to math is one-hot encoding: pick a vocabulary of, say, 50,000 words, and represent word number 7 as a vector of 50,000 zeros with a single 1 in slot 7. "cat" might be slot 312, "dog" slot 6,201, "airplane" slot 18,540. Three vectors, each a lone spike on its own axis.

Now measure how near any two of them are. The dot product of two different one-hot vectors is always zero — their 1s never line up — so the cosine between any two distinct words is exactly $$\cos(\text{cat},\text{dog}) = 0 = \cos(\text{cat},\text{airplane}).$$ Read that again. "cat" and "dog" are exactly as similar as "cat" and "airplane": not similar at all, identically not-similar. Every pair of distinct words sits the same fixed distance apart, the corners of a giant simplex, equidistant and meaningless. This is the Lexical Gremlin's home turf — the brick wall he wedges between "couch" and "sofa" is built into the representation itself.

And it's not just one-hot. BM25 and the bag-of-words document vectors inherit the same disease, because they're sums of one-hot word axes: a document about felines and a document about cats share no terms, so they share no coordinates, so they're orthogonal — zero overlap — even though they're about the same thing. Stemming and synonym lists are buckets bailing the boat, hand-patched, brittle, never enough. The real fix has to change the representation: stop giving each word a private axis, and start giving it a position that other words can be near.

The company a word keeps

So where do the coordinates come from? Not from a dictionary, not from hand-labelling — from a single, deep observation made by the linguist J. R. Firth in 1957, and it's worth quoting straight: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." This is the distributional hypothesis, and once you see it you can't unsee it: a word's meaning is the distribution of contexts it appears in.

Run the thought experiment Firth was pointing at. Suppose you've never met the word "wug", but you keep reading sentences like "the wug purred and curled up on the warm windowsill", "she fed the wug and scratched behind its ears", "the wug knocked a cup off the table, naturally". You don't need a definition. The company — purred, fed, windowsill, knocked the cup off the table — has already told you a wug is a cat, or close enough to live next to one. Meaning leaked out of the contexts and into the word.

Here's the leap that makes it computable. If two words appear in the same contexts — "couch" and "sofa" both sit near "cushion", "living room", "sit down", "comfortable" — then their context distributions are nearly identical, and we'll define them to be near in our space. Flip it around: we'll search for a set of word coordinates such that words sharing contexts end up close together. The contexts are the gravity; the coordinates are what settles under it. Every embedding method in this chapter is some clever way of doing exactly that — turning "the company a word keeps" into a vector.

The company — but which company?

Firth's line is a hypothesis, not yet an algorithm, and the gap between them is one decision: what counts as company? Answer it one way and you get one map; answer it another way and you get a different map from the same corpus with the same code. The main dial is the window — how many words on each side count as context. Make it narrow, a word or two, and the neighbours you collect are mostly the ones that could stand in the same grammatical slot; the space that settles under that pressure puts substitutable words together — "cat" beside "dog" and "hamster", verbs beside verbs, one tense beside another. Widen it to ten or twenty and the neighbours are simply whatever shares a subject; now "cat" sits beside "vet", "litter" and "purr". Same hypothesis, same algorithm, two genuinely different notions of near.

Nor does context have to be a window at all. Replace "the words within \(k\) positions" with "the words attached to this one in the sentence's parse" — dependency contexts (Levy & Goldberg, 2014) — and the pressure sharpens further toward substitutability, because a word's syntactic dependents follow it around wherever it moves in the sentence. This is the technical content of a distinction the chapter will meet again as a benchmark question: similarity (could one word replace the other?) versus relatedness (do they belong to the same story?). It is also, read honestly, the mechanism behind the ugliest failure of the whole approach — "hot" and "cold" are maximally substitutable, so any window-based context will call them near, and no amount of extra text will fix that, because the text is telling the truth about the contexts.

For search this is a design choice, not trivia. A retriever wants relatedness: a query and the passage that answers it are almost never substitutable for one another, they merely belong to the same story, so wide contexts serve it. A spelling corrector, a query-expansion component or an autocomplete wants substitutability, so it should be trained narrow. Pick the window to match the job, and state which one you picked when you report that your embeddings are "good". And notice the ceiling that no setting of this dial can raise: whichever context you choose, every occurrence of the word is still poured into one vector — a single average of all the companies it ever kept.

word2vec: learning the coordinates

In 2013 Mikolov and colleagues at Google turned the distributional hypothesis into a fast, trainable recipe — word2vec — and it's the one worth working through in full. The variant we'll follow is skip-gram: a tiny prediction game whose by-product is the map. Slide a window over the text; for each centre word, try to predict the words around it. Centre "cat" in "the lazy cat sat on the", and the model must raise the probability of its neighbours — "lazy", "sat", "on" — within a window of, say, two words each side.

Mechanically, every word gets two vectors: one as a centre word (\(v_w\)) and one as a context word (\(u_w\)), both the coordinates we're trying to learn. The model scores how well a context word \(o\) fits a centre word \(c\) by their dot product \(u_o^\top v_c\), and turns the scores over the whole vocabulary into probabilities with a softmax: $$P(o \mid c) = \frac{\exp(u_o^\top v_c)}{\sum_{w \in V} \exp(u_w^\top v_c)}.$$ Train by maximizing the probability of the true neighbours across the whole corpus. The pressure is exactly the distributional hypothesis made numerical: to predict "cat"'s neighbours well, "cat"'s vector must come to resemble the vectors of words that share its company — so "cat" and "dog", which keep nearly the same company, drift together.

But look at that denominator. The softmax sums over every word in the vocabulary — 50,000, a million — for every training step. That's hopeless at scale; you'd spend all your compute normalizing. word2vec's decisive trick is negative sampling: don't ask "which of all million words comes next?", ask a far cheaper yes/no question. For each real (centre, context) pair, draw a handful — typically \(k = 5\) to \(20\) — of random words as fake "negative" contexts, and train a logistic classifier to say yes to the real pair and no to the fakes: $$\log \sigma(u_o^\top v_c) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n}\big[\log \sigma(-u_{w_i}^\top v_c)\big].$$ Here \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid. Each step now touches \(k+1\) words instead of a million — and that single change is what turned a beautiful idea into something you can train on billions of words over a weekend.

Two details that matter in practice and are pure distributional-hypothesis wisdom. The negatives are drawn not uniformly but from the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power — \(P_n(w) \propto \text{count}(w)^{3/4}\). That fractional exponent is the whole trick: raising counts to the 3/4 power flattens the distribution, sampling frequent words a bit less than their raw frequency and rare words a bit more, so common junk like "the" doesn't dominate the negatives while genuinely rare words still get a turn. And frequent words get subsampled — each token is discarded with probability \(1 - \sqrt{t / f(w)}\) (Mikolov's tweak, with \(t \approx 10^{-5}\)), where \(f(w)\) is the word's relative frequency (its share of all tokens), so \(\sqrt{t/f(w)}\) is well-defined only for words more frequent than \(t\) — rarer words are kept with probability 1. So "the" and "of" are thrown out most of the time while a rare word is almost always kept — because the ten-thousandth time "the" sits next to "cat" it teaches nothing new. The result, after a pass over enough text: a few hundred numbers per word, and a space where the company each word keeps has hardened into a position.

Why every word gets two vectors

Skip-gram hands every word a centre vector \(v_w\) and a context vector \(u_w\), which looks like doubling the bookkeeping for nothing. Try the thrifty version and watch it break. Tie them, \(u_w=v_w\), and the score of a word against itself becomes \(v_w^{\top}v_w=\lVert v_w\rVert^{2}\) — necessarily non-negative, and typically the largest score that word can produce against anything. The model is now structurally biased toward predicting every word as its own neighbour, which is close to the opposite of what corpora say: a word almost never sits next to a copy of itself. With two matrices, \(u_w^{\top}v_w\) is an ordinary number the model is free to make small, and the bias disappears.

The deeper reason is about what is being factorised. Both roads in this chapter end up fitting a table of who-keeps-company-with-whom by a product of two matrices, \(M\approx UV^{\top}\). Forcing \(U=V\) restricts you to the symmetric, positive semi-definite matrices — and the co-occurrence statistic the objective is actually chasing is neither. "of" follows "kind" far more reliably than "kind" follows "of"; the conditional distribution is not symmetric, and a single matrix cannot express a relation that reads differently in the two directions. Two factors are not redundancy — they are the minimum needed to represent an asymmetric relation between the same set of objects.

What do you actually ship? The convention is to keep the centre vectors \(v\) and discard \(u\), though summing them, \(v+u\), often works slightly better and is what GloVe recommends for its own pair: the two factors carry nearly the same information, so adding them averages away part of the noise each picked up separately. Keep the shape in mind, because it comes straight back in the next chapter under different names. One token, two learned projections of it, and a score built from the pair — because what a thing offers and what a thing seeks are two different questions about the same object, and one vector cannot answer both.

The negatives decide what the vectors mean

Negative sampling arrived a moment ago as a speed trick — the softmax was too expensive, so we swapped it for a cheap yes/no question. That framing undersells it badly, and the correction matters. Negative sampling does not approximate the softmax; it optimises a different objective, with a different optimum. Solve the logistic problem exactly — for each pair, is this a real (centre, context) pair or one drawn from the noise distribution \(P_n\)? — and the score that makes the classifier right is a log-odds: \(u_c^{\top}v_w=\log\dfrac{p(c\mid w)}{P_n(c)}-\log k\). Not the log-probability the softmax would have chased, but a ratio of the observed distribution to the one you invented.

Read that formula slowly, because your choice of \(P_n\) is sitting inside the answer. Sample negatives uniformly and the denominator is a constant, so the score reduces to \(\log p(c\mid w)\) plus a shift — and then "the" scores high beside literally every word, because it is genuinely likely beside every word, and every neighbourhood on the map fills with function words. Sample them from the corpus's own unigram frequencies and the denominator cancels exactly that: a context now scores high only if it is more likely beside this word than it is in general. That quantity has a name — pointwise mutual information — and the fractional exponent the previous beat quoted is a dial between the two extremes, flattening the noise distribution part of the way toward uniform. It is not a speed hack. It is the knob that decides whether your map measures frequency or measures association.

Which is why the theorem this chapter quotes a little further on — that this procedure is implicitly factorising a shifted mutual-information table — is a statement about negative sampling in particular, not about neural methods in general. Change the noise distribution and you change the matrix being factorised, and therefore the map. Carry the lesson forward as a general one, because it is exactly the shape of the argument in the chapter after this: what you contrast against is what defines the geometry. The positives tell the model what to pull together; the negatives silently decide what "apart" is going to mean.

The skip-gram network is fed one centre word — here “king”. At the input it is not a vector yet, just a single index: a one-hot column, a 1 in king's slot and 0 everywhere else across the eight-word vocabulary. A bare position, nothing learned about meaning yet.

Multiplying a one-hot by the embedding matrix W does nothing clever — it just SELECTS one row. King's 1 picks row 0 of W, and that row IS king's embedding: the four numbers that become the hidden vector. This is the whole secret — the embedding matrix is literally a lookup table, one learned row per word.

To predict context, the same matrix is reused the other way (tied weights). The hidden vector is dotted with every row of W in turn, giving one raw score — a logit — per vocabulary word. Big logit means the word's row points the same way as king's; the bars on the right read out those eight scores.

Softmax turns those scores into probabilities: every bar is now positive and the eight together sum to exactly 1 — a proper distribution over context words. The top prediction is highlighted, with the runner-up just behind it. (A schematic forward pass on sliced GloVe rows, not a trained model — it shows the data FLOW, not a learned distribution.)

The “network” is a table

The picture calls skip-gram a neural network, so count its non-linearities: between the input and the embedding there are none. Multiplying a one-hot vector by a matrix selects a row and nothing else — \(e_w^{\top}E=E_{w,:}\) — so the "input layer" is a lookup, and calling it a matrix product describes the software rather than the mathematics. The whole model is then a single bilinear score, \(u_o^{\top}v_c\): no hidden non-linearity anywhere, one table of rows read from either side. word2vec is a matrix factorisation wearing a network's clothes, which is exactly what the next beat's theorem will say out loud.

Two consequences follow, and the practical one is why any of this was trainable in the first place. Because the input is a row selection, the gradient of a training step is sparse: it touches the centre word's row and the rows of the handful of contexts and negatives you drew, and every other row in the table receives exactly zero. A genuine dense hidden layer would touch every parameter on every step, and a vocabulary-sized model would be hopeless. Negative sampling cut the width of each step; the lookup structure is what makes the step's cost independent of the vocabulary at all. The two tricks together are the reason a map of a billion tokens fits on one machine over a weekend.

Hold on to the shape, because this is the last time it will be this simple. In the next chapter the same table is still sitting at the very bottom — a row per token, read by index — but it stops being the answer and becomes the raw material: the row is merely the input to a stack that rewrites it using the rest of the sentence. Every embedding model in this course begins with a row lookup. What distinguishes them, entirely, is what happens to the row afterwards.

The word2vec family tree

Before we cash in the map, three threads from word2vec are worth pulling, because each one quietly reappears for the rest of the course. The first is a sibling to skip-gram. We trained the model to predict context from a centre word; flip the arrow and you get CBOW (continuous bag-of-words), which predicts the centre word from its averaged context. The trade-off is clean: CBOW sums up the surrounding words and guesses the middle, which makes it fast and smooth on frequent words; skip-gram makes a separate prediction for each context word, which is slower but squeezes more signal out of rare words and small corpora. As a rule of thumb — skip-gram for a modest corpus where every rare word counts, CBOW when you have a firehose of text and want speed. Same space, same distributional idea, two directions of the same arrow.

The second thread fixes a wall we've been ignoring. word2vec and GloVe both learn one vector per word in a fixed vocabulary — and the moment a query contains a word the training corpus never saw (a typo, a brand name, a rare morphological form like "unfriendliness"), the model has no vector at all. It's the same out-of-vocabulary cliff that BPE tokenization climbed back in First Contact. The fix here rhymes with it: fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017) represents each word as the sum of its character n-grams — "where" becomes <wh, whe, her, ere, re> plus the whole word — and learns vectors for those subword pieces. Now an unseen word still has a vector: assemble it from its n-grams. "unfriendliness" inherits meaning from "friend", "un-", and "-ness" even if that exact string never appeared in training. The vocabulary wall comes down.

The third thread is the deepest, and it reaches all the way back to L3. Remember LSA — taking the term-document matrix and running truncated SVD to get dense topic vectors? It feels like a completely different machine from word2vec's neighbour-prediction game. It isn't. Levy and Goldberg (2014) proved that skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) is implicitly factorizing a word-context matrix whose cells are the shifted pointwise mutual information: $$M_{ij} = \text{PMI}(w_i, c_j) - \log k,$$ where \(k\) is the number of negatives. In plain terms: the "predictive" neural method and the "count-based" matrix method are not rivals — they're two faces of one coin, both factoring a PMI-flavoured co-occurrence table into vectors. word2vec just does it with stochastic gradient steps instead of one big eigendecomposition.

So the family resolves into a single idea seen from three angles. CBOW and skip-gram are the same objective run forwards and backwards; fastText is that objective taught to spell, so it never meets a word it can't represent; and Levy-Goldberg show the whole predictive enterprise is classical matrix factorization in disguise — the very SVD lineage L3 used for LSA, rediscovered. One map, many roads, and they all lead to the same country. Now let's spend the map: the arithmetic the next beat promised.

Direction is meaning, length is bookkeeping

Every measurement on this map is a cosine, which means we have been quietly throwing one number per word away: its length. That deserves a defence. Split a word vector into a direction and a norm, and ask what the training pressure actually pins down. The objective scores a pair by a dot product, and a dot product rises for two different reasons — the vectors point the same way, or one of them is simply long. So the direction is what the company-it-keeps pressure genuinely shapes, and the norm is what accumulates as a side effect of how the training went for that particular word.

And you can say what the side effect tracks. A frequent word is visited by the optimiser again and again; if its contexts are consistent, those updates keep pushing the same way and the vector grows long. A rare word gets few updates and stays short. A word pulled by contradictory contexts — the polysemous ones, and the ones that sit in every kind of sentence — gets updates that partly cancel, so it also stays short despite being common. The norm is a record of how often and how consistently the corpus spoke about a word, which is a fact about the corpus, not about the meaning. Rank by raw dot product and frequent words would drift toward the top of every neighbour list simply for being long, and the map would be reporting the word counts you already had.

So the ruler is angular on purpose: Sir Cosine divides that length out and leaves only the part the distributional hypothesis earned. Two riders, both useful later. The norm is not garbage — it is a legitimate signal of how well the corpus determined a word, and it is the reason a short vector deserves less trust than a long one at the same cosine. And the same argument scales up: a document must not rank higher for being longer either, which is why every similarity in this course, from here to the last chapter, is measured as an angle and not as a length.

Meaning becomes arithmetic

Now comes the moment that made the whole field sit up — the thing nobody put in the training objective, that fell out anyway. We trained the space only to predict neighbours. We never told it about gender, or royalty, or plurals, or capital cities. And yet, when you go looking, the space has quietly organized itself by relation.

Watch what that means geometrically. Take the vectors for "man" and "woman" and subtract them: \(\text{woman} - \text{man}\) is an arrow, a direction in the space. Now take "king" and "queen" and subtract: \(\text{queen} - \text{king}\) is another arrow — and it points the same way. The relationship "…and its female counterpart" became a single, consistent direction you can travel along. The same is true for "capital of" ("paris" → "france"), for tense, for plural. Meaning didn't just become a place; the relations between meanings became vectors. Which means we can do arithmetic on them — and the next page does exactly that, with the real numbers.

Give every word a list of numbers — a vector — and it becomes a point in space. On this little map, gender runs left to right and rank runs bottom to top: “man” and “king” sit on the left, “woman” lower-right, and meaning becomes a place you can point to.

Now meaning becomes arithmetic. Start at “king”, subtract the “man” direction and add the “woman” direction — the same move that turns man into woman. Geometrically it is one corner of a parallelogram, and it carries you to a fresh point in the space.

And the nearest real word to that point is queen — cosine 0.861, far ahead of the runner-ups prince (0.764) and throne (0.764). The analogy holds with real GloVe vectors: the relation king→queen is the very same direction as man→woman.

The same ruler measures any pair. cat·dog scores 0.922 — almost the same direction — while cat·airplane is only 0.365 and king·computer just 0.228. Symbols said every pair was equally far apart; geometry finally says near means related.

Why differences of vectors line up

The parallelogram is usually presented as a happy accident that fell out of the training. It isn't an accident, and the argument is short enough to hold in your head. Both methods in this chapter push a dot product toward a log co-occurrence — that is literally what GloVe writes down, and what skip-gram does implicitly — so read the fitted model as \(u_c^{\top}v_w\approx\log p(c\mid w)+\text{const}\). Now take a difference of two word vectors and dot it against a context: \((v_{w_1}-v_{w_2})^{\top}u_c\approx\log\dfrac{p(c\mid w_1)}{p(c\mid w_2)}\). The constant cancels, and what is left is a log-ratio of context distributions. A difference of vectors is not a vague geometric gesture; it is the object that reads off how much more often a context appears beside one word than beside the other.

Now the analogy states itself. "man is to woman as king is to queen" is the claim that both pairs differ by the same ratio: whatever factor you multiply a man-word's contexts by to get a woman-word's contexts, you multiply a king's by to get a queen's. If those ratios agree for every context \(c\), then the two vector differences have the same dot product against every \(u_c\); and once the context vectors span the space, having the same dot product against all of them means being the same vector. So \(v_{\text{woman}}-v_{\text{man}}=v_{\text{queen}}-v_{\text{king}}\), which is the parallelogram, rearranged. The arrow was never decoration — it is the logarithm of a ratio, and it lines up exactly when the ratio is constant.

That is also the honest source of the failures. A relation shows up as a clean direction precisely when it acts as a constant multiplicative factor on the context distribution, which "…and its female counterpart" roughly does. Relations that don't — one-to-many ones like "cities of a country", rare words whose ratios are estimated from a handful of counts, negation, anything whose effect on contexts depends on what it is applied to — have no constant factor, so there is no constant arrow to find, and the nearest word to the computed point is a near miss or nonsense. The geometry is not being flaky. It is reporting, correctly, that the ratio you assumed does not exist.

Where the arithmetic lies

That parallelogram is intoxicating, so let me be the one to spoil the magic trick before someone else does — because an honest map shows its blank spots. First, the analogy is cherry-picked more than it looks. When you compute \(\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman}\) and ask for the nearest word, the standard evaluation code excludes the three input words from the answer. Leave them in and the top hit is very often just "king" itself — the vector barely moved, and the single closest neighbour to a small nudge from "king" is "king". The famous result quietly depends on that exclusion, a point made sharply by later analyses (Linzen, 2016). The arrow is real, but a chunk of the headline accuracy is the exclude-the-question trick doing the heavy lifting.

Second, it breaks outside the clean cases. "king : queen" works because the gender direction is crisp and the words are frequent. Ask for analogies over rare words, many-to-one relations (a country has one capital but many cities), or non-linear relations, and the parallelogram buckles — the nearest word is a near-miss or nonsense. The geometry encodes some relations as clean directions and smears the rest. Treat "meaning is arithmetic" as a vivid demo of structure, not a reliable calculator.

Third — and this is the corner that should unsettle you — the same machinery that gives us "king − man + woman ≈ queen" also gives us "doctor − man + woman ≈ nurse" and "computer_programmer − man + woman ≈ homemaker." The space learned gender as a direction, yes; it also learned which jobs the training corpus associates with which gender, and it will hand you those associations as confident geometry. Bolukbasi et al. (2016) documented exactly these analogies; Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan (2017) showed embeddings reproduce the full battery of human implicit-association biases — gendered names to careers, ethnic names to "pleasant"/"unpleasant" words. The map didn't invent the prejudice; it faithfully absorbed the prejudice latent in billions of words of human text. That's not a bug in word2vec. It's a mirror, and the reflection is us.

This matters the instant an embedding ranks résumés, autocompletes a sentence, or retrieves documents — the bias rides along into every downstream decision. There are debiasing methods: Bolukbasi's identifies the gender direction from definitional pairs (he–she, man–woman) and projects gendered components out of words that should be neutral ("nurse", "engineer"), while leaving legitimately-gendered words ("king", "mother") alone. It helps, but later work showed bias hides in subtler geometry that a single projection misses — debiasing is real, partial, and unfinished. The honest lesson for anyone who ships embeddings: the map is only ever as fair as the territory it was drawn from, and the Cartographer who forgets that draws prejudice onto the world with the authority of mathematics. There's also a quieter failure worth naming, and it bites every distributional method.

Here it is. Antonyms look similar. "hot" and "cold", "good" and "bad", "up" and "down" — opposites in meaning — sit at high cosine, often higher than many true synonyms. Why? Because they keep almost identical company: "the water was very ___", "a really ___ idea", "prices went ___." The distributional hypothesis says same contexts → near in space, and antonyms share contexts almost perfectly. So the space that nails "couch ≈ sofa" also places "hot" snug beside "cold" — and concludes they're alike, when they're exact opposites. It's the sharpest reminder of what these vectors actually encode: not meaning in the dictionary sense, but distributional similarity — the company a word keeps — which usually tracks meaning, and sometimes, precisely for opposites, points the wrong way entirely.

A second road: GloVe

word2vec earns its map by prediction — slide a window, guess the neighbours, let the gradients sculpt the space. A year later, Pennington, Socher and Manning at Stanford arrived at the same kind of map from the opposite direction, by counting. Their method is GloVe — Global Vectors — and the contrast between the two is the whole story of how embeddings work.

Start by building the co-occurrence matrix \(X\): go over the entire corpus once and tally \(X_{ij}\) = how many times word \(j\) appears in the context of word \(i\). This is a giant, global table of who-keeps-company-with-whom — the distributional hypothesis written out in full as counts. word2vec never builds this table; it streams the corpus and learns locally, window by window. GloVe stares at the whole thing at once.

GloVe's insight is that the ratios of co-occurrence probabilities carry the meaning. For "ice" versus "steam": the word "solid" co-occurs much more with "ice", "gas" much more with "steam", while "water" co-occurs with both and "fashion" with neither — and it's those ratios, not the raw counts, that pin down the relationship. So GloVe learns vectors whose dot products match the log of the co-occurrence counts, minimizing $$J = \sum_{i,j} f(X_{ij})\,\big(w_i^\top \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2.$$ Read the inside of that sum as a promise GloVe is trying to keep for every pair: the dot product of two word vectors, plus a bias for each, should land on \(\log X_{ij}\) — the log of how often they co-occur. Two vectors that share a lot of company have a large log-count to hit, so they're pulled into alignment; that's the distributional hypothesis re-expressed as least-squares on a table of logs.

The weighting \(f(X_{ij})\) out front is the quiet hero of the whole loss. Without it, the rarest pairs (a single noisy co-occurrence) would carry the same weight as the most reliable ones, and the millions of "the"-with-everything pairs would swamp the signal. So GloVe sets $$f(x) = \begin{cases}(x/x_{\max})^{\alpha} & x \lt x_{\max}\\ 1 & x \ge x_{\max}\end{cases}$$ with \(\alpha = 3/4\) — note that 3/4 again, the same flattening exponent word2vec used on its negatives — and a cap \(x_{\max}\) (the original paper uses \(100\); the worked mini-corpus below uses \(x_{\max} = 10\)). The weight grows with the count but caps out at 1, so frequent pairs never dominate, and it goes to zero at \(x = 0\) so empty cells cost nothing. Watch it on real numbers: in our toy corpus the pair "the"–"loves" co-occurs \(X = 6.0\) times (the largest cell), giving \(f = (6/10)^{0.75} = 0.682\); a one-off pair with \(X = 1.0\) gets only \(f = 0.178\); and a genuinely rare \(X = 0.25\) ("king"–"and") is squashed to \(f = 0.063\). The reliable cells shout, the flukes whisper.

Now walk a single cell through the whole machine, with the trained vectors. Take "king" and "queen". They co-occur \(X = 0.667\) times (a fractional count because GloVe weights a context word \(d\) steps away by \(1/d\)), so the target is \(\log X = -0.406\) and the weight is \(f = 0.131\). The fitted model reconstructs that cell as \(w_{\text{king}}^\top \tilde{w}_{\text{queen}} + b_{\text{king}} + \tilde{b}_{\text{queen}} = (-0.034) + (-0.247) + (-0.127) = -0.408\) — against a target of \(-0.406\), a residual of just \(-0.002\). The "the"–"king" cell tells the same story at the high-count end: target \(\log X = 1.658\), model \(1.654\), residual \(-0.004\). Across the 21 top-weighted cells the mean absolute residual is \(0.0025\) — the factorization fits the log-count table almost exactly. And it gets there fast: the weighted loss falls from \(18.04\) at initialization to \(0.005\) after 600 AdaGrad iterations, a \(99.97\%\) drop. Two roads, one destination. Predictive (word2vec) learns the map by repeatedly guessing local context; count-based (GloVe) learns it by factoring a global co-occurrence table. That count-vs-predict split is the same one Levy and Goldberg dissolved a beat ago — SGNS turned out to be implicitly factorizing a shifted-PMI matrix, so word2vec is also a count-based factorizer underneath, just done with SGD instead of explicit least-squares. GloVe makes the factorization explicit; word2vec hides it in the gradient. No surprise, then, that they land in remarkably similar places — the same neighbourhoods, the same analogy directions — which is the deepest hint that the map itself, not the algorithm, is the real object. The company a word keeps determines where it lives, however you do the bookkeeping.

Start from raw counts, not predictions. A small window slides over the mini-corpus, and every time two words fall inside it together their co-occurrence count grows — closer words count more (a word d steps away adds only 1/d). The result is the symmetric matrix X on the right: how often each word keeps company with each other word. This table of context statistics is the only thing GloVe ever looks at.

Not every count deserves equal trust. A pair seen once or twice is noisy; a pair seen very often shouldn't drown out everything else. GloVe weights each entry by f(x) = (x/x_max)^0.75, but caps it at 1 once x reaches x_max. The curve climbs gently from zero — rare pairs get little say — then flattens to a ceiling so the most frequent pairs can't dominate the fit. The dots are the real f-values for the matrix cells.

Now the model itself. GloVe asks each pair's dot-product plus two bias terms to land near the log of its count: w_i·w̃_j + b_i + b̃_j ≈ log X_ij. Take a real pair from this corpus and read every term off the data — the count X, its logarithm log X, the weight f(X), the model's dot-product and biases, and the resulting residual times its weight. That weighted squared error, summed over all pairs, is the whole loss.

Fitting that objective is exactly factorising X: rows of W and W̃ are pushed until their dot-products reproduce log X across the whole table. The learned vectors are summed (W+W̃) and projected to two dimensions — and meaning falls out as geometry. Royal words, people words, and animal words separate into their own regions, the same kind of map the predictive route produces. The loss collapses from its starting value to nearly zero.

So the two routes meet. GloVe is count-based — it factorises a matrix of log co-occurrence counts explicitly. word2vec is predictive — it slides a window and predicts context words. Levy and Goldberg showed these are two faces of one coin: skip-gram with negative sampling is implicitly factorising a shifted pointwise-mutual-information matrix built from the very same counts. Different algorithms, the same underlying co-occurrence geometry.

Did you know? The same trick is everywhere

Pause the map for a moment — Serega has a did-you-know he can't keep to himself. Notice that nothing in skip-gram is about words. The recipe is: take a sequence of tokens, slide a window, push things that co-occur close together, push random pairs apart. "Token" can be anything. Feed it a different kind of sequence and the exact same machine learns a map of that domain. Once you see this, embeddings stop being an NLP trick and become a way of thinking — and it shows up in places that have nothing to do with language.

Start with graphs. A social network, a web of citations, a molecule — how do you embed a node? DeepWalk (Perozzi et al., 2014) and node2vec (Grover & Leskovec, 2016) had a gorgeous idea: take random walks over the graph. Start at a node, hop to a random neighbour, hop again — and the sequence of nodes you visit is a "sentence." Nodes that get walked near each other are "words that keep company." Run skip-gram on those walks and you get node embeddings where graph-neighbours land near each other in vector space. A random walk on a graph is a sentence; the Cartographer never needed to know it was looking at a network instead of a paragraph.

Now shopping. Replace "sentence" with "a customer's purchase history" and "word" with "product," and skip-gram learns which items keep company in baskets — that's item2vec (Barkan & Koenigstein, 2016) and its cousin prod2vec. Products bought together drift together in the space; "customers who bought this also bought…" falls out as nearest-neighbour search on the resulting vectors, no ratings matrix required. The recommendation engines behind a thousand storefronts are, under the hood, word2vec with the vocabulary swapped from English to a catalog.

And — the one closest to home for many of you — biology. A protein is a string of twenty amino-acid "letters"; treat it as a sentence and you can pre-train a transformer on hundreds of millions of sequences. That's ESM (Rives et al., 2021) and ProtBERT: amino-acid sequences as "sentences," self-supervised the same way Firth's company-it-keeps idea suggests. The payoff is exactly the L6→retrieval move — instead of running BLAST's slow alignment to find related proteins, you embed a query protein and do cosine nearest-neighbour search in the learned space; near-in-embedding means similar structure or function. The same idea reaches into genomics (DNABERT, gene2vec — k-mers or co-expressed genes as tokens) and into biosignals (EEG / ECG models where a "token" is a short time window of the recording, and the position axis is literally time). Proteins, genomes, brain waves, purchases, social graphs — the alien from First Contact wasn't special. Everything is a sequence of tokens in a learned space, and the map we just drew is the same map, over and over, in every domain that has structure to capture.

Start with four inputs that could not look more different: a sentence of text, an image, a sound waveform, and a protein. Nothing about them obviously belongs in the same picture — yet every one of them is about to be handled by the exact same recipe.

Step one is always the same: cut the thing into tokens. Text splits into words, an image into ViT patches, audio into spectrogram frames, a protein into amino-acid residues. Wildly different objects, but each is now just a sequence of small pieces.

Step two: embed every token into a vector, and the same model places it as a point in ONE shared space. The four modalities settle into four corners here, but they live on the same axes, measured by the same ruler — text dots and image dots are now directly comparable.

Add the axes and a legend, and the point is made: text, image, audio and protein all live in one geometry. That is exactly why image↔text search works — a caption and the picture it describes can land near each other, the foundation of CLIP-style cross-modal retrieval.

Three hundred numbers you can’t see

Time for the catch, and it's a big one. The maps people actually ship aren't the six-number sketch we've been drawing — production embeddings live in 300 dimensions (GloVe's standard size; word2vec and friends use 100–1000). Three hundred numbers per word. And that's the good news: 300-D is exactly what gives the space enough room to encode gender and royalty and tense and topic and register all as separate, simultaneous directions. The richness is the dimensionality.

Here's the problem the richness creates. You cannot see 300 dimensions. Your visual system maxes out at three, and you really only reason comfortably in two. Every picture in this chapter — the parallelogram, the islands of meaning — has been a lie of convenience, a 2-D shadow I drew so the idea would fit on a page. The real space is a 300-dimensional cloud of forty-thousand points, and no eye will ever look at it directly.

This matters beyond the pretty pictures. You debug what you can see. When an embedding model misbehaves — synonyms drifting apart, two senses of a word tangled together, a cluster that shouldn't exist — you need to look. And you can't look at 300-D. So we need a way to take that vast, high-dimensional map and fold it down to two or three dimensions you can actually plot, while keeping as much of the real shape as possible. Enter a new character, whose entire job is exactly that fold.

How big a map, and is it any good?

Two questions the "300 numbers" stakes raise but don't answer. First: why 300? The dimensionality is a knob, and it's a genuine trade-off, not a magic constant. Too few dimensions — say 10 — and the space runs out of room: gender and tense and topic and register all have to share axes, so they bleed into each other and the analogies blur. Too many — say 5000 — and you're mostly modelling noise, paying compute and memory for dimensions that capture nothing real, and every downstream nearest-neighbour search gets slower. Empirically the sweet spot for static word embeddings sits around 50 to 300: enough room for the relations that matter, little enough that the dimensions stay meaningful. It's the same elbow logic as the scree plot you're about to meet — add dimensions until each new one buys you almost nothing.

Second, the sharper question: how do you know the map is any good? "The analogies look cool" is not a measurement. The field built intrinsic evaluations — quick scores you can compute on the vectors alone, no full search system required. Two flavours. Similarity benchmarks like WordSim-353 and SimLex-999 hand you word pairs with human-rated relatedness, and you check whether your embedding's cosines correlate with those human judgments. (SimLex is the stricter one: it deliberately rates similarity, not loose association, so it punishes a model that thinks "coffee" and "cup" are near — the antonym/association trap from a few beats ago made into a test.) And analogy benchmarks — the Google analogy set, BATS — score whether king−man+woman lands on queen across thousands of relations.

The trap is to trust those scores too far. A model can top WordSim and still be mediocre at the thing you actually care about — retrieving the right document, ranking the right product. Intrinsic scores correlate with downstream usefulness, but loosely; the only evaluation that fully counts is the extrinsic one, measured on your real task with the metrics from L5. Intrinsic benchmarks are the fast smoke-test; the real exam comes later. Treat a high SimLex number the way you'd treat a green unit test — necessary, encouraging, and not remotely the same as shipping.

One last connection before we fold. PCA, which the Cartographer is about to use, is the simplest possible autoencoder: squeeze the data through a narrow linear bottleneck and reconstruct it, and the best linear bottleneck is exactly PCA's top components. That reframing is the doorway to the rest of deep learning — make that bottleneck non-linear, stack layers around it, and you get the neural autoencoders and representation learners that the next chapters are built on. PCA isn't just a plotting tool; it's the linear ancestor of every learned embedding in this course. With that, let's meet the Cartographer and fold the map for real.

Enter the Cartographer

Meet the Cartographer. Her one job, the only thing she does, is dimensionality reduction: take a map that lives in 300 dimensions and crease it down to 2 or 3, so a human can finally hold it. Like every good map-folder, she faces a trade-off — the folded map can't be the territory, something is always lost in the creases — and the whole craft is in choosing what to keep.

Frame the task precisely. We have forty thousand points in 300-D. We want a new set of forty thousand points in 2-D such that the structure that matters — which words are near which, which clusters hang together — survives the projection. "Structure that matters" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it splits the field in two. One school says: keep the directions of greatest variance, the axes along which the data is most spread out — that's PCA, and it's linear, fast, and honest about how much it threw away. The other says: forget global geometry, just keep each point's local neighbours close — that's t-SNE and UMAP, non-linear and gorgeous and treacherous. We'll walk through both, with the real numbers, in the climb that follows.

How PCA actually folds — and how to round the cloud

Before the widget runs the numbers, here is the whole mechanic in four moves. One: center the data — subtract the mean from every vector, so the cloud sits at the origin and "variance" means spread, not offset. Two: form the covariance matrix \(C = \tfrac{1}{n}X^\top X\) — a \(d \times d\) table whose entry \((i,j)\) is how dimensions \(i\) and \(j\) vary together. Three: take its eigenvectors. These are the principal directions — the orthogonal axes along which the data is most spread — and each comes with an eigenvalue \(\lambda_k\) that is exactly the variance the data has along that axis. Four: sort by eigenvalue, keep the top \(k\), and project every point onto those \(k\) directions. That projection is the folded map. Nothing else happens; PCA is just "rotate so the axes line up with the spread, then drop the flattest axes."

The eigenvalues are not abstract — they are the budget you spend, and they let you state your loss honestly. Add up all the variances (\(\sum_k \lambda_k\)); the fraction sitting on the top two axes is the fraction of the picture you actually kept. On our 50-dimensional GloVe words, those top two axes hold a modest share — about \(37.7\%\) of the total spread survives the squash to 2-D, which is why the PCA scatter has real clusters but loose, overlapping edges: the other \(62\%\) of the structure is genuinely gone, folded into the creases. The rotation demo cloud is the opposite, easy case — it was built to live almost entirely in a plane, so its top two axes keep \(97.21\%\), and projecting it loses almost nothing. Same operation, two honest numbers: a thin, near-flat cloud projects cleanly; a fat, high-dimensional one like real embeddings cannot, and PCA tells you so to the decimal.

Now the move that's hiding in those eigenvalues: whitening. After projecting, the \(k\) new axes still have wildly unequal variances — the first principal axis might carry ten times the spread of the third, so Euclidean distance and cosine are quietly dominated by whichever direction happens to be biggest. Whitening fixes that with one extra step: divide each component by its own standard deviation, \(\sqrt{\lambda_k}\). Every axis now has unit variance, the cloud becomes decorrelated and equally scaled — a round, isotropic ball instead of a stretched cigar — and no single high-variance direction can hijack your similarity measure. The trade-off is real and worth saying plainly: dividing by a small \(\sqrt{\lambda_k}\) blows the low-variance axes up to the same size as the big ones, which means it amplifies whatever noise was hiding in the tail directions. Whitening is sphering for free, but it trusts your smallest components more than they may deserve.

Hold onto this word — whitening — because it returns in L14 wearing a different costume. There, contextual embeddings from a raw Transformer collapse into a narrow anisotropic cone, all vectors pointing roughly the same way, and one of the cures is precisely this transform: center, then rescale by the inverse square root of the covariance, re-rounding the squashed cloud into an isotropic ball so cosine regains its dynamic range. Same linear move, same goal — make every direction count equally — just applied to a learned embedding space instead of a PCA plot. You're meeting the tool here, in its simplest form, before it does serious work in L14.

PCA loses nothing until the very last step

Split PCA into its two halves, because only one of them is lossy and people blame the wrong one. The covariance matrix is symmetric, so the spectral theorem hands you an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors: the change of basis is an orthogonal matrix \(Q\) with \(Q^{\top}Q=I\), which is to say a rotation. And a rotation preserves everything you might want to measure — \(\lVert Qx-Qy\rVert=\lVert x-y\rVert\), and angles with it. After step one the numbers on the page have all changed and the cloud is geometrically identical to what it was. Nothing has been decided, nothing thrown away; the axes have merely been renamed so that the spread lines up with them.

The loss lives entirely in step two, and it comes with an exact price tag rather than a vibe. Drop the components with the smallest eigenvalues and the mean squared reconstruction error is precisely the sum of the eigenvalues you dropped: \(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i\lVert x_i-\hat{x}_i\rVert^{2}=\sum_{k\gt m}\lambda_k\). That is the entire content of "the projection keeps such-and-such a share of the variance" — the retained fraction \(\sum_{k\le m}\lambda_k/\sum_k\lambda_k\) is the share of squared spread that survived, and no other \(m\)-dimensional subspace does better. When PCA reports its loss, it is not being modest; it is quoting a bound.

So read the scree plot as an invoice. The elbow is the point where each extra axis stops buying you much error reduction, and that is a decision about budget, not about truth. But the more important reading is the one people skip: if the eigenvalues decay slowly, with no elbow at all, the data is telling you it does not live near any low-dimensional plane, and no linear fold will ever produce a faithful picture of it — a fact worth knowing before you present a scatter plot to a room. That is the honest version of folding. The tools that come next produce far prettier pictures by giving this guarantee up entirely, and it is worth knowing exactly what was traded away.

Each word lives as 300 numbers — 300 dimensions. We can hold three at most in our heads, so 300-D is just a blur: real structure is in there, but no eye can see it. The Cartographer's job is to fold the map small enough to carry, keeping the shape.

PCA finds the directions of greatest spread and projects onto the top two. The 44 words drop into a flat scatter, and same-meaning words land together — royalty, animals, countries, tech each form their own island. These two axes keep 37.7% of the original variance.

Why only 37.7%? Look at the variance each component carries: PC1 holds 19.6%, PC2 18.1%, and after that the bars shrink fast. The first two axes are the richest pair, but the rest of the meaning is spread thin across many smaller directions we had to drop.

t-SNE chases a different goal: keep each word near its true neighbors, even if the global layout bends. The clusters pull in tight and the islands separate cleanly — a far more readable map. But beware: in a t-SNE plot the gaps between clusters and the cluster sizes are NOT meaningful distances.

Here is a real correlated cloud of points, drawn in a 3-D box. Most of its spread runs along one slanted direction; a second direction carries a little more; and along a third direction there is almost no spread at all. The structure is there, but our chosen axes don't line up with it.

PCA finds the cloud's OWN axes — the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The longest arrow (PC1) points along the greatest spread, the next (PC2) along the second, and the short one (PC3) along the direction where the cloud is nearly flat. These three arrows are what we will turn to.

Now turn the whole cloud so its own axes swing toward ours. This is a rigid rotation — nothing is stretched or distorted, every distance between points is preserved. Watch the cloud and the arrows pivot together, partway toward alignment with the screen.

Fully turned: now the cloud's own axes ARE the screen axes. PC1 runs left–right, PC2 up–down, and the thin PC3 points almost straight at us. The variance splits 79.79% on PC1, 17.42% on PC2, and only 2.79% on PC3 — almost everything lives in the PC1–PC2 plane.

Drop the thin PC3 and look straight down the PC1–PC2 plane: the cloud flattens into a flat 2-D scatter. We threw away a whole dimension, yet 97.21% of the original spread survives — only 2.79% was lost. That is PCA: rotate to your own axes, then keep the fattest ones.

PCA never promised your neighbours

Before the Swiss roll gets blamed on PCA's linearity, state precisely what PCA optimises. It minimises one number: the total squared reconstruction error summed over all points. Each point contributes its own distance to the plane and nothing else; the word "neighbour" appears nowhere in the objective, and no pair of points is ever mentioned. So the false-neighbour catastrophe is not a limitation somebody could patch with a better solver — it is the objective declining to pay for something it was never asked to buy. That is precisely the reading this chapter will apply to t-SNE a few beats from now, run on the other tool first.

Now the guarantee it does give, which is more useful than it sounds. The projection is a linear map with orthonormal rows, so it can only shorten distances, never lengthen them: \(\lVert Px-Py\rVert\le\lVert x-y\rVert\), by Pythagoras on the discarded components. Read the consequence off the plot. Two points that look far apart on a PCA scatter genuinely are far apart in the original space — the projection cannot manufacture separation it did not have. What it can do is the reverse: collapse genuinely distant points on top of each other, since the whole difference between them may have lived in the axes you dropped. On a PCA plot, large distances are trustworthy and small ones are not.

Hold that sentence next to the one the t-SNE warning will give you, because together they are a single rule worth memorising. PCA is honest about the global layout and lies about neighbourhoods; the neighbour-preserving methods are careful about neighbourhoods and say nothing meaningful about the global layout. Neither is the good one. You choose which lie you can afford for the question you are asking — and when the answer matters, you plot both and only believe what survives in both pictures.

When the map is curved

PCA is honest, fast, and linear — and that last word is its ceiling. PCA can only rotate the space and flatten it with a single straight cut; it assumes the structure worth keeping lies along straight axes. Often the structure doesn't. Picture data shaped like a Swiss roll — a 2-D sheet rolled up in 3-D. Two points on opposite ends of the rolled-up sheet sit physically close in 3-D, but along the surface, the path that actually matters, they're far apart. Slice that roll flat with one straight PCA cut and you glue the far ends together: a catastrophe of false neighbours.

The fix is to stop preserving global distances and preserve neighbourhoods instead. The idea, called manifold learning, assumes the data — though embedded in 300-D — actually lies on a lower-dimensional curved surface, a manifold, like that 2-D sheet curled through 3-D space. We don't care about the straight-line distance between two far-apart words; we care that each word keeps the same close neighbours after the fold. Get the local relationships right, let the global layout bend however it must, and unroll the curl instead of slicing through it.

That's the philosophy behind t-SNE (van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008) and its faster cousin UMAP (McInnes, Healy & Melville, 2018). Both turn distances into neighbour probabilities in the high-D space, then arrange points in 2-D so those neighbour relationships match as closely as possible. The picture they produce is the iconic one — tight, well-separated islands of meaning, the plot that launched a thousand papers. We already met it on the last step. It's beautiful. It is also, in a very specific way, a liar — which is the next beat.

The readable picture

Let's read the actual t-SNE plot we just built — 44 real words, 50-D GloVe vectors, projected down with perplexity 14 — but first, let's earn it by walking the algorithm through in four exact moves on a smaller worked example: 9 real GloVe-50 words (animals and royalty), anchored on "cat", at perplexity 5. t-SNE is not magic; it's four well-defined steps.

One — high-D affinities. For the anchor word "cat", t-SNE asks how probable every other word is as its neighbour, using a Gaussian centred on "cat": $$p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(-\lVert x_i - x_k\rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}.$$ The width \(\sigma_i\) is not fixed — it's tuned, per point, by binary search so the row hits a target perplexity, the effective number of neighbours. Perplexity is \(2^{H}\) where \(H\) is the entropy of the row in bits; demand perplexity 5 and you demand entropy \(\log_2 5 = 2.322\) bits. For "cat" the search settles on \(\sigma = 2.003\), and the resulting row reads exactly that: nearest neighbour "dog" gets \(p = 0.405\), "puppy" \(0.196\), "lion" \(0.140\), "kitten" \(0.136\), down to far-off "throne" at \(0.0027\). Crank \(\sigma\) too small and only "dog" survives (perplexity → 1); too large and all eight words look equally close (perplexity → 8). The binary search finds the width that makes "cat" attend to about five neighbours.

Two — symmetrize. Those conditionals aren't symmetric ("cat" may call "dog" a 0.405-neighbour while "dog"'s own row disagrees), so t-SNE averages each pair into a single joint probability \(p_{ij} = (p_{j\mid i} + p_{i\mid j}) / (2N)\), with \(N\) the number of points (here 9, so \(2N = 18\)). For our anchor that turns "cat"–"dog" into a joint \(p_{ij} = 0.0454\), the strongest tie in the cat-row. Three — low-D similarities with a heavy tail. Now place the points in 2-D and measure their similarity with a Student-t kernel (one degree of freedom) instead of a Gaussian: $$q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\ne l}(1 + \lVert y_k - y_l\rVert^2)^{-1}}.$$ The Student-t's heavy tail is the fix for the crowding problem: in the cramped 2-D plane there isn't room to honour all the high-D distances, so moderate-distance points would pile up at the centre — the heavy tail lets them sit comfortably far out without paying a huge probability penalty. In the laid-out plot "cat"–"dog" settles at \(q_{ij} = 0.0604\) (close, as it should be) while "cat"–"throne" sits at \(0.0020\) (far).

Four — match P to Q, and move the points. t-SNE measures the gap between the high-D neighbour distribution \(P\) and the low-D one \(Q\) with the Kullback–Leibler divergence $$\mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q) = \sum_{i\ne j} p_{ij}\,\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}},$$ which is small exactly when every neighbour-probability in 2-D matches its high-D target. On the converged 9-word layout that divergence is down to \(\mathrm{KL} = 0.0411\). And the points get there by gradient descent: the gradient on each point, $$\frac{\partial \mathrm{KL}}{\partial y_i} = 4\sum_{j}(p_{ij} - q_{ij})(y_i - y_j)\,(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j\rVert^2)^{-1},$$ reads as a tug-of-war of springs — where \(p_{ij} \gt q_{ij}\) (closer in high-D than the plot shows) the point is pulled toward its neighbour; where \(p_{ij} \lt q_{ij}\) it's pushed away. At the anchor the gradient \(\partial\mathrm{KL}/\partial y_i\) is a small \((-0.0018,\ 0.0021)\), magnitude \(0.0028\) — the update step moves the point along \(-\nabla\): "cat" has nearly settled. The widget below runs these four moves in order — affinities \(p\) → Student-t \(q\) → the KL objective → the gradient that migrates each point. One practical knob the four moves hide: for the first ~250 iterations t-SNE multiplies every \(p_{ij}\) by an early-exaggeration factor (~12 in scikit-learn) so the clusters coalesce before fine placement — drop it and the layout often jams in a tangled local minimum.

Now back to the 44-word plot, where the same four moves ran at perplexity 14. The payoff is plain in the result. The seven groups we planted — royalty, family, animals, countries, capitals, tech, transport — pull into seven tight, cleanly separated islands. "king", "queen", "prince", "throne", "crown" huddle in one corner; "cat", "dog", "puppy", "kitten", "tiger", "lion" in another; "computer", "software", "data", "algorithm", "internet" off in the tech quarter. The Lexical Gremlin's nightmare made visible: words that mean similar things now sit together, with no synonym list, no stemming, nothing hand-coded — just the company they keep, learned from raw text and folded onto a page you can read at a glance.

Compare it to the PCA plot from the same data and the difference is instructive. PCA's islands are real but loose and overlapping, because a single linear cut keeping 37.7% of the variance can't pull them fully apart. t-SNE's are crisp and well-spaced, because it bent the layout to honour local neighbourhoods. This is exactly why t-SNE and UMAP are the default tools whenever someone wants to show you an embedding space. The two are not interchangeable, though: UMAP's fuzzy-graph objective and spectral init retain more of the large-scale layout, so its inter-cluster arrangement is a bit more trustworthy than t-SNE's (still not a literal ruler) — and, unlike t-SNE, UMAP can transform new points after fitting. Which is precisely where the danger lives — because that crisp, convincing picture invites you to read things into it that simply aren't there.

Start in high-D. For the anchor word 'cat', t-SNE turns distances into a probability: each neighbor j gets a Gaussian affinity p(j|i) = exp(−‖xᵢ−xⱼ‖²/2σᵢ²), normalized to sum to 1. Near words (other animals) get tall bars; far words (royalty) get almost nothing. The width σ is not guessed — it is tuned by binary search so the row has a chosen PERPLEXITY (here 5), which acts like the effective number of neighbors the anchor pays attention to.

Now the low-D side. t-SNE lays the points out in 2-D and measures their closeness with a Student-t kernel q_ij ∝ (1+‖yᵢ−yⱼ‖²)⁻¹ — not a Gaussian. The inset compares the two: the Student-t (orange) has a HEAVY TAIL, so even far-apart points keep a little affinity. That tail is the cure for the 'crowding problem': it lets clusters spread out and gives moderately-distant points room to move apart in the plane.

What does t-SNE optimize? It wants the low-D closeness q to match the high-D closeness p. We overlay the anchor's q bars (orange) on its p bars (blue): where they differ, the layout is wrong. The single number measuring the total mismatch is the Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(P‖Q) = Σ p_ij log(p_ij/q_ij) — shown here for the real layout. t-SNE moves the points to drive this cost down (it is always ≥ 0, and 0 only if q equals p everywhere).

How do the points move? Take the gradient of KL with respect to each point yᵢ: ∂C/∂yᵢ = 4 Σⱼ (p_ij−q_ij)(yᵢ−yⱼ)(1+‖yᵢ−yⱼ‖²)⁻¹. Each arrow is the descent direction for that point. Where p > q the term pulls two points together (an attractive force); where p < q it pushes them apart (a repulsive force). Following these forces, step after step, is exactly the migration the companion view animates — same algorithm, seen as forces.

One caution before you read a t-SNE map. Perplexity is a knob: change it and the picture changes — a few tight blobs at low perplexity, looser structure at high. And because t-SNE only preserves WHO-is-near-WHOM locally, the gaps between clusters and the sizes of clusters are not meaningful global distances. Trust the neighborhoods; do not measure the empty space between islands.

t-SNE starts from a PCA layout, so at iteration zero the points sit almost on top of one another near the center — the four colored groups are jumbled and hard to tell apart. Nothing has migrated yet; this is only the starting picture.

A few hundred iterations in, each point begins to drift toward its true neighbors. The four colors start to peel apart — you can see the clusters forming, though they still touch. This is the migration that gives t-SNE its name's spirit: like points seek like points.

Halfway through, the clusters tighten and the gaps between them widen. The colored islands are now clearly separate — each group has pulled into its own compact knot, and the overlap from the start is gone.

After a thousand iterations the layout settles into four clean islands — a map you can read at a glance. But beware: in a t-SNE plot the GAPS between islands and the SIZES of the clusters are not meaningful distances. t-SNE preserves who-is-near-whom locally, not the global geometry.

How to misread a t-SNE plot

Here's the trap, and it's the single most important caveat in this whole chapter — call it the Cartographer's warning. A t-SNE plot is not a map you can measure. It preserves who's-near-whom and almost nothing else, and three things on it that look meaningful are mirages.

One: the distances between clusters mean nothing. If the royalty island sits far from the animals island while countries sits close to capitals, you cannot conclude royalty is "more different" from animals than countries is from capitals. t-SNE optimizes local neighbourhoods; it makes no promise about the spacing between well-separated clusters, and that spacing is largely an accident of the optimization. Two: cluster sizes mean nothing. A tight little knot and a big loose blob can hold the same number of equally-similar points — t-SNE's adaptive bandwidth \(\sigma_i\) expands dense clusters and contracts sparse ones to keep everything legible. The area a cluster takes up is not how spread-out it really is.

Three: it's not even stable. Change the perplexity — the neighbour-count knob — and the picture reorganizes. Low perplexity shatters the data into many tiny islands; high perplexity merges them into continents; the Wattenberg, Viégas & Johnson (2016) "How to Use t-SNE Effectively" interactive shows the same data producing wildly different plots as you turn that one dial. Re-run with a new random seed and clusters rotate and drift. So the discipline is strict: read a t-SNE plot for which points group together, full stop. Never read distances, never read cluster sizes, never read a single run as ground truth. The picture is a lossy projection presented with the confidence of a photograph — and that confidence is exactly what makes it dangerous.

What the cost actually buys

The map's distortions come from its objective, not just from implementation. t-SNE minimises \(C=\sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})\), a Kullback–Leibler divergence between symmetrized high-dimensional affinities and low-dimensional affinities. The weights \(p_{ij}\) emphasise source neighbours.

If a source neighbour has large \(p_{ij}\) but tiny \(q_{ij}\), the cost grows. When \(p_{ij}=0\), its direct term vanishes, but every \(q\) shares one normalizer. Moving that pair changes other terms. The gradient includes repulsion for \(p_{ij}<q_{ij}\), so placing distant source points close together is not free. The resulting gaps reflect a balance of attraction and repulsion, not a calibrated map of source distances.

Bandwidth \(\sigma_i\) is chosen per point for a common perplexity. Dense and sparse source regions therefore use different local scales. Cluster area need not preserve source density, and inter-cluster distances need not preserve source separation. UMAP uses a different neighbourhood objective with its own attraction and repulsion; neither method licenses reading every visible gap as an original-space distance.

UMAP pays a different price

The chapter keeps saying "t-SNE and UMAP" in one breath, and mostly that is fair — both build a neighbour graph in the original space and then arrange points on a plane so the graph survives. But they differ in what the layout is billed for, and the difference shows on the page. t-SNE's objective is normalised over all pairs: the denominator of \(q_{ij}\) sums over every pair of points, so moving one point changes the cost of every other, and every point feels a repulsion from every other. UMAP writes a cross-entropy over the edges of a \(k\)-neighbour graph instead — an explicit attraction along the edges that exist, and an explicit repulsion applied to a sample of the edges that don't.

Three practical consequences fall out of that one change. Because repulsion is sampled rather than summed, the cost per step stops growing with the square of the number of points, which is why UMAP scales to data sizes where t-SNE stops being usable. Because the objective pulls against a fixed graph rather than a globally normalised distribution, the arrangement between clusters is anchored a little more firmly — this is the honest content of "UMAP keeps more of the large-scale structure", and it is a matter of degree, not a promise, and still not a ruler. And because the layout is fitted as a map from the neighbour graph, UMAP can place new points afterwards without redrawing everything; t-SNE cannot, which is a real engineering difference the moment your index keeps growing.

What does not change is the list of three forbidden readings, and it is worth saying why. Cluster area is still a rendering decision — min_dist alone will inflate or shrink every blob without a single point changing its neighbours. Between-cluster gaps are still barely paid for, because the sampled repulsion has no reason to place two well-separated groups at any particular distance. And re-running with another seed still reshuffles the picture. The two knobs to know are n_neighbors, which plays exactly the local-versus-global role perplexity played, and min_dist, which controls how tightly points may pack — that is, it controls precisely the thing you were told not to read. If a picture changes when you turn a dial that only affects the drawing, the picture is not a measurement.

The Gremlin, cornered

Step back and look at what we've done to the Lexical Gremlin. For three chapters he wedged a brick wall between every pair of words that meant the same thing and were spelled differently — "couch" and "sofa" scored a flat zero, as far apart as "couch" and "crocodile". The map breaks his wall. "couch" and "sofa" now land as neighbours, "cat" and "dog" sit at cosine 0.922, synonyms cluster, and exact spelling stopped being destiny. He's cornered. For the first time, he's losing.

But — and read this carefully, because it sets up the next chapter — he is not yet caged. There's one move he has left, and it's a good one. Our map gives each word exactly one vector. One point, one position, forever. Now think about the word "bank". It means the side of a river and a place that holds money — two genuinely different senses — and our embedding is forced to cram both into a single location. So "bank" lands at the hopeless average of "river" and "money", a smear between two meanings, fully near to neither. "Apple" the fruit and "Apple" the company; "python" the snake and "python" the language; "spring" the season, the coil, the water source — every one of them collapsed to a single dot.

This is polysemy, and it's the Gremlin's last redoubt. A static embedding — word2vec, GloVe, every method in this chapter — assigns one vector per word type, blind to the fact that the same string means different things in different sentences. "I sat on the river bank" and "I deposited cash at the bank" hand the model the identical vector for "bank", and it cannot tell them apart. To finish the Gremlin off we'll need a vector that changes with its context — a representation where "bank" near "river" and "bank" near "money" become different points. That's a contextual embedding, and it's the whole project of the next chapter. The Gremlin is cornered. Caging him is L7.

Why near really means related

Before the expedition is tallied, settle the one claim the whole map rests on. «Near means related» is not something we noticed about the picture and got lucky with — it is forced by the objective that drew it. Read the skip-gram softmax as the rule it is: \( p(c\mid w)=\frac{\exp(u_c^{\top}v_w)}{\sum_{c'}\exp(u_{c'}^{\top}v_w)} \), one dot product between a word vector \(v_w\) and a context vector \(u_c\) deciding how likely \(c\) is to be seen beside \(w\). Everything the model can say about a word, it says through that vector.

Now state the distributional hypothesis as an equation instead of a slogan: suppose two words have the very same context distribution, \(p(\cdot\mid w_1)=p(\cdot\mid w_2)\). Equal softmax outputs for every context force the dot products to agree up to a constant the normaliser swallows, so \( (v_{w_1}-v_{w_2})^{\top}(u_c-u_{c'})=0 \) for every pair of contexts. The difference of the two word vectors is orthogonal to every difference of context vectors — and once those differences span the space, the only vector orthogonal to all of them is the zero vector. Same company, same coordinates. Real corpora only make the distributions approximately equal, so the difference is small rather than zero, and a small difference between two vectors is exactly what a high cosine is. That, and nothing else, is what gives Sir Cosine authority over this map.

The same line also explains why the redoubt you just watched the Gremlin retreat into cannot be stormed from here. A word with two senses still has only one distribution to fit, and that distribution is the blend of both senses; so the single vector settles wherever the blend points, orthogonal to nothing and faithful to neither. More training will not help, more dimensions will not help, because the model was never handed the sentence — only the word and the company it keeps on average. Give it the sentence and the argument changes shape entirely, which is the business of the next chapter.

The map is drawn

So tally the expedition. We started with symbols that knew no nearness — "couch" and "sofa" exactly as unrelated as "couch" and "crocodile". We took Firth's old observation, a word is known by the company it keeps, and turned it into machinery: word2vec learning coordinates by predicting neighbours, negative sampling making it fast enough to matter, GloVe reaching the same map by counting. The relations fell out as directions — king to queen the very same arrow as man to woman, the analogy holding at cosine 0.861 on real vectors. Then the Cartographer folded the unseeable 300-D map down to a page: PCA keeping 37.7% of the variance honestly, t-SNE drawing the readable islands, with the strict warning never to over-read them. The Map of Meaning is drawn, and Sir Cosine — the ruler of nearness we forged back in First Contact — finally has a map worthy of his measure. He rides it from here through the rest of the course.

And the alien? The word it said three chapters ago, the one our index had no slot for, the one that came back [UNK] — it finally has a place. Not an ID in a dictionary, but a position on the map, surrounded by the words that keep its company, near everything it means and far from everything it doesn't. First Contact is, at last, fully resolved.

Two things travel with us to the next chapter. The map is gorgeous, but it's static — one frozen vector per word, and the Gremlin's last trick, "bank" with its two meanings crushed into one dot, still stands. And we drew the map; we haven't yet searched it at the scale of a billion vectors, which is its own hard problem down the road. The next chapter takes the first one head-on: we give the map context, so a word's coordinates can finally shift with the sentence around it — and the Gremlin, cornered here, gets caged for good. Words have coordinates now. Next, we teach them to read the room.
