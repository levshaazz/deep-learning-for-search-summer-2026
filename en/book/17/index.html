The Artificer's Quill — advanced query rewriting & decomposition

The answer is already home

Here is the paradox that keeps me up in the workshop. You ask, why does my espresso taste sour and watery? — and the answer is already sitting in the archive, written by a barista who knew exactly what went wrong. The passage is right there. Yet type your question and the retriever walks straight past it.

The trouble is the door. At the archive gate stands the Lexical Gremlin, and he checks only one thing: do your words match the words on the shelves? Your question says "sour" and "watery." The good passage says "under-extraction" and "channeling." Different words, same meaning — and the Gremlin, who cannot read meaning, waves the wrong scroll through and turns yours away.

In L15 I laid out the menu of query-side tricks — the names, the shapes, when a team reaches for each; in L16 we learned to grade the answers that come out the far end, and the scorecard kept pointing at the same culprit: the retriever never saw the right passage, so the Oracle never had a chance. This chapter is different. Here we go to the forge and hammer the quill that rewrites the query itself — and, just as important, we learn the exact moments when each stroke of that quill lies.

Change the query, not the retriever

When retrieval fails, the reflex is to blame the retriever — swap the encoder, retrain the index, buy a bigger model. Resist it. The far cheaper fix is usually the other side of the arrow: change the query, not the retriever. The corpus is fine; the question is malformed for the shelves it must search.

A raw, human-typed query fails in three distinct ways, and naming which one you have is the whole skill. There is the vocabulary gap — right meaning, wrong words. There is the specificity gap — the query is pitched at the wrong altitude, too specific to match a principle, or too vague to match a fact. And there is the compositionality gap — one question whose pieces of evidence live in different passages that no single retrieval can gather.

One instrument closes all three: a language model reshaping the query before it reaches the door. But every reshape is at least one extra LLM call — latency, money, and a fresh surface for the model to hallucinate on. Rewriting is high-ROI, never free. So the real craft is diagnosis: read the failure, name the gap, and reach for the one stroke that fits it.

Before the door or after it

Fix the four verbs first, because the field uses them loosely and you are billed for different things. Rewrite — replace the query with a better-aimed one. Expand — keep the query and add the terms it lacked. Decompose — cut one question into several. Step back — generalise: lift the question to the principle underneath it. Four different operations on one string, and this chapter is a workshop for all four.

Now the more useful cut. Every repair in a search pipeline sits on one of two sides of the retriever. Before it you change what you ask. After it you change what you keep: reranking reorders the candidates, compression squeezes out of them what the reader needs. Both moves are useful and neither substitutes for the other, because the cascade only ever narrows — a passage the first stage never returned is simply not in the list the reranker sorts. The best reranker in the world cannot promote a document it was never handed.

So the ceiling on recall is set at the door, and everything downstream can only spend it. That is the exact sense in which change the query, not the retriever is not a slogan but arithmetic about the funnel: once the gold has failed to enter the candidate list, no late stage rescues it, and exactly three levers remain — the query itself, the construction of the index, and the depth of the cut. The first two cost retraining and reindexing, the third is bought with latency, which is why diagnosis starts by asking which of the three you actually hold.

When the trap outranks the answer

Let me make the vocabulary gap painfully concrete, because you have felt it. The query is why does my espresso taste sour and watery? The gold passage — the one a barista would hand you — explains under-extraction and channeling: the water rushed through a crack in the puck and never pulled the sweetness. Correct, complete, and it does not contain the words "sour" or "watery" at all.

Now meet the villain of this toy: a lexical trap. Somewhere in the corpus sits a note about sour candy — "a sharp, sour, mouth-watering treat." It is useless to you. But it shares your surface words — sour, watering — and so the Lexical Gremlin, delighted, waves it to the front. The trap outranks the answer.

This is the deep, quiet law behind the whole chapter: the terms of a query need not appear in a document that answers it, and the terms that do appear are not evidence of relevance. Surface overlap and meaning are two different coordinate systems. Everything the quill does is an attempt to translate the query out of the user's words and into the archive's words.

The old quill: pseudo-relevance feedback

Long before any LLM, the field already knew the query was too poor to stand alone — and it built a quill to fatten it. The idea is pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF): run the raw query, assume the top handful of results are relevant even though nobody said so, harvest the words that recur in them, and fold those words back into a second, richer query. Retrieve, trust the top-k, expand, retrieve again.

It is worth seeing that PRF grew as two sibling families, not one lineage — the same idea discovered twice in two different theories of retrieval. On the vector-space side sits Rocchio: treat query and documents as vectors and physically shove the query point toward the centroid of the assumed-relevant set and away from the rest. On the language-model side sits the Relevance Model family — RM1, RM3 — which asks instead: what distribution over words would have generated these top documents, and how do I blend it into my query?

Both answer the vocabulary gap the same way — borrow words from documents the query already surfaced. Rocchio moves a point; RM moves a distribution. Keep the two apart in your head: they are parallel roads to one destination, not parent and child.

Rocchio is three pulls on one point

Rocchio was described as moving the point — so write down what moves it. The new query vector is a sum of three terms: the original query itself, the centroid of the documents assumed relevant, and, with a minus sign, the centroid of everything else. Three weights decide how far you trust yourself, the feedback, and the negative evidence. Read it as three pulls applied to one point: one holds it in place, one drags it toward the feedback, one pushes it away from the rest.

In pseudo-relevance feedback the third pull is almost always the weakest, and for an honest reason: nobody judged anything, so not relevant here only means did not come up top, which is a statement about the first pass and not about relevance. In practice that weight is set small, or to zero. What remains is a blend of what you asked and what came back — exactly the shape you are about to meet in RM3's \( \lambda \), one written on points and the other on distributions.

Hence the whole family shares one failure and one useless dial. The failure: both schemes are anchored on the feedback set, so a trap that made it into the top-\(k\) drags the query toward itself in either dialect — the geometry drifts, the distribution drifts, and it is the same drift. And the dial does not rescue you: adding trust in the feedback is literally sliding the point further along the same vector, which is to say drifting faster toward whatever came back. Two theories, one ceiling — exhibited next on RM3's equation, and then on its support.

Inside RM3: interpolating two vocabularies

Let us open RM3 and look at the one equation that runs it. RM3 builds a new query language model by interpolating — mixing — your original query with a relevance model \( R \) estimated over the top-\(k\) pseudo-relevant documents:

$$ P(w \mid \theta_{q'}) = (1-\lambda)\, P(w \mid q) + \lambda\, P(w \mid R) $$

Read it left to right. Every word \( w \) gets a new weight. The first term keeps you anchored to what you actually asked; the second term, \( P(w \mid R) \), pours in the words that were frequent across the top documents. The dial \( \lambda \) sets how far you drift from your own query toward the feedback set — \( \lambda = 0 \) is the raw query untouched, larger \( \lambda \) leans harder on the borrowed vocabulary.

Now stare at the second term until the whole ceiling of this method becomes visible. \( R \) is estimated only from documents the first pass already retrieved. So RM3 can lift, drop, or re-weight any word that already appears in that pass — but it can never introduce a word that was absent from it. It re-weights an existing vocabulary; it cannot mint a new one.

The support is the ceiling

RM3 left a strong claim standing: it cannot mint a word the first pass did not already contain. A claim that carries the whole turn to the LLM deserves a proof rather than an observation. Write \( \operatorname{supp} P \) for the support of a distribution — the set of terms it gives non-zero probability. The relevance model is estimated over the feedback documents and nothing else, so $$ \operatorname{supp} P_{\mathrm{RM}} \subseteq \bigcup_{d \,\in\, D_k} \operatorname{supp} d. $$ The borrowed vocabulary is contained in the vocabulary of what the first pass already returned.

Now take a term \( w \) that sits neither in the query nor in any of the top-\(k\) documents, and evaluate the interpolation at it. The first component is \( P(w \mid q) = 0 \); the second is \( P_{\mathrm{RM}}(w) = 0 \) by the inclusion above; and a convex combination of two zeros is zero for every \( \lambda \). So under-extraction was never merely improbable in the expanded query. It was impossible.

That reading also disarms the dial. \( \lambda \) moves probability mass between the two components — it never widens the support, so no setting of it can rescue you. Turn it up and you only lean harder on the feedback set, which is to say you drift faster toward the trap's words. A ceiling made of support cannot be raised from inside the loop; only a source that read beyond the first pass breaks it, and that is exactly the turn ahead.

The ceiling of an echo

So the old quill has a hard ceiling, and it is worth naming exactly why. RM3 draws its new words only from its own first pass. It cannot look at the web, cannot reason, cannot know that a barista would call your problem under-extraction. That word never appears in the retrieved set, so no amount of interpolation can conjure it. The one word that would have solved you is the one word PRF structurally cannot reach.

Worse than merely capped: PRF is fragile. It reinforces whatever it already surfaced. Remember the sour candy trap? If it rode into the top-\(k\), its confectionery vocabulary now joins the feedback model, and the second query drifts even harder toward candy and away from coffee. Feed the echo its own echo and it does not converge on truth — it amplifies the loudest early mistake.

That is the shape of the whole family: free, but capped and brittle. No extra model, no API bill — and no world knowledge either. To invent the missing word, you need a quill that has read far beyond this corpus. Which is exactly the turn the next beat takes.

A quill that has read the world

Here is the turn the whole chapter pivots on. PRF was trapped inside the corpus because it had never read anything else. An LLM has read the web — millions of documents this archive never held. So when your query lacks the word under-extraction, the model does not have to find it in the top-\(k\); it can simply supply it, from everything it absorbed in training. The missing vocabulary is no longer trapped behind the first pass.

The first clean expression of this move is GAR — Generation-Augmented Retrieval. Its recipe is disarmingly plain: prompt the model to generate helpful context for the query — a likely answer sentence, related entities, a title — and append that generated text to the original query before retrieving. The query keeps its own words and gains the archive's words alongside them. Expansion, not replacement.

Mark GAR carefully on the family tree, because it is easy to misfile. GAR is the antecedent of the expansion branch — it grows straight into Query2doc, which concatenates a generated pseudo-document onto the query for a sparse retriever. It is not the ancestor of HyDE. HyDE will make a stranger, sharper move — throw the question away entirely and search with the answer alone — and that is where the next section forges the real blade.

Don't embed the question — embed the answer

Here is the sleight of hand that makes HyDE feel like a trick and then, on reflection, feel inevitable. The user asks "why does my espresso taste sour and watery?" — and the archive never once uses those words. It says under-extraction, it says channeling. So the question, embedded as-is, lands nowhere near the passage that answers it.

The Artificer's move is to stop embedding the question. Instead the quill drafts a hypothetical answer — a short, confident paragraph that pretends to already know the reply — and we embed that. The draft may be wrong on the facts; that is fine. What matters is that a plausible answer, written by a model that has read the whole web, naturally reaches for the same technical register the real passage uses.

So the retrieval query is no longer the user's words but a forgery of the answer, and we hand that forgery to the encoder. The gold passage and the forgery now speak the same dialect — and the nearest-neighbour search finally points at the truth.

HyDE, formally

Strip the metaphor and the recipe is three steps. First, prompt an instruction-tuned generator to write \( k \) hypothetical documents \( d_1, \dots, d_k \) that would answer the query. Second, push each through an encoder \( f \) — the original paper uses Contriever, a contrastively pre-trained dense model — and average the resulting vectors into a single query embedding:

$$ e = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} f(d_i) $$

Third, retrieve the real passages whose embeddings have the highest cosine similarity to \( e \). Optionally you can fold the encoded question back in — add \( f(q) \) into the sum — as a small anchor to the literal query, but the heavy lifting is done by the drafted answers.

One label people love to misapply: HyDE is zero-shot in the sense that it needs no relevance judgments, no query-passage pairs, no fine-tuning of the retriever. But it is emphatically not unsupervised — it leans on a generator that was itself instruction-tuned to follow the prompt. The supervision moved; it did not vanish.

Closeness to the query IS the cosine — 0 is far, 1 is identical. Retrieval ranks by it.

The raw query shares only surface words. The lexical trap sits CLOSER than the gold passage — so retrieval buries the answer.

HyDE's move: don't embed the question — embed a hypothetical ANSWER, which lives in the documents' vocabulary.

Embedding that pseudo-doc lifts the gold far to the right (a big cosine jump) and drops the trap left. The gold is now rank 1.

Two spaces, one geometry

Sir Cosine, who measures angles for a living, has a clean way to see why the forgery works. Imagine the embedding space split, informally, into two neighbourhoods. Question-space is where short, interrogative, symptom-first phrasings cluster — "why is it sour?" lives here, and so does the lexical trap "sour candy", because it shares the same surface symptom words. Document-space is where explanatory, declarative, technical prose clusters — the gold passage on under-extraction lives here.

The raw question sits in question-space, and its nearest neighbour is the trap: same words, wrong meaning. That is the whole disaster. The hypothetical answer, by contrast, is written as a document — declarative, technical, in the archive's own register — so it lands in document-space, right next to the gold.

Nothing about the retriever changed. We simply moved the query from the neighbourhood where the trap wins to the neighbourhood where the answer lives, and the nearest neighbour flips — from impostor to gold. Change the query, not the retriever.

One map, two arguments

Two spaces was a picture; Sir Cosine now takes the chalk and writes the algebra under it, which is shorter than the picture. Ordinary dense retrieval scores a pair as \( s(q,d) = \langle f(q),\, f(d) \rangle \) — one encoder \( f \), applied to both sides. HyDE touches neither \( f \) nor the space: it changes the argument, \( s(\hat d, d) = \langle f(\hat d),\, f(d) \rangle \). The very map that embedded the shelves now embeds the draft, so the comparison stops being question-against-document and becomes document-against-document. That, and nothing more mystical, is why a forged answer is commensurable with real passages at all.

The averaging step is just as literal. The inner product is linear, so scoring with the mean of \( k \) draft vectors is identical to averaging the \( k \) per-draft similarities: $$ \big\langle e,\, f(d) \big\rangle = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i} \big\langle f(\hat d_i),\, f(d) \big\rangle. $$ No clever aggregation hides inside \( e \). A document is ranked by how well it agrees with the drafts on average — one vote each, all votes equal.

And the cosine's divisor \( \lVert e \rVert \) is the same constant for every candidate, so it cannot reorder anything; it measures only how far the drafts agree with each other. Hold those two facts together and the next beat's boundary arrives proved rather than asserted: a mean cancels whatever points in random directions and preserves whatever all the drafts share — while the one quantity that would betray a shared lean drops straight out of the ranking.

What averaging can and cannot fix

Why draft \( k \) documents and average, rather than trust one? Because any single draft carries independent noise — an odd word choice, a tangent, one hallucinated detail unique to that sample. Average \( k \) drafts and these idiosyncrasies, pointing in random directions, partly cancel; the vector \( e = \frac{1}{k}\sum_i f(d_i) \) converges toward the drafts' shared, stable core. This is plain variance reduction: independent errors shrink like \( 1/\sqrt{k} \).

But averaging is powerless against a systematic bias. If the generator is confidently wrong about the domain — it thinks your espresso question is about a cocktail, and every one of the \( k \) drafts leans that way — then the shared error is not noise, it is signal in the wrong direction. Averaging does not cancel it; it amplifies it, sharpening the vector's aim at the wrong neighbourhood.

This is why the optional \( + f(q) \) anchor earns its keep. Folding the literal question back into the sum tethers \( e \) to what the user actually asked, so a confidently-drifting draft cannot drag the query all the way into a foreign topic. Averaging fights jitter; the anchor fights drift. You need both, because they fail in different directions.

Where the forgery fails

A forged answer is only as safe as the model that forged it, and two boundaries mark where HyDE stops helping and starts hurting. The first is hallucination drift. If the quill drafts a hypothetical answer in the wrong domain — plausible-sounding but about the wrong subject — then its embedding lands in a confidently-wrong neighbourhood, and cosine search dutifully retrieves passages that are wrong in exactly the same direction. A bad hypothesis does not merely fail to help; it steers you toward wrong answers that look consistent, which is worse than a random miss.

The second is a matter of ceiling, not correctness. HyDE is a zero-shot, low-resource move — its whole appeal is that you get a lift without labelled query-passage pairs. But when you do have those labels and can train a strong fine-tuned dense retriever, that retriever has already learned to close the vocabulary gap directly, and HyDE tends to underperform it. It also drifts on entity-centric queries — a question hinging on one specific name, date, or code — because the model may confidently forge the wrong entity, and no amount of averaging rescues a wrong proper noun.

So the honest framing: HyDE shines in the cold-start, no-labels, unfamiliar-corpus regime. It is a bridge you build when you have no fine-tuning budget — not a replacement for a retriever that has actually been taught your domain.

HyDE's sparse twin

HyDE lives in dense-embedding space, but its idea has a sparse twin for the world of BM25 and inverted indexes: Query2doc. The recipe rhymes — prompt the LLM to write a short pseudo-document that answers the query — but instead of embedding it, you concatenate the pseudo-doc onto the original query and hand the whole string to a keyword retriever.

Why does bolting a made-up paragraph onto the query help a lexical engine? Because BM25 can only match words it can see, and the raw query simply lacks the archive's words — it never says under-extraction. The pseudo-doc supplies those missing terms, so the expanded query now overlaps the gold passage on the very vocabulary that used to be absent. It is the same vocabulary-gap cure, delivered through term overlap rather than through geometry.

And it inherits the same shape of trade-off. The biggest lift comes exactly where the base retriever is weak and label-poor — sparse or zero-shot settings — while against a strong, fine-tuned dense retriever the gain shrinks to a sliver, because that model had already learned the missing words on its own. Cheap magic where you're poor; near-nothing where you're already rich.

Replace or append

Put the two moves side by side, because one draft \( \hat d \) serves both and the LLM call is paid for once. HyDE substitutes the encoder's input: it embeds the draft, \( e = f(\hat d) \), and the query's terms never reach the index at all. Query2doc substitutes the query string: \( q' = q \Vert \hat d \), so the query's terms stay exactly where they were and the draft's terms simply join them. Replace on the dense side; append on the sparse side.

Read past the surface and both branches are answering one and the same question — do you keep a thread back to what the captain actually asked? In dense space that thread is a summand you may add or omit: the optional \( + f(q) \) anchor from the averaging beat. In sparse space the thread is the construction, because a concatenation cannot drop the original terms — they are half of the string you handed the retriever.

So the difference between their failure modes is structural, not a matter of taste. HyDE's anchor is optional, and that is exactly why drift is HyDE's characteristic boundary rather than Query2doc's: leave the anchor out and nothing in the retrieval score still remembers the question. Choose the substitution knowing which of the two you are choosing — an input swap that is able to forget, or a string extension that is not.

Expand the query, or expand the document

Once you accept that the fix is more of the right words, HyDE and Query2doc are just two points on a wider family called query expansion. The lightest version skips writing a whole answer: simply prompt the LLM for a handful of expansion terms — synonyms, near-synonyms, the technical names a domain expert would reach for — and append them to the query. Ask for the words that surround sour, watery espresso, and the model hands you under-extraction, channeling, grind size — precisely the archive's vocabulary, without drafting a full pseudo-document.

But there is a mirror image of this whole idea, and it is worth staring at until it clicks. Every technique so far expands the query at query time — one extra LLM call per search, paid on every request, forever. Flip it: what if you expanded the documents instead, once, at index time? That is document expansion (Doc2Query) — for each passage in the archive, have a model generate the questions that passage would answer, and store those questions alongside the passage. Now the user's plain question matches the pre-generated question directly, and the vocabulary gap was closed before the query ever arrived.

The two are dual solutions to one problem: the query and the document speak different dialects, so someone must translate. Query expansion translates the question toward the documents, live, paying per query. Document expansion translates the documents toward likely questions, once, at build time. Same gap, opposite ends — pick your side by where you can afford to spend: latency at query time, or compute at index time.

Climb to retrieve, descend to answer

The vocabulary gap was about the wrong words. The second gap is subtler: the words are fine, but the query is pitched too low. A student asks the archive an ultra-specific question — a precise dose, a basket size, a shot time to the second — and the shelves stay silent. No passage was written at that altitude. The archive keeps principles, not case files.

The quill's move here is to abstract upward. Before retrieving, ask the generic question that sits behind the specific one: not "why did this exact shot go wrong?" but "what governs espresso extraction and sourness?" Retrieve against the principle, then reason back down to the case. Step-back prompting: climb to retrieve, descend to answer.

Notice the shape of it. The concrete details that made the raw query fail — the ones no passage shares — become reasoning inputs at the end, not retrieval keys at the start. You do not throw the specifics away; you park them until the principle is in hand.

What the climb does to the region

Write the over-specific query as a conjunction: a topic \( t \) — the principle it is really about — plus the case's constraints \( c_1, \dots, c_m \), the dose, the basket, the shot time. Retrieval can only answer from the region those conditions cut out, $$ R(q) = \{\, d \in D : d \models t \wedge c_1 \wedge \dots \wedge c_m \,\}, $$ where \( d \models \cdot \) reads "the document covers it". Over-specific means that region is empty: no chunk covers the whole conjunction at once, so there is nothing to rank in the first place.

Step-back drops conjuncts. Retrieve against \( q^{\uparrow} = t \) alone and the region becomes \( R(q^{\uparrow}) = \{\, d \in D : d \models t \,\} \supseteq R(q) \) — an inclusion, not a trade. Removing conditions can only widen; abstraction is monotone. That is the precise sense in which the climb is safe for recall: it cannot lose a document that the raw query would have found.

The descent, then, is not decoration but bookkeeping. The constraints were never thrown away — they migrate from the retrieval condition to the answer condition, applied by the reader to the principle it now holds. And both of the act's failure modes read straight off the inclusion: drop more conjuncts and \( R(q^{\uparrow}) \) keeps widening, holding more and more irrelevance; have no conjuncts to drop and \( q^{\uparrow} = q \), so the region does not move and the call bought nothing.

The generic form lands on the principle

Walk the toy. The over-specific query carries three numbers and a device — an exact dose, a basket size, a shot time. The principle passage in the archive never mentions any of them; it speaks of under-extraction and channeling in the abstract. Lexically the two are far apart: the query's surface has almost nothing to grab onto, so the Lexical Gremlin waves it past the very passage that would answer it.

Now abstract the query up: "what governs espresso extraction and sourness?" The specific numbers are gone, and what remains is exactly the vocabulary the principle passage is built from. The generic form lands right on it — the same page that the raw query could not reach is now the top result.

The lesson lives in the gap between the two queries. Same intent, same archive; the only thing that changed was the altitude we retrieved from. Climb up to meet the principle where it was written; keep the numbers in your pocket for the descent.

Step-back wins twice — and that is a measurement trap

Step-back has a second effect, and published measurements show it: on tasks where there is no retrieval at all, asking the general question first still improves the final answer. Nothing was retrieved — so the gain cannot be about having found the principle. It is about the principle having been said out loud, landing in the reader's context before the model started reasoning about the particular case.

Good news for the technique, bad news for your ruler. Inside a RAG pipeline step-back changes two things at once: the string that goes to the retriever, and the material the reader sees. So a measured lift is the sum of two effects, and the workshop discipline — hold everything fixed except the query — quietly stops holding, because the query is not the only thing that moved.

Separate them the cheap way. Score the retrieval stage on its own, with no reader involved: did the gold rise in the list, and how high — the same two rulers you used in L16, aimed at the result list instead of the answer. Then freeze the retrieved set and vary only what is handed to the reader. If the lift lives in the retrieval column, step-back closed your specificity gap. If it lives in the reading column, your gap was not specificity at all: you bought a better prompt and paid for a rewrite.

Up is not sideways

It is easy to blur two moves that both "rewrite for retrieval," so pin the difference. Step-back goes up: one query becomes more generic, trading specifics for the principle beneath them. Decomposition — the next section's tool — goes sideways: one query becomes several more atomic sub-questions, each retrieving its own fact. Abstraction and decomposition point in different directions.

Both cuts have a failure mode when you overshoot. Climb too high and you commit over-abstraction: the query becomes so generic it drops the very constraint you must eventually satisfy — you retrieve a fine essay on extraction that never touches sourness, and the descent has nothing to land on.

The mirror error is climbing when there was nothing to climb. If the query is already generic — "what causes sour espresso?" — a step-back call abstracts a principle into a vaguer principle, buys no new passage, and bills you for an LLM round-trip anyway. The diagnosis comes first: is this query too specific, or already broad? Only the first case wants the ladder.

One question, many angles

A single phrasing is a single bet. The Lexical Gremlin only lets through words that already sit on his shelves, so if your one query happens to miss the archive's vocabulary, you lose — even when the right passage is right there. The coverage move is to stop betting on one phrasing.

Ask the quill to paraphrase the question into several perspectives: a formal version, a colloquial version, one that leans on the mechanism, one that leans on the symptom. Retrieve for each, then take the union of what they surface. Where a single phrasing had one narrow doorway into the archive, several phrasings open several — and a passage only one of them can reach still makes it into the pool.

But a plain union has a defect: it is a set, and a set has no order. Merge three ranked lists into one bag and you have thrown away exactly the signal you paid to retrieve — where each passage sat. Coverage bought you recall and cost you the ranking. The next beat buys the ranking back.

Consensus beats a single favourite

The union needs an order, and we already own the right tool — the same Reciprocal Rank Fusion from Lecture 3, borrowed for a new job. Each paraphrase returns a ranked list; RRF scores a document by summing a small reward for each list it appears in, weighted by how high it appears:

$$ s(d) = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{k + r_i(d)}, \qquad k = 60. $$

Here \( r_i(d) \) is the rank of document \( d \) in the \( i \)-th list, and \( k = 60 \) is Cormack's constant. Read the sum aloud: a document that several phrasings agree on — appearing in list after list, even at middling rank — accumulates reward from each and climbs. A document that a single phrasing loves at rank 1 but no other phrasing sees gets one term and stalls. Consensus across angles beats one phrasing's favourite.

Multi-query paraphrasing plus RRF fusion is exactly the recipe called RAG-Fusion. And the whole move rests on one condition from the previous beat: the paraphrases must genuinely differ. Fuse three phrasings that all miss the archive's word the same way and consensus just re-confirms the same blind spot.

Why rank, not score

Why fuse on rank and not on the raw retrieval scores? Adding the similarity scores directly is tempting, and here it is not even a unit mismatch — every paraphrase is scored by the same encoder, so the cosines share a scale. The catch is subtler: their per-query distributions still drift. One phrasing packs its cosines into a tight band near the ceiling, another spreads them wide, and a raw sum quietly lets whichever paraphrase outputs bigger magnitudes dominate the fusion — a calibration accident, not a relevance signal. (The blunt incomparable-scales problem — a BM25 score against a cosine — is the L3 BM25-vs-dense hybrid, not this same-retriever case.)

Rank fusion sidesteps the whole problem by throwing the magnitudes away and keeping only position. \( 1/(k + r) \) reads a document's rank, never its score, so every list contributes on the same ruler: first place is first place whether the encoder was confident or lukewarm. The scales can no longer fight.

The constant \(k\) controls how quickly contributions fall with rank. In \(1/(k+r)\), a one-position change matters more near the head than in the tail. A larger \(k\) flattens that curve. The commonly used \(k=60\) is a starting setting to validate on the target workload. A bounded contribution does not guarantee majority robustness: one misleading list can still change the winner, depending on the ranks and which lists contain each document.

Paraphrase diversity can improve coverage, but neither more rewrites nor more varied wording guarantees better ranking. Evaluate the query-generation policy together with fusion. One LLM call can return several rewrites, followed by one retrieval per rewrite; independent generation calls are an optional implementation. Account for output tokens and retrieval work separately from the number of LLM calls.

What bounded contribution guarantees

Score fusion sums \(s_\sigma(d)=\sum_i w_i\sigma_i(d)\). Its bounds depend on the scorer and weights: cosine itself is bounded, whereas an unnormalised score need not share that range. Even with one cosine encoder, query-specific score distributions can differ. Rank fusion removes dependence on score magnitudes.

For nonnegative \(k\), every present rank contributes \(1/(k+r_i(d))\le1/(k+1)\); a document missing from a truncated list contributes zero. This caps each list's absolute contribution, but it does not guarantee that a majority of lists chooses the final winner. A single list can still change the result when its rank differences outweigh the differences in the other lists.

Moving a document one rank changes its contribution by $$\frac{1}{k+r}-\frac{1}{k+r+1}=\frac{1}{(k+r)(k+r+1)}.$$ This sensitivity decreases with depth. Bounded contributions and decreasing sensitivity are mathematical properties of RRF; robustness to misleading rewrites is an empirical property to test, not a theorem following from the cap.

Agreement is not yet truth

RRF rewards agreement, and it is worth asking what agreement is evidence of. The classical argument for merging votes rests on the independence of the voters: if three lists, each wrong in its own way, nevertheless converge on one document, that coincidence can hardly be chance, so it is informative. But paraphrases are not independent voters. They came out of one model, reading one query, in one prompt — their errors are correlated by construction.

The failure follows immediately. If the model misread the sense of the question, that misreading is common to every formulation: each one retrieves the same wrong neighbourhood, and their agreement crowns it. Meanwhile the single formulation that did land on the archive's word contributes one term and loses to the chorus. The property the previous beat praised — consensus beats one list's affection — is here the very same property, read from the other side.

Two practical consequences follow, and neither of them is use fewer paraphrases. First: diversity is not decoration, it is the premise the merge rule quietly leans on, and paraphrases must differ in how they read the question, not only in wording. Second: RRF cannot demote a bad list, it can only bound that list's contribution from above. Nothing in the rule inspects quality, because the rule itself discarded the only evidence — the scores — by which quality could have been suspected. If you know a branch is unreliable, say so with a per-list weight or drop the branch; do not expect the formula to do it for you.

One question wearing many masks

Some questions only look like one question. "Which of my two espresso machines pulls a hotter shot, and does the hotter one also grind finer?" asks about temperature, about grind, and about a comparison — three facts that almost never share a chunk. The archive stored the temperature spec on one page, the grind spec on another, the review that compares them on a third. No single retrieval, however clever the rewrite, can pull all three at once, because the evidence is scattered by design. This is the compositionality gap — a callback to L15, where we first watched a retriever succeed on each atomic fact yet fail on their conjunction.

The quill's move here is not to reshape one query but to fan it out. Read the compound question, cut it along its natural seams into atomic sub-questions — one fact apiece — and send each to the retriever on its own. Where one shot found nothing coherent, several narrow shots each land squarely in the chunk that holds their answer.

Then you compose: gather the sub-answers and let the reader assemble the whole. Fan out, answer each, fold back together. The gap closes not because retrieval got smarter, but because we stopped asking it to find in one place what the archive scattered across three.

Moving the conjunction outside the quantifier

The fan-out has a one-line statement, and it is worth having, because it makes the diagnosis checkable. The joint query asks the archive for a single document satisfying all the sub-questions at once, \( \exists\, d \in D \quad \bigwedge_{j} A_j(d) \), where \( A_j(d) \) reads "document \( d \) answers sub-question \( j \)". Decomposition moves the conjunction outside the quantifier and asks instead that each sub-question have some document of its own, \( \bigwedge_{j} \exists\, d_j \in D \quad A_j(d_j) \).

The entailment runs one way only. A single document covering everything certainly gives each sub-question a document, so the first condition implies the second; the converse fails exactly when the archive scattered the evidence — which is what chunking did on purpose. So decomposition does not make the retriever sharper. It weakens the condition the retriever has to meet, and a strictly weaker condition is satisfiable where the stronger one is empty. The evidence was always in the corpus; what changed is the demand placed on it.

That one-way gap is also the invoice. Where the joint query wanted one document, you now hold \( n \) of them, and something further down the pipeline has to put the pieces back together — the reading step, whose per-hop reliability the next-but-one beat will charge you for. And the corollary is a diagnosis you can genuinely run: when the query carries a single sub-question the two sides coincide, there is nothing left to weaken, and every extra hop is pure risk.

Three flavors, not three equals

"Decompose the query" hides three genuinely different moves, and the honest way to keep them apart is by abstraction level — how the breaking-apart happens, not just that it happens. Treating them as interchangeable is the mistake; each answers a different question about the fan-out.

Least-to-most is ORDERING. Solve the simplest sub-problem first, then feed its answer forward into the next, harder one, so each step stands on the resolved ground beneath it (Zhou). The insight is sequence: some sub-questions can only be asked once an earlier one is answered.

Self-ask is ELICITATION. The model asks itself the follow-up questions out loud, answers each, and lets those intermediate answers surface the missing link before it commits (Press). The insight is that the decomposition need not be planned up front — the model can elicit the next hop from its own partial reasoning.

Decomposed Prompting is ARCHITECTURE. Here the sub-tasks become a modular library of reusable programs — a decomposer that dispatches to sub-task handlers, each independently improvable, some recursive (Khot). The insight is engineering: turn a one-off prompt trick into composable, swappable parts. Ordering, elicitation, architecture — the same fan-out seen from three heights.

The tax on every hop

Fanning out is not free, and the cost is not just latency — it is fragility. A decomposed answer is only correct if every sub-answer is correct, and it is built on top of the ones before it. If each of \( n \) sub-answers succeeds independently with probability \( p \), the composed answer succeeds with $$ p^{n}. $$ A chain is exactly as strong as the product of its links, and a product of numbers below one shrinks with every factor.

Read that exponent as a warning, not a formula. Even hops that each land more often than not can compound into a coin-flip once you string enough of them together — a single wrong hop propagates, dragging every downstream step off the true trail. Depth is where decomposition earns its keep and where it quietly bleeds it back.

So the diagnosis is a wager. Decompose only when the multi-fact gain — evidence you could never have retrieved in one shot — outweighs the exponential decay of stringing hops together. And the corollary is sharp: a single-fact query should never be decomposed. Splitting one atomic question into pieces adds hops, adds LLM calls, adds \( p^{n} \) decay, and buys nothing back. Fan out for scattered evidence; keep it in one shot when the answer already lives in one chunk.

A self-ask, traced

Watch the quill think out loud on a two-hop question: "Was the barista who won the 2019 world title trained on the same machine I own?" The model does not lunge at an answer. It first asks itself a gate question — "Are follow-up questions needed here?" — and answers yes, because two facts must meet before this can be settled.

So it opens the first follow-up: "Who won the 2019 world barista title?" It retrieves, reads, and writes down the intermediate answer — a name. Only with that name in hand can the next hop even be phrased: "What machine was that barista trained on?" A fresh retrieval, a fresh intermediate answer — a model. Each hop is a small, self-contained self-ask, and each depends on the answer that came before it.

When the model asks itself the gate question once more and finally answers "no more follow-ups needed," it composes: it sets the trained-on machine beside the one I own and commits to the final answer. This is the HotpotQA-style multi-hop pattern — ask whether you need to ask, answer each intermediate question in turn, and only then commit. The reasoning trace is not decoration; it is the scaffold that keeps a scattered answer from collapsing.

The quill that learns

Everything so far leaned on a frozen quill: a prompted rewriter that drafts a hypothetical answer, steps back, fans out, decomposes — but never remembers whether the rewrite actually helped. It is generic by construction. Change the archive, change the retriever, and the same generic prompt keeps drafting the same generic rewrites. So the natural question, the pivot from enchantment to apprenticeship, is simple: can the quill learn?

RRR — Rewrite-Retrieve-Read (Ma) — answers yes. Put a small language model in front of the pipeline as the rewriter, run the full loop, and let the downstream answer be the teacher. Did the rewritten query lead to a passage that produced a correct answer? Reward it. Did it retrieve noise? Punish it. Train the rewriter by reinforcement learning on that final reward, not on any hand-labelled 'good rewrite'.

This is the quill sharpening its own nib. The prompted rewriter is a tool a smith hands you; the trained rewriter is a tool that files itself against the one archive and the one reader it serves — until its edge matches the wood it has to cut.

How the reward reaches the weights

"Train it by reinforcement learning" hides the one identity that makes the whole arrangement possible. Let \( \pi_\theta(\hat q \mid q) \) be the rewriter's policy — a distribution over rewrites — and \( R(\hat q) \) the reward the finished answer earned. The gradient of the expected reward is $$ \nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\hat q \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\, R(\hat q) \,\big] = \mathbb{E}_{\hat q \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\, R(\hat q)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\hat q \mid q) \,\big]. $$ Sample rewrites, weight each one's log-gradient by the reward it earned, take a step.

Now look at where the derivative lives. It differentiates the probability of the text that was written — never the retriever, never the reader. The reward enters as a scalar multiplier, so \( R(\hat q) = R(\mathrm{read}(\mathrm{retrieve}(\hat q))) \) may run through an inverted index, a hosted API, anything at all non-differentiable. Freezing the rest of the pipeline is therefore not modesty but a consequence: those boxes never needed opening.

One practical rider and one warning. The rider: subtracting a reference value from the reward leaves the estimator unbiased, because the expectation of \( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta \) is zero, while its spread falls — which is why baselines are standard practice rather than a trick. The warning has a name. The learned signal is whatever \( R \) measures, and \( R \) merely stands in for "the captain was served well"; hone a quill against a proxy long enough and Goodhart comes to collect. The trained rewriter will write toward the reward, not toward the intention behind it.

Every technique is a tax

There is no free rewrite. Each move on the shelf buys you recall with a currency of its own, and a good Artificer reads the bill before reaching for the tool. Three prices sit along one frontier.

Classical PRF — pseudo-relevance feedback like RM3 — is free: no extra model, no training, just a second pass over your own index. But it is weak, and we already watched it hit a ceiling, unable to invent the word the archive uses. A prompted LLM — HyDE, step-back, multi-query, decomposition — is flexible: it carries world knowledge and can conjure the missing vocabulary. Its tax is latency and money, paid every single query, plus a new surface where things can go wrong. A trained small rewriter — RRR — is cheap at inference: once the small model has learned, each rewrite is nearly free again. Its tax is paid up front, in training data and the reward loop that shapes it.

So the frontier is not 'which technique is best' but 'which price can you afford for the gap you actually have'. Pay for the gap in front of you — not for the one in the paper.

Reading the ruler

Before you drive the workshop's widget, know the procedure it runs, because the discipline is the whole point. Hold everything but the query still: the same corpus \( D \), the same gold passage \( g \), the same retriever, the same depth. Only then does a lift belong to the rewrite rather than to some quiet change in the index. Attribution is not a nicety here — it is the only reason the comparison means anything at all.

Run technique A, take back its ranked list and find where \( g \) sits in it: that position is \( \mathrm{rank}_A \), and its reciprocal is the reciprocal rank, \( \mathrm{RR}_A = 1/\mathrm{rank}_A \). The two questions divide cleanly — recall asks did the gold make the cut at all, reciprocal rank asks how high. Repeat for B, take \( \Delta = \mathrm{RR}_B - \mathrm{RR}_A \), and keep a call meter in the next column over, because a technique's quality is never readable without its price.

Then comes the step everyone skips. Reciprocal rank is a per-query quantity and it jumps about: one query's \( \Delta \) is an anecdote rather than a finding, and the size of a single \( \Delta \) says almost nothing. What you may claim is its sign, holding across a set of queries — which is exactly why the honest way to report a toy is the share of runs on which an ordering holds, and never the magnitude of one lucky gap.

How to read the bill

The frontier compared families; now learn to count a concrete pipeline, because one LLM call hides three different growth laws. Some techniques have a constant price: HyDE, step-back, Query2doc — one draft, one call, however big the query. Multi-query costs one call for the paraphrases and then one retrieval per paraphrase: the generation bill does not grow, the retrieval bill grows with \(N\). Decomposition costs a call for the plan plus a call and a retrieval per hop — and if the hops are discovered as you go, you do not even know their number when you start.

Money and latency are two different columns, and they add up differently. Money is a sum: every call is paid for, whether or not the calls ran in parallel. Latency is the longest path: fire the \(N\) paraphrase searches at once and you wait for the slowest, not for all of them in turn. That is why multi-query is affordable where decomposition already is not — the hops of a chain are sequential by definition, each needing the previous answer, so their delays add. One word, cost, and two completely different laws.

And two lines everybody forgets to write into the bill. First: everything downstream is charged per candidate, so a rewrite that inflates the candidate list also multiplies the reranker's work and the length of the generator's context. Second: a technique that fails silently is billed exactly the same — a hallucinated draft costs the same call as a good one. So read the bill in the same breath as the lift: an honest report on any technique in this chapter is always a pair — how far the gold rose, and what the counter showed.

Raw query: the lexical trap d4 outranks the gold d1 on shared surface words — gold sits at rank 4. No LLM call, but no answer either.

RM3 folds back terms from the top passages: it gains 'channeling' (gold → rank 3) but drifts toward the trap's words and can't invent 'under-extraction'. Free, but capped.

HyDE embeds a hypothetical answer written in the documents' vocabulary — gold jumps to rank 1. It costs one LLM call.

The trade-off: RR climbs raw → RM3 → HyDE, but so does cost. Rewriting is high-ROI, not free — pay for the gap you actually have.

Diagnose the gap, then pick the move

The whole chapter collapses into one habit: diagnose before you reach. Every failed retrieval is a kind of failure, and the kind tells you the cure. Guess the tool first and you will pay for a gap you never had.

So run the checklist. Does the query use everyday words while the archive speaks jargon — a vocabulary gap? Reach for HyDE or expansion: draft the answer in the archive's dialect, or grow the query toward it. Is the query too specific to match any principle passage — a specificity gap? Step back to the generic question, retrieve the principle, reason down. Is one phrasing too narrow, brittle to a single word choice? Fan into a multi-query and fuse the ranks with RRF; consensus across phrasings beats a single top hit. Does the question braid several facts that live in different chunks — a compositionality gap? Decompose it into hops.

And when you own labelled data and can spare a training budget, promote the habit into a model: train a rewriter with RRR, so the diagnosis stops being a prompt you write and becomes a reflex the small model has learned. The tree is not a menu to sample from — it is a diagnosis to make.

From observation to corollary

Look back and notice what changed along the way. Every act of this chapter named a boundary — PRF cannot invent, HyDE drifts, step-back over-abstracts, rank fusion is robust, decomposition is billed per hop, a trained rewriter chases its reward — and every one of them arrived first as an observation, something practitioners kept noticing. Each has since been re-derived as a corollary: of a support inclusion, of the linearity of an inner product, of the monotonicity of dropping conjuncts, of a bounded decreasing map, of a one-way entailment, of the score-function identity.

That upgrade buys more than tidiness. An observed boundary can only be re-measured; a derived one can be predicted — before spending a single call you already know that turning the trust dial up will not rescue PRF, that more drafts will not cure a confident misreading, that splitting a single-fact question cannot help. And it tells you what to measure: not whether a technique works, but whether its precondition holds on your archive. That is the difference between owning a shelf of tricks and owning a craft.

The honest small print

Two honesties, before you carry any of this out the door. The first is about the tool: every rewrite is another LLM call. Latency, dollars, and a fresh surface where a confident hallucination can steer retrieval into a wrong-domain neighbourhood — none of that vanishes because the recall number went up. Rewriting is high-ROI, but it is never free.

The second is about this chapter's toy. The espresso example is constructed — hand-built so the lexical trap outranks the gold and so the vocabulary-gap cure visibly wins. It faithfully reproduces the ordering of the failure and its repair; it does not reproduce real-world magnitudes. Read the widgets for shape, not for numbers you would quote in a paper.

And keep one eye on Goodhart, who never left the room. Optimize a rewriter purely for recall and you may reward it for being verbose and hallucinated — stuffing the query with plausible-sounding terms that happen to drag in more documents. The metric climbs; the answer rots. Measure what you actually want, or you will get exactly what you measured.

One sentence to keep

Step back from every technique and one shape remains. HyDE, step-back, multi-query, decomposition, RRR — none of them touched the index, the embeddings, or the retriever's weights. Every single one reshaped the query. The archive was always able to answer; the question was simply not yet phrased in a way it could hear.

And each move closes exactly one of three gaps. Vocabulary — the user's words are not the archive's words. Specificity — the query sits at the wrong altitude, too fine or too coarse for the passage that holds the answer. Compositionality — the question braids facts that no single passage carries. (Multi-query is the hedge: when you cannot name which gap you face, fanning into several phrasings and fusing with RRF covers your bet on all three.)

So carry one sentence out of this chapter, and let it survive when the paper titles have faded: change the query, not the retriever.

The captain picks up the Quill

Séréga sets the Quill down on the workbench. It is not magic anymore — it is a well-understood instrument with a known bill and a clear diagnosis for when to draw it. The enchantment was only ever apprenticeship, finished. The captain steps up and picks it up.

And notice what has not changed. The rewritten query still flows into the same retriever and reranker you built two chapters back — the rewrite is a better question handed to the very same machine. The answer it eventually produces is still graded by the same RAG metrics — faithfulness, context precision, recall — that L16 taught you to trust. The Quill did not replace the pipeline; it fed it a query the archive could finally answer.

That is the whole craft of the Artificer, and the last line to carry: the best question is the one the archive can answer. Write that question, and the rest of the machine does what it was always able to do. And then turn around: in L18 the question is already fine, and the damage is on the other side of the desk — in how the documents were cut before anyone asked anything.
