First Contact

I'm Séréga, and I've got a problem. There's an alien on my screen, and it won't stop talking. The translator spits out symbols. Are two of its phrases saying the same thing, or completely different things? I genuinely cannot tell.

Here's the thing about meaning: a machine never gets to touch it directly. It only ever sees strings — sequences of characters with no built-in sense of which are alike. And strings are stubborn. A grey pixel halfway between black and white is a perfectly real, meaningful colour; you can interpolate it. But there is no word halfway between cat and dog. Symbols are discrete — they share no inherent ruler. "Cat" and "car" differ by one letter and mean wildly different things; "couch" and "sofa" share no letters and mean the same thing. You cannot read closeness off the spelling.

So the mission splits into two jobs, and they have to happen in that order. First, turn the alien's text into something countable — numbers a machine can hold. Then, on top of those numbers, manufacture a notion of distance so the machine can finally judge: are these two meanings close, or far? Get Data, then Measure. Buckle up — we're going to build both halves from scratch.

Raw text is not computable

First snag: a computer can't do anything with raw letters. It needs numbers. The lazy fix is to build a dictionary — give every whole word its own integer ID — and feed those IDs to the model. It works right up until the alien says a word we never logged. One unseen word and the whole pipeline shrugs: out of vocabulary. The model has no slot for it, so it collapses everything it hasn't memorised into a single useless [UNK] token. Names, typos, hashtags, code identifiers, the alien's whole language — all flattened to the same blank.

You might hope: just make the dictionary bigger. Read more text, keep adding words, surely it saturates? It does not. Two empirical laws say so, and we'll meet them as a figure in a moment. Zipf's law says word frequency falls off as roughly one-over-rank: on a real 3.7-million-token corpus, the top 10 words alone cover 20.6% of everything, the top 100 cover 47.3%, the top 1000 cover 71.4% — and then a colossal tail of words seen exactly once. Heaps' law says the number of distinct word-types keeps growing as you read, like \(V \approx K\,N^{\beta}\) with \(\beta \approx 0.59\) — sublinear but unbounded. After 3.66 million tokens we'd already counted 94,287 distinct types and the curve was still climbing.

Put those two facts together and the verdict is non-negotiable: no fixed list of whole words can ever cover real language. New words arrive forever. So we stop trying to enumerate words — and start breaking them into pieces small enough to never run out.

The head is cheap; the tail is the design

Those two laws are usually met as two graphs. Read them together and they are a budget. Zipf describes the running text: a handful of types account for most of the tokens you will ever process. Heaps describes the inventory: the count of distinct types keeps climbing for as long as you keep reading. Lay one against the other and the arithmetic of any fixed vocabulary falls out. The first slots you spend are absurdly profitable — a few entries buy you most of the stream. Every slot after that buys less than the one before it, the curve of diminishing returns flattens without ever touching the floor, and Heaps guarantees the next word you have never seen is still on its way.

So the interesting decision was never how large the vocabulary should be. Every size is wrong in the same way; the sizes only differ in how quickly they become wrong. The decision is what happens to the words the vocabulary does not have. A list of whole words has exactly one answer — nothing happens, they collapse into a single dead symbol — and that is the whole argument for units which compose. If the entries can be glued back together, the tail no longer has to be enumerated; it only has to be spellable. The head keeps its cheap one-token treatment, because a frequent word survives the merges intact, and the tail degrades gracefully into pieces instead of falling off a cliff.

The same head-and-tail shape turns up all over search, which is why it is worth naming once and keeping. In a lexical index the head means a few posting lists are enormous, so systems prune them or handle them specially; the tail means most terms are seen once or twice, and that is exactly where recall goes to die. In query logs the head is what everybody types and the tail is what nobody repeats — and the tail, added up, is most of the traffic. Train the reflex now: whenever a distribution has this shape, the average describes nobody, and the engineering lives at the edges.

"Split on spaces" is parochial

There's a quieter reason whole-word lists fail, and it has nothing to do with running out of vocabulary — it's that the unit "a word" barely exists once you leave English. Take Turkish, an agglutinative language that glues meaning together: evlerinizden is a single written word — ev + ler + iniz + den, "from your houses". One word, four morphemes. A whole-word vocabulary would need a separate entry for every such combination — and Turkish, Finnish, and Hungarian generate them by the millions. The same blow-up Furnas et al. (1987) warned about, now multiplied by morphology.

And the very assumption that words are delimited by spaces is an English-centric one. There are roughly 7000 languages and many scripts: Arabic and Hebrew run right-to-left; Chinese writes 今天我很高兴 ("I am happy today") with no spaces at all, so where one word ends and the next begins is itself a learned, ambiguous decision; Thai has no spaces either; Japanese mixes kanji, hiragana and katakana in one breath. Even English leaks — "New York" is two tokens for one place, "can't" is one token for two words, and URLs and #hashtags obey no rule a space could catch.

So "tokenize = split on whitespace" is a rule that only ever worked for a slice of one language family. We want a method that learns its units from the data itself and works on any script — no hand-written boundary rules, no per-language exception list. That is exactly what the sub-word tokenizers in this chapter do (and why SentencePiece, which we'll meet, treats the space as just another symbol).

One string, many meanings

Everything so far has been about not having a symbol. There is a second obstacle running underneath, and it has the opposite shape: one symbol, too many meanings — and it fires at every level of language at once. Lexical: bank is the edge of a river or the place that holds money; bat either flies or hits a ball. Syntactic: "I saw the man with the telescope" — who is holding the telescope? Two structures, one string, and the letters do not choose between them. Scope: "every student read a book" — one book between them, or one each? Referential: a pronoun points at something the sentence never spells out. The Alien is not only using words we do not have; it is using words that mean several things at once, exactly as we do.

The referential layer has the cleanest demonstration, and it is worth carrying around: the Winograd schema. "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big" — it is the trophy. Change one adjective — "…because it was too small" — and it is now the suitcase. Almost nothing about the string moved, and the referent flipped completely. No amount of normalising, stemming or clever cutting recovers that, because the deciding information was never in the symbols: you have to know that large objects do not fit inside small containers. That is a model of the world, not a property of the text.

So be honest about the division of labour in this chapter. Cutting and measuring solve the problem of form: they make the stream countable, and they let us ask whether two vectors point the same way. They do not touch ambiguity. A tokenizer hands bank one id whichever bank it is, and every measure built on top inherits that single answer, confidently. Ambiguity is not a corner case waiting for a patch — it is the ordinary condition of language, and the repair has a name we reach much later in the course: representations that depend on a token's neighbours and not only on the token. Book the debt now; the course settles it when it has the machinery.

Before you cut, you clean

Raw text doesn't go straight to the tokenizer. The real pipeline is normalize → pre-tokenize → tokenize → ids, and each box earns its place. "This is an example." eventually becomes a list of integer ids like [40134, 2052, 133, 389, 12] — but only after the first box quietly fixes problems you can't even see.

Normalization handles invisible differences that silently break matching. The character "é" has two legal Unicode encodings: NFC stores it as one code point U+00E9, while NFD stores it as plain e plus a combining accent U+0301. They render identically and compare unequal byte-for-byte — so "café" typed two ways fails to match itself. The Unicode normal forms (NFC/NFD compose-or-decompose; NFKC/NFKD also fold look-alikes, ﬁ→fi, full-width Ａ→A, ²→2) exist to collapse these. But normalization is a double-edged knife: lowercasing merges "US" the country with "us" the pronoun, and aggressive NFKC rewrites x²→x2, destroying the very signal a chemistry or math search needs. The rule of thumb: NFC plus minimal folding for a generation model (modern byte-level tokenizers like GPT-2/4's do almost no normalization), heavier NFKC + casefold only for a lexical search index, where recall on surface form beats precision.

Then there's the older trick of collapsing word forms to a root, and it comes in two flavours. Stemming is a crude, rule-based chopping of suffixes — the Porter stemmer turns studies → studi, caring → car — fast and language-light, but the output is a stem, not always a real word, and it over-merges (universe, university → univers). Lemmatization maps a word to its dictionary lemma using a vocabulary and morphology, often with the part of speech: studies → study, drove → drive, better → good, and saw → see (verb) or saw (noun). Both shrink open / opened / opens / opening to one form so a query matches every variant — the classic fix for the lexical gap the Gremlin guarded in the last chapter. Crucially: sparse retrieval like BM25 still leans on stemming and lemmatization, while neural and sub-word models usually skip them and let the tokenizer plus learned embeddings absorb morphology. So this whole preprocessing pipeline lives mostly on the classical-IR side — but you have to know it's there, because it shapes recall.

The cut before the cut

Between normalization and the learned merges the pipeline hides a step that sounds like plumbing and behaves like a prior: pre-tokenization. Before any algorithm counts a single pair, something slices the raw string into spans and declares the rule of the game — merges may happen inside a span, never across one. In the byte-level family the pre-tokenizer is a regular expression, and what it does is unglamorous and decisive: it peels a leading space off and keeps it with the word that follows, keeps a run of letters together, keeps a run of digits together, and keeps a run of punctuation together.

That one expression quietly decides things the "learned from data" story would like to take credit for. Letters and digits sit in different spans, so no merge can ever glue a digit onto a word — the model will never receive a product code as a single unit, however often it appeared. The leading space attaches forward, so the at the start of a word and the in mid-sentence are two different entries, and the tokenizer has silently encoded the claim "a word begins here". And because a human wrote the expression, this is the one place where the parochial habit we spent a whole beat throwing out can walk back in through the front door.

Which is precisely why SentencePiece refuses to have one. No pre-tokenizer, whitespace demoted to an ordinary character written ▁, and Chinese or Thai stop being special cases that need their own rule. The general lesson is uncomfortable and useful: two tokenizers can share an algorithm, a corpus and a vocabulary size and still end up with different vocabularies, because the pre-tokenizer changed which pairs were ever allowed to be neighbours. When you compare cutters, compare their pre-tokenizers first — it is the variable everyone forgets to hold fixed.

Enter the Tokenosaurus

So we stop chasing whole words and chop them into sub-words. Meet the Tokenosaurus — a friendly beast that snips text into reusable chunks. Never seen the word internationalization? Fine — it still splits into pieces we have seen: inter + national + ization. The vocabulary stays finite, every input is representable, and [UNK] simply stops happening.

But how big should the chunks be? That's a genuine three-way tug-of-war — a trade-off triangle between vocabulary size, sequence length, and out-of-vocabulary risk, and you can only ever keep two corners happy. Go to the extreme of single characters (or raw bytes) and the vocabulary shrinks to about 256 symbols and OOV vanishes entirely — but a sentence balloons to four or five times as many tokens, which is brutal when attention costs grow with the square of the length. Go to the extreme of whole words and sequences are short — but the vocabulary is unbounded (that's Heaps again) and OOV is everywhere. Sub-words sit in the sweet interior: a bounded vocabulary of roughly 30k–200k pieces, moderate sequence length, near-zero OOV. That's why every modern tokenizer — GPT-2's 50,257, GPT-4's ~100k, BERT's 30,522, LLaMA's 32k — lives there.

The real magic is that we don't hand-pick the chunks. We let the data decide which pieces are worth keeping, by a beautifully simple loop. That loop is the next figure — watch the merges happen.

Two loops, not one

It is easy to read what you just watched as a single procedure. It is two, and they run at different times, on different inputs, at wildly different cost. Training reads the whole corpus, counts every adjacent pair, merges the most frequent one, writes that merge down as the next line of an ordered ledger, and repeats until the vocabulary hits its target size. Encoding counts nothing at all: it takes one word and replays the ledger from the top, applying each merge wherever it fits, and stops when the lines run out. Statistics live in the first loop only. The second loop is obedience.

The ledger's order is the entire model. Line one is the gluiest pair in whatever you trained on — usually a dull, enormously frequent pair of characters — and every later line operates on text already rewritten by the lines above it. That is why a merge table cannot be edited like a dictionary: appending a merge changes nothing before it and potentially everything after it, and pulling a line out of the middle invalidates the rest of the file. It is also why the opening lines are worth reading for their own sake — they are a compact confession of what the training corpus mostly consisted of, and a tokenizer that begins by learning the glue of one language will spend the rest of its budget elsewhere.

The asymmetry of cost is what makes the whole design shippable. Training is a batch job over a corpus, run once, by whoever builds the model. Encoding carries no statistics, no state and — for the merge-based family — no randomness: the same string yields the same ids on any machine, this year and next. That is why a tokenizer ships as a file sitting next to the weights rather than as a service you call. It is a deterministic function, and everything it needs to know is in the ledger.

A word has no "correct" split

Before we leave BPE, kill a myth that trips up everyone: a word does not have one correct tokenization. In principle a string can be cut many ways — linear could be li + near or li + n + ea + r; algebra could be al + ge + bra or al + g + e + bra. There is no fact of the matter inside the word. BPE makes the cut deterministic only by fixing the order of its learned merges and applying them greedily, top of the list first.

So the segmentation is a property of the trained merge table, not of the word. Watch two tokenizers disagree on singer. Merge table A learned {e+r→er, sin+g→sing} and produces [sing, er] — 2 tokens. Merge table B learned {s+i→si, n+g→ng} and produces [si, ng, e, r] — 4 tokens. Same word, two trained tokenizers, different segmentation, different token count, different model inputs. This is one concrete reason you can never mix a tokenizer from one model with the weights of another — the ids simply don't mean the same thing.

One last upgrade that makes BPE bullet-proof: run it on raw bytes instead of characters. There are only 256 possible bytes, so the base alphabet is tiny and fixed, every possible input — every emoji, every script, every corrupted blob — is representable, and [UNK] becomes literally impossible. That byte-level move (GPT-2, Radford et al. 2019, and every frontier model since) is why modern tokenizers also fold whitespace into tokens: a leading space is part of the byte stream, so the and Ġthe ("space-the") are simply different tokens. Hold that thought — it comes back to bite us in the consequences beat.

The id is an address

After the cutter comes the dullest step in the pipeline, and the one most worth understanding: numericalization — every token is handed an integer. It is tempting to read those integers as quantities. They are not. An id is a row index into the embedding matrix and nothing else. Id one is not "less" than id one thousand, the gap between two ids means nothing, and sorting them sorts nothing. All the meaning sits in the row the index points at. Renumber the whole vocabulary and, provided you permute the matrix rows the same way, the model is untouched — which is the cleanest proof that the number itself carries no information at all.

That turns the vocabulary into a contract, signed once, at the moment training begins. The tokenizer file and the weight file are two halves of one artifact: the tokenizer promises that this string always lands on this row, and the weights are trained under that promise. Break it in either direction and nothing crashes — you get fluent, plausible garbage. Feed a model ids produced by a different cutter and every lookup arrives at a stranger's row. Add entries to a trained vocabulary and you are appending rows whose vectors are still whatever the initializer left there. The Tokenosaurus does not only cut; it signs.

Two practical consequences drop straight out. First, vocabulary size is not a knob you tune late: it is a dimension of the embedding matrix and of the output layer, so it is priced into memory and into the softmax before the first training step is taken. A larger vocabulary buys shorter sequences and pays for them in parameters — the same trade-off triangle, now with an invoice attached. Second, "the tokenizer" is not a preprocessing detail you swap for a better one when a better one appears. Swapping it means retraining, and that is why an ecosystem of models drags its vocabularies along for years.

Where the token came from

An id is an address into a matrix, and for the model that is the whole story. For search it is not nearly enough, because search has to point back at the text: a snippet, a highlight, a quoted span in an answer. So a real tokenizer returns more than a list of ids. Alongside them it hands you an offset mapping — for every token, the span of characters in the original string that the token was cut from. It is the receipt for a lossy operation, and it is the only bridge left from what the model saw to what the user typed.

Two of those extra outputs earn their place immediately. The offsets are what turn a model's verdict into something a person can read: a reranker that likes positions eleven through fourteen has said nothing until the offsets translate that into a substring you can put on a results page — and the translation has to be exact, because normalization and pre-tokenization already rewrote the stream before the merges ever ran. The other is the attention mask: batching pads sequences to a common length, and the mask is what marks which positions are real text and which are filler. Get it wrong and padding stops being invisible — it becomes content the model dutifully attends to.

The general shape is worth carrying past this chapter. A tokenizer is a function whose inverse you are responsible for maintaining. Byte-level and SentencePiece cutters are built so that detokenizing recovers the input exactly, which is why they can be trusted round-trip; a classical analysis pipeline that lowercased, stripped accents and dropped punctuation is not invertible, and its offsets are the only thing that still connects an index term to the sentence it came from. That is the honest difference between the two cutters this course keeps side by side: the analyzer for a lexical index is allowed to destroy form, and the tokenizer in front of a model must always be able to point at it.

WordPiece: merge by surprise, not by count

BPE has a cousin that powers BERT: WordPiece, from Schuster & Nakajima (2012) (originally for Japanese/Korean voice search). The single conceptual difference is which pair it merges. BPE merges the most frequent adjacent pair. WordPiece instead merges the pair with the highest likelihood score $$\mathrm{score}(A,B) = \frac{\mathrm{freq}(AB)}{\mathrm{freq}(A)\cdot\mathrm{freq}(B)}.$$ Read that as pointwise mutual information without the log: dividing by the two marginal counts favours pairs that co-occur more than chance. A pair of two very common pieces (th + e) has a big numerator but an even bigger denominator, so it scores modestly; a rare piece that almost always follows one specific neighbour scores high. That's why WordPiece tends to keep meaningful morphemes together.

It also marks word-internal pieces with a ## continuation prefix: playing → play + ##ing, tokenization → token + ##ization. The bare first piece starts a word; every ## piece glues onto the previous one — which preserves word boundaries for tasks like named-entity recognition. To make the score reproduce on a toy corpus we follow the common variant that scales the numerator by the current vocabulary size $$\mathrm{score}(A,B) = \frac{\mathrm{freq}(AB)\cdot|V|}{\mathrm{freq}(A)\cdot \mathrm{freq}(B)},$$ where $|V|$ grows by one with each merge. On a tiny corpus where q, u, i, c each appear once (so every count is 1 and $|V|$ starts at 29), the first three merges score $$(1\cdot 29)/(1\cdot 1) = 29,\quad (1\cdot 30)/(1\cdot 1) = 30,\quad (1\cdot 31)/(1\cdot 1) = 31,$$ for (q,##u), then (qu,##i), then (qui,##c) — assembling quick one piece at a time.

Encoding uses greedy longest-match, left to right. Our toy's unseen quicker becomes quick ##e ##r because those pieces exist. WordPiece can emit the whole word as [UNK] if it cannot finish segmentation, even when individual characters have appeared before: initial and continuation pieces differ. Implementations can also impose a word-length limit. Google BERT defaults to max_input_chars_per_word=200. Byte-level BPE with a full byte alphabet avoids missing-character coverage, a separate issue from such limits.

Unigram & SentencePiece: prune, don't merge

The third family runs the loop backwards. Unigram LM, from Kudo (2018), starts from a deliberately large seed vocabulary, fits a unigram language model over it, and then iteratively prunes the tokens whose removal least hurts the corpus likelihood — trimming down to the target size. Where BPE and WordPiece build up (merge), Unigram builds down (prune). The probabilistic view buys something neither merge-based scheme has: sub-word regularization — at training time you can sample among alternative segmentations of the same word, a free data-augmentation that makes the downstream model robust to exactly the non-uniqueness we just saw.

SentencePiece, from Kudo & Richardson (2018), is often conflated with Unigram but is a different thing: it's a toolkit that can run either BPE or Unigram, and its real contribution is treating whitespace as an ordinary character, rendered ▁ (U+2581): "Hello world" → ▁Hello ▁world. That one decision makes it fully reversible (you can detokenize back to the exact original) and language-agnostic — no pre-tokenizer, no "split on spaces" assumption, so it works on Chinese and Thai out of the box. That's precisely the parochial assumption we flagged earlier, now dissolved. It's why SentencePiece dominates multilingual models — T5, ALBERT, XLNet, LLaMA, Mistral all ride it.

Three cutters, one table

Step back and the whole tokenizer zoo fits in one line. BPE merges by frequency; WordPiece merges by likelihood gain; Unigram prunes by likelihood loss. All three end with a bounded sub-word vocabulary — they differ only in how they choose the inventory and how they mark continuations. BPE is bottom-up, marks nothing (or works on bytes), and powers GPT-2/4 and RoBERTa. WordPiece is bottom-up, marks word-internal pieces with ##, and powers BERT (Devlin et al. 2019). Unigram is top-down, marks word starts with ▁ via SentencePiece, and powers T5, ALBERT, XLNet and mT5. The lineage is a tidy arc — word → sub-word → byte: rule-based word splits (Penn Treebank, 1993), BPE born as a compression trick (Gage 1994), WordPiece (2012), BPE finally entering NLP for rare words (Sennrich et al. 2016), Unigram + SentencePiece (2018), and byte-level BPE killing OOV for good (GPT-2, 2019). The algorithm waited twenty-two years for the right problem.

One more category that BPE never learns: special tokens — reserved ids the framework inserts around your text. The classic BERT set: [CLS] (a sequence-summary slot for classification), [SEP] (separates two segments — e.g. query | document in a reranker), [PAD] (batch filler, masked out of the loss), [MASK] (the hidden position a masked-language-model must predict, ~15% of tokens), plus [UNK] and the begin/end markers <s>/</s> (BOS/EOS). The [SEP] case is your bridge to next chapter: a BERT cross-encoder reranker scores [CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP] for relevance. Chat models add their own: ChatML uses <|im_start|>/<|im_end|>, LLaMA-3 uses <|begin_of_text|> and friends, all injected by tokenizer.apply_chat_template(). A real foot-gun lurks here — apply a chat template and pass add_special_tokens=True and you insert BOS twice, quietly degrading quality. Pick one.

Where the cutter leaves scars

The tokenizer isn't a neutral pre-processing step — its arbitrary cuts leave fingerprints all over model behaviour. Start with numbers. Train a byte-level BPE on real text and the split of 327 shifts with vocabulary size: at a 1k vocab it's 3 | 2 | 7 (three tokens), at 4k and 16k it's 32 | 7 (two tokens). The model never sees place value line up consistently — which is a big reason LLMs are quietly bad at arithmetic. (LLaMA and PaLM force single-digit number tokens precisely to fix this.) Code has the same disease: in Python, indentation is the block structure, yet early tokenizers spent one token per space, fragmenting nested code and burning context — GPT-3.5/4 added single tokens for runs of 4/8/16 spaces, a concrete reason they got better at code. And format choice is token cost: YAML usually needs fewer tokens than JSON for the same data (no braces, fewer quotes and commas), a real prompt-engineering lever.

One glyph is not one token. A "family" emoji 👨‍👩‍👧 is really man + ZWJ + woman + ZWJ + girl — several byte tokens glued by zero-width joiners — and under UTF-8 a single Chinese character like 高 is three bytes, so byte-level BPE spends 2–3 tokens on it. The visual atom and the token atom are simply not the same thing. Morphology stress-tests the cutter too: German concatenates freely (Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaftskapitän → six morpheme-pieces), which sub-words handle gracefully, whereas Arabic is non-concatenative — the triliteral root k-t-b ("write") weaves through the vowels and patterns of kataba / kattaba / iktataba, so a contiguous left-to-right merger can't capture the shared piece cleanly. A known weak spot for BPE/WordPiece on Semitic languages.

Two more quirks that come straight from "the model's atoms are tokens, not letters." Trailing-space sensitivity: " the" and "the" are different tokens (remember byte-level BPE folds the leading space in), so a prompt ending in a stray space lands off-distribution and degrades the completion — trim it. And the famous "how many R's in strawberry?" failure: the model sees str + aw + berry, never s-t-r-a-w-..., so any character-level operation requires it to have memorized each token's spelling. It isn't looking at letters at run time. None of these is a reasoning bug; every one is a tokenization scar.

Tokens nobody ever trained

Every scar so far came from where the cutter cut. Here is one that comes from the contract itself, and it starts with an assumption almost everyone makes without noticing: that every entry in the vocabulary means something — that if a string earned a slot, the model knows it. It never had to. The tokenizer is trained on one pile of text and the model on another, and those two piles are almost never the same one: the model's corpus is usually the filtered, deduplicated, cleaned-up descendant of the crawl the vocabulary was built from.

So a string can be frequent enough in the raw crawl to win a slot — a scraped username, a boilerplate fragment, a chunk of some leaked log — and then be filtered almost entirely out of what the model actually reads. Its row in the embedding matrix stays where the initializer put it: never pulled anywhere by a gradient, pointing in an arbitrary direction inside a space where every other row has been shaped by training. Feed it in and the model does something strange — repeats an unrelated word, refuses, drifts off. These are glitch tokens; the case that named them (Rumbelow & Watkins, LessWrong, 2023) was a scraped forum handle that sat in the vocabulary long after it had vanished from anyone's training set.

The lesson generalizes past the joke. A vocabulary slot is a promise of coverage that only the training data can keep, so the vocabulary and the training corpus have to come from the same distribution — and if you filter one after building the other, you have manufactured dead rows on purpose. It is the same mismatch as mixing a tokenizer with foreign weights, only quieter: nothing errors, almost every prompt is fine, and a small set of inputs behaves as though it belongs to a different model. The hygiene is simple. Build the vocabulary on the corpus you will actually train on, and treat an embedding that never moved as a defect rather than a curiosity.

The cutter cannot see place value

The digit scar was stated as a fact — the same number breaks into different pieces at different vocabulary sizes — and left there. Take one more step and it stops being untidy and starts being fatal, because the damage is not that the pieces are odd. It is that they are anchored to the wrong end of the number.

Positional notation is a right-anchored code. A digit means what it means because of how far it stands from the right-hand end, and every procedure you were ever taught for adding or multiplying starts at the right and carries leftward. A merge-based tokenizer knows none of this. It glues the pairs its corpus made frequent, and the frequency of digit pairs is an accident of what people happened to write down — years, prices, street numbers, version strings. So the groups it forms are anchored to the string: the same digit ends up inside a piece of one width here and a piece of another width there, depending on what stood around it.

Now follow that into the arithmetic. Two numbers of different length break into groups that do not line up, so a rule of the form "add the units, then add the tens" has nothing to bind to — the units of one number and the units of the other sit inside pieces of different widths, with different neighbours, and there is no representation at all of a carry crossing a boundary between two pieces. What is left for the model is to have memorized the arithmetic of piece against piece, pair by pair, as unrelated facts. That is why the failure looks like bad reasoning and is not: the representation destroyed the alignment the algorithm needed before any reasoning was asked for.

Which explains why both known repairs attack the grouping and leave the model alone. The blunt one: force every digit into a token of its own, so the pieces align by construction and one rule can be learned instead of a table of pairs — several model families do exactly this, and pay for it with longer sequences on numeric text. The subtler one: group digits from the right in fixed-size blocks, which is precisely what the thousands separator does for a human reader, so that a group means the same magnitude no matter how long the number is. And take the lesson past arithmetic, because it outlives it: the tokenizer imposes an alignment on everything downstream, and any task whose procedure depends on position — tables, fixed-width identifiers, dates, aligned code — is capped by that alignment. Before blaming the model, check whether the units it was handed even line up.

Control tokens are granted, not earned

Special tokens arrived as a list — a summary slot, a separator, a filler, the begin and end markers — with one word doing quiet work: they are inserted. They do not come from the string. Somebody's call adds them around your text, which means that on the way in the tokenizer has to decide what to do when a run of characters looks like one of those markers. Should it become the reserved id, or stay ordinary text? That is not a property of the string. It is a flag on the encode call.

Now feed it text you did not write — which in search is not an edge case, it is the job. Retrieved passages are documents someone else authored, and a document can contain the literal characters of an end-of-turn marker as easily as it contains a comma. Encode it with special tokens enabled and the model receives a structural boundary exactly where you intended content: the passage stops being data and starts being framing, closing a turn you never closed and opening a role you never granted. That is special-token injection, and the repair is the flag itself — encode untrusted spans with the reserved markers disabled, and add the structure yourself, once, outside the untrusted region. It is the same seam that duplicates the begin marker when a chat template and the default flag are applied together: both bugs are the boundary between data and control being drawn twice.

Two things to keep, one of them a limit. The general rule: the line between data and control is drawn by the tokenizer, so it belongs in your threat model rather than in the model's. The limit, stated honestly: disabling the flag stops the token from being forged; it does nothing about instructions written in ordinary words, which the model will read as words and may follow as instructions. That second problem is not a tokenization scar and no encoding flag closes it — it is the one the course meets much later, when generation is grounded in retrieved text and someone has to decide how much authority a retrieved passage gets.

Which scars a bigger corpus heals

The scars arrived as a list, one after another, which makes them look like one kind of problem. They are two kinds, and sorting them is the difference between waiting for a better model and redesigning the thing you have. The test is a single question, and it is worth asking of every complaint you ever have about a tokenizer: would this defect disappear if the cutter had been trained on the ideal corpus?

Say yes and the defect is statistical — the cutter behaved badly because its training text was small, skewed, or missing something. Odd digit groupings, indentation that fragments because deeply indented code was never frequent enough to earn a token, rare words that shatter into single characters, a scraped username that won a vocabulary slot it never deserved: every one of those is a report on a training set, not a law of tokenization. Feed the same algorithm a large, diverse, deliberately balanced corpus and the defect shrinks on its own. These are the complaints you fix by choosing a different model, or by waiting a year.

Say no and the defect is structural — it follows from what a token is, and no amount of text repairs it. A model whose atoms are chunks cannot inspect the letters inside a chunk, however much it has read; counting the letters of a word or reversing a string would require the spelling of every piece to have been memorized as a separate fact. And the multilingual tax is worse than a data problem, because the vocabulary is a fixed budget of slots divided among all the languages the cutter serves. Balancing the corpus does not create slots; it moves them, so every point of fertility one language gains is a point some other language pays. You can shift that tax. You cannot abolish it by collecting more text.

Which is exactly the frontier's argument, and now you can hear it properly. If the structural defects come from having tokens at all, then remove the tokens: token-free models read raw bytes end to end, so there is no vocabulary to divide up, no fertility gap between languages, and no chunk hiding its own spelling — ByT5 is the name to know. What you give up is the free compression a sub-word vocabulary was buying: the sequences get much longer, and everything downstream that scales with length gets more expensive. That is not the industrial default and it is not a curiosity either. It is the honest answer to the second column of the list — and the reason to keep the two columns apart is so you can see which problems that trade actually buys you out of.

The token tax — and why search pays it

All those scars converge on one number with real-world teeth: fertility, the average tokens-per-word a tokenizer spends. Train a 16k-vocab byte-level BPE on a mostly-English corpus and English tokenization costs 3 tokens — but the Russian word «привет» ("hello") costs 12 byte-level tokens. That gap is the multilingual token tax, and it isn't just inefficiency, it's a fairness problem. Attention is \(O(n^2)\), but for typical model sizes the linear feed-forward and projection layers dominate total compute, so 2× fertility is roughly 2× the training compute in that regime — approaching 4× only once the \(O(n^2)\) attention term dominates at very long sequences; at inference it's about 2× the API bill, 2× the latency, and a smaller effective context window. A speaker of a low-resource language literally pays more, waits longer, and gets a smaller usable prompt — for the same information.

And this lands squarely on search, which is why the whole tokenization half wasn't a detour. Sparse retrieval like BM25 matches surface terms, so it is acutely sensitive to tokenization, stemming and stop-words: under exact BM25 a query for ensembling simply won't match a document that says ensemble — plurals and tenses miss, which is the lexical gap from the previous chapter wearing a new coat. Worse, sparse and dense systems often tokenize differently: euthanasia is one BM25 term, but a multilingual model like XLM-R splits it into several sub-words, so when you try to fuse a sparse ranker with a neural one, the units don't line up. And fertility means non-English queries are more likely to be truncated and under-retrieved — uneven recall across languages, a search-fairness bug baked into the cutter. Tokenization is not a preprocessing footnote; it shapes recall.

So Part one of the mission is complete: we turned the alien's raw stream into bounded, computable units — sub-words learned by BPE, WordPiece or Unigram, normalized and cleaned, robust against Heaps' endless tail and the world's 7000 scripts. Every phrase is now a vector of numbers. Which raises the question the second half exists to answer: given two of these vectors, how do we measure that they mean the same thing? On to the Instruments.

Why a space of separate axes says nothing

An id is an address, so something still has to turn an address into a position before anything can be measured. The obvious construction is the honest one to try first: give every entry of the vocabulary its own axis, and write a token as the vector carrying a \(1\) on its own axis and \(0\) everywhere else — the one-hot encoding. As an encoding it is flawless. No two tokens collide, nothing is lost, and you can always read the token back off the vector.

Now measure inside it and watch the whole thing evaporate. Two distinct one-hot vectors share no coordinate, so their dot product is \(0\), so their cosine is \(0\), and the distance between them is \(\sqrt{2}\) — the same \(\sqrt{2}\) for every pair in the vocabulary. The geometry announces, with perfect confidence, that every pair of distinct words is exactly as different as every other pair. couch sits precisely as far from sofa as it sits from Thursday. That is the vocabulary gap of the previous chapter written out as geometry: on separate axes synonymy is not merely unlearned, it is unrepresentable, and the Lexical Gremlin gets to keep its wall for free.

What went wrong is the axes. A vector space only tells you something when the coordinates are shared — when two different texts can put weight on the same direction and thereby be seen to agree. Counting terms shares coordinates, and two documents that both talk about space meet on that axis; that sharing is the entire reason the previous chapter's lexical machinery worked at all. Learned embeddings go further and share directions that correspond to no single word. Where those directions come from is a later chapter's job. Take them as given, and the question that occupies the rest of this one is the right one: given two vectors whose coordinates are shared, how do you decide that they point the same way?

How a whole text becomes one vector

One-hot gave a single token a position. A text is many tokens, and something still has to collapse many positions into one object, because a measure compares two things and not two lists of different lengths. The standard construction is the simplest one available: add the one-hot vectors up. What falls out carries, on the axis of each vocabulary entry, the number of times that entry occurred — a bag of words. Its dimension is the size of the vocabulary, not the length of the text, so a tweet and a thesis become points in the same space and are finally comparable at all.

Now read what the sum destroyed and what it saved, because both matter. Addition is commutative, so every permutation of the same tokens produces the identical vector: dog bites man and man bites dog are one point, and word order — the material syntax is made of — is gone at the very first step. In exchange the construction fixes the objection of the previous beat. The axes are now shared: two texts that both talk about space put weight on the same coordinate and can be seen to agree. Order-blind and shockingly cheap, and enough to carry a surprising amount of retrieval on its own.

Two things to hold onto. First, the counts are raw, so a long text has larger numbers on every axis it touches — the loudness that the next beat is about to catch red-handed, and the reason every classical weighting scheme sits directly on top of this same vector rather than replacing it. Second, this is why a lexical index can exist at all: a mostly-zero vector of named counts is a posting list read sideways, and the whole inverted-index machinery of the previous chapter is the sparse arithmetic of the bag. Learned embeddings later give up the named axes. They never give up this move — turning a variable-length text into one fixed-width vector.

Length lies

Tokenization done, every phrase is now a vector of numbers — a point in space, where each coordinate counts (roughly) how much of some feature the phrase carries. To compare two phrases, the obvious move is to measure the straight-line distance between their points: \(\lVert a - b\rVert\), the Euclidean distance you learned in school. Obvious… and wrong.

Here's the trap, with exact numbers. Take the phrase whose vector is \(a = (1, 1)\) and the same phrase said twice as emphatically, \(b = (10, 10)\). They carry the same meaning — both point in exactly the same direction, up-and-to-the-right at 45°. But the Euclidean distance between them is $$\lVert a - b\rVert = \sqrt{9^2 + 9^2} = \sqrt{162} \approx 12.73.$$ Raw distance screams "these are totally different!" — when in fact one is just a louder version of the other.

That's the bug in one line: length lies. Euclidean distance conflates how much a vector says with what it says. A long document and a short document on the identical topic land far apart simply because the long one has bigger counts. We don't want loudness. We want a measure that throws away magnitude entirely and listens only to direction — to where the vector points.

Measure the angle, not the length

Enter Sir Cosine and the Knights of the Unit Sphere. Their whole creed: forget distance, measure the angle between two vectors. The instrument is cosine similarity — the cosine of that angle, $$\cos\theta = \frac{a\cdot b}{\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert} = \frac{\sum_i a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i b_i^2}}.$$ The numerator is the dot product, \(\sum_i a_i b_i\); the denominator divides out both lengths, so the magnitude cancels and only direction is left. Point the same way → angle \(0\) → \(\cos\theta = 1\) (as similar as it gets). At right angles → \(\cos\theta = 0\) (nothing in common). Opposite → \(\cos\theta = -1\). For ordinary word-count vectors, which can't go negative, the score lives in \([0, 1]\).

Run it on our trap from the last beat: \(a = (1, 1)\), \(b = (10, 10)\). Dot product \(= 1\cdot 10 + 1\cdot 10 = 20\); \(\lVert a\rVert = \sqrt{2}\), \(\lVert b\rVert = \sqrt{200}\); so $$\cos\theta = \frac{20}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{200}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{400}} = \frac{20}{20} = 1.0.$$ Where Euclidean said \(12.73\) ("miles apart"), Sir Cosine says \(1.0\) ("identical"). He saw through the loudness to the meaning.

And here's the elegant payoff that makes vector search practical. Expand the squared distance: \(\lVert x - y\rVert^2 = \lVert x\rVert^2 + \lVert y\rVert^2 - 2\,(x\cdot y)\). If you first normalize every vector to unit length — push every point onto the surface of the unit sphere — that collapses to $$\lVert x - y\rVert^2 = 2\,(1 - \cos\theta).$$ On the sphere, cosine, dot product, and (negative) Euclidean distance all rank neighbours in the exact same order. That's why real systems L2-normalize their embeddings and then run a blazing-fast dot-product index: they get cosine's meaning at a dot product's speed. The next figure is the worked example on the sphere — watch the numbers, no jokes.

Two vectors, u = (1,1) and v = (10,10). They point the same way — but one is much longer.

Euclidean distance measures the gap between their tips: ‖u − v‖ = √162 ≈ 12.73. It calls them far apart.

But the angle between them is θ = 0°, so cosine = 1. Cosine says they are identical in direction.

Project both onto the unit sphere (normalize). They land on the same point — direction is all that's left.

For unit vectors the two views connect: ‖x − y‖² = 2(1 − cos θ). Same direction ⇒ cosine 1 — and distance 0 only AFTER normalizing: the raw u and v above share a direction but not a length, so ‖u − v‖ stays large.

Two phrases, two vectors of counts: a = (1, 1) and b = (10, 10). Same direction, very different lengths. We are going to compute their cosine by hand — dot product on top, the two lengths underneath — and watch a single number fall out.

First the numerator: the dot product a·b. Multiply matching coordinates and add — 1·10 plus 1·10 — which gives 20. That single sum measures how much the two vectors pull in the same direction, before any lengths are divided out.

Now the denominator: each vector's length, ‖a‖ = √(1² + 1²) = √2 ≈ 1.41 and ‖b‖ = √(10² + 10²) = √200 ≈ 14.14. Two irrational radicals — not numbers you can divide by cleanly yet.

Multiply the two lengths and the radicals cancel: √2 · √200 = √400 = 20 — a clean integer. That is exactly the scale we need to divide the loudness out of the dot product.

Divide the dot product by the product of lengths: cos θ = 20 ⁄ (√2 · √200) = 20 ⁄ 20 = 1.0. The magnitudes cancelled perfectly — only direction survived. Cosine = 1.0 means a and b point exactly the same way: as similar as it gets.

The dot product: when magnitude is signal

Cosine threw magnitude away on purpose. But sometimes magnitude is the signal — and that's the dot product standing on its own, not buried inside the cosine formula. It's just $$a\cdot b = \sum_i a_i b_i = \lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert\cos\theta,$$ which is cosine without the normalization, so it rewards both alignment and length. Quick worked example: \(u = 2i - 3j\), \(v = 4i + 2j\) give \(u\cdot v = 2\cdot 4 + (-3)\cdot 2 = 8 - 6 = 2\). Unlike cosine, its range is the whole line \((-\infty, \infty)\), and — careful — it is not a metric: it can be negative, and it doesn't satisfy the distance axioms.

So why ever prefer it? Because of MIPS — maximum inner product search, \(\arg\max_b\, a\cdot b\). Trained dual encoders (the two-tower retrievers behind dense search, like DPR, Karpukhin et al. 2020) deliberately learn meaningful norms — they push a confident, information-rich passage to a larger magnitude so it wins on dot product, something cosine would flatten away. When the model was trained to put signal in the length, you must score with the dot product or you throw that signal out.

And the flip side, the rule of thumb: don't use raw dot product when length is an artifact rather than signal — e.g. raw TF-IDF, where a long document scores high just for being long. There, magnitude is noise: normalize it away and use cosine. The choice isn't aesthetic; it follows directly from whether your representation earned its lengths.

The whole Lp family

Euclidean distance — the \(\sqrt{\sum(\Delta)^2}\) that length lies taught us to distrust — is just one member of a whole family, the Minkowski \(L_p\) distances: $$\lVert x\rVert_p = \Big(\sum_i |x_i|^{p}\Big)^{1/p},\qquad d_p(x,y) = \Big(\sum_i |x_i - y_i|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}.$$ Turn the single dial \(p\) and you get the whole zoo. \(p = 2\) is Euclidean (straight-line): for \(P(3,2), Q(4,1)\), \(d_2 = \sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.414\). \(p = 1\) is Manhattan (city-block, the taxicab sum of absolute differences): for \(P(2,9), Q(3,5)\), \(d_1 = |3-2| + |5-9| = 1 + 4 = 5\). And \(p \to \infty\) is Chebyshev, which collapses to the single largest coordinate gap, \(L_\infty = \max_i |x_i - y_i|\) — on that same \(P,Q\) it's \(\max(1, 4) = 4\).

Two warnings on the dial. Push \(p\) below 1 — a fractional \(0 \lt p \lt 1\) — and you get a quasi-norm: it still measures something, but it violates the triangle inequality, so it is no longer a true metric. And the reason anyone reaches for low \(p\) at all is the punchline of this chapter's second half: in high dimensions, \(L_1\) keeps contrast better than \(L_2\) — Aggarwal, Hinneburg & Keim (2001) showed that lower \(p\) resists the distance concentration the Wraith is about to unleash. So when scales differ or outliers bite, prefer \(L_1\)'s linear penalty over \(L_2\)'s quadratic one.

And here is the elegant bridge back to Sir Cosine, the single most important identity in vector search. Expand the squared Euclidean distance: \(\lVert x - y\rVert^2 = \lVert x\rVert^2 + \lVert y\rVert^2 - 2\,(x\cdot y)\). L2-normalize every vector — push it onto the unit sphere, \(\lVert x\rVert = \lVert y\rVert = 1\) — and it collapses to $$\lVert x - y\rVert^2 = 2\,(1 - \cos\theta).$$ On the sphere, ranking by cosine \(\uparrow\), by dot product \(\uparrow\), and by squared-Euclidean \(\downarrow\) give the identical nearest-neighbour order. Off the sphere they part ways — that's our (1,1) vs (10,10) trap, \(\cos = 1\) yet \(\lVert a - b\rVert = \sqrt{162} \approx 12.73\). Magnitude is exactly where the three measures agree or diverge.

What "is it a metric?" actually buys you

The family table keeps a column that looks like pedantry — is it really a metric? — and it is the most operational column in the whole table. A metric is a distance obeying four rules: it is never negative, it is zero exactly when the two points coincide, it is symmetric, and it satisfies the triangle inequality, \(d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)\). The first three are hygiene; they keep the arithmetic from embarrassing you. The fourth is the one with cash value.

The triangle inequality is what lets an index skip work. Suppose you have already measured the distance from your query to some reference point, and you know how far a whole group of candidates sits from that same reference point. The inequality turns those two facts into a bound on how close any member of the group could possibly be — and if that bound is worse than the best candidate you have already found, you discard the entire group without touching a single one of its members. That is the engine inside every classical metric index. No triangle inequality, no bound; no bound, no pruning; and search collapses back into scanning everything.

Now read the column again, with money in mind. Euclidean and \(L_1\) are metrics, so they prune. Cosine similarity is not a distance at all — it is largest when the points coincide — and even flipped around it fails the inequality, though the angular distance \(\arccos(\cos\theta)/\pi\) is a metric, which is the standard repair. The dot product is worse off still: a point is not even closest to itself, since a longer vector can beat it. That is exactly why maximum inner product search needed its own literature instead of reusing the old trees, and why the standard move is to push everything onto the unit sphere first, where the ordering becomes the Euclidean one and the old machinery applies again. Choosing a measure is never only a choice about what "similar" means; it is a choice about which indexes are allowed to be exact.

Sets and strings need their own rulers

Cosine, dot and \(L_p\) all assume dense vectors of real numbers. But a lot of data isn't that — it's sets (the words shingled out of a document, the items a user clicked, categorical features) — and sets want their own ruler. The natural one is Jaccard: $$J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \in [0, 1],$$ the fraction of the union that the two sets share. Its complement, the Jaccard distance \(1 - J\), is a genuine metric. The beautiful part is how you scale it. MinHash (Broder, used at AltaVista) builds tiny signatures with a magic property: the probability that two sketches collide equals their Jaccard — so you estimate set similarity in sub-linear time, the trick still used to deduplicate web-scale LLM training data. Its dense cousin SimHash (Charikar) does the same for cosine via random hyperplanes. The rhyme to memorize: MinHash ↔ Jaccard (sets); SimHash ↔ cosine (dense). Pair either with LSH banding and you get sublinear near-duplicate search.

And when the unit of comparison is a string itself — spell-check, fuzzy matching, fixing a typo'd query — you want edit (Levenshtein) distance: the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, or substitutions to turn one string into another. It's computed by a small dynamic program over a grid, $$D[i,j] = \min\begin{cases} D[i\!-\!1, j] + 1 & \text{(delete)}\\ D[i, j\!-\!1] + 1 & \text{(insert)}\\ D[i\!-\!1, j\!-\!1] + \mathbb{1}[s_i \neq t_j] & \text{(substitute, free if equal)} \end{cases}$$ Worked example: HONDA → HYUNDAI. They share the H and the run N D A; the cheapest alignment substitutes O→Y, inserts U, and inserts a trailing I (1 substitution + 2 insertions), so the bottom-right cell of the grid reads 3 edits. Levenshtein is a true metric on strings, but the DP costs \(O(nm)\) — fine for short strings, hopeless for long documents, which is exactly why for documents you shingle and MinHash instead.

Why the collision probability is exactly Jaccard

The MinHash property was stated and then walked past, which is a shame, because it is one of those proofs that fits in a paragraph and changes how you read the whole family. Fix a random permutation of the universe of elements. The sketch of a set is defined as the element of that set which comes first under the permutation. Now ask the only question that matters: when do two sets get the same sketch? Look at the first element of their union, and everything follows from where that element lives.

It lives either in the intersection or in exactly one of the two sets. If it is in the intersection, it belongs to both, so it is the first element of the first set and the first element of the second, and the two sketches agree. If it belongs to only one of them, it is that set's minimum while the other set's minimum sits somewhere later, so the sketches disagree. The sketches therefore agree precisely when the union's first element lands in the intersection — and a uniformly random permutation makes every element of the union equally likely to be first. Hence $$\Pr[h(A) = h(B)] = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} = J(A,B),$$ exactly, not approximately.

Read what that buys. Each independent permutation is one unbiased coin flip whose bias is the Jaccard, so the fraction of agreeing rows across many permutations estimates \(J\), and the error shrinks like one over the square root of the number of rows. You buy precision with signature length — and, this is the whole trick, the signature length does not depend on how large the sets are. A pair of sets with millions of elements each is compared by two short fixed-width sketches. The price is stated in the same line: what you estimate is \(J\) and nothing else, so every blind spot Jaccard has is inherited, undiminished, by every sketch of it.

Turning a sketch into a shortlist

A sketch makes one comparison cheap; it does not remove the need to make all of them, and all of them is quadratic in the number of documents. The escape is to stop comparing and start bucketing. Cut a signature into \(b\) bands of \(r\) rows each, hash every band separately, and declare two documents candidates if they land in the same bucket for at least one band. Nothing is compared pairwise; documents simply meet, or fail to meet, inside a hash table.

The arithmetic of that rule is worth doing by hand, because it explains the whole design. Two documents of Jaccard similarity \(s\) agree on any single row with probability \(s\). They agree on all \(r\) rows of one band with probability \(s^{r}\); they fail a given band with probability \(1 - s^{r}\); they fail every band with probability \((1-s^{r})^{b}\). So the probability of becoming a candidate is $$P(s) = 1 - (1 - s^{r})^{b}.$$ Plot that against \(s\) and it is an S-curve: near zero for dissimilar pairs, near one for similar ones, with a steep step between. The step sits near \(s \approx (1/b)^{1/r}\), and that is the threshold you are actually setting.

Which makes \(b\) and \(r\) the design, not implementation details. More rows per band demands more agreement to survive a band, so the step moves right and fewer junk pairs get through; more bands gives a pair more chances, so the step moves left and fewer true pairs are missed. You are choosing where the wall stands and how sharp it is — trading candidate volume, which is work, against missed pairs, which is recall you will never get back. And notice the shape you are looking at: a cheap, approximate, high-recall first stage feeding an exact comparison that runs only on the survivors. It is the funnel from the previous chapter, one abstraction lower and built out of hash buckets.

When size decides instead of overlap

The family table listed Jaccard's pitfall as set-size disparity, which sounds like a caveat and is a whole failure mode. Watch it happen. Take a small set \(A\) entirely contained in a much larger set \(B\): every single element of \(A\) was found. The intersection is \(A\), the union is \(B\), so \(J = |A|/|B|\) — a small number, arbitrarily small, purely because \(B\) is big. Perfect containment scores as near-total dissimilarity. Nothing went wrong: Jaccard answered the question it was built to answer, how much of the union do the two share, and that question quietly charges you for a size difference nobody asked about.

The repair is to change the denominator, because the denominator is where the question lives. Containment, \(C(A,B) = |A \cap B| / |A|\), asks how much of \(A\) sits inside \(B\) — deliberately asymmetric, and equal to one in the case that just embarrassed Jaccard. That is exactly why plagiarism detection uses containment rather than Jaccard: a paragraph lifted into a book has a microscopic Jaccard and a containment of one. And it is exactly why deduplication keeps Jaccard: there, two near-duplicate documents are supposed to be about the same size, so a size gap is evidence of difference rather than an artifact.

The general lesson is bigger than these two formulas: with the intersection held fixed, the normalizing choice is the measure. Divide by the union and you get Jaccard; by the left set and you get containment; by something between the two sizes and you get the Dice family, the set-flavoured cousin of cosine. Three denominators, three different questions, three different rankings on the same data. So before you pick a set measure, say out loud whether a difference in size means a difference in meaning, or whether it is an artifact of how the sets were built — document length, shingle width, a user who happens to click a lot. If it is an artifact, keep it out of the denominator.

You never run the grid over a corpus

The dynamic program filling that grid costs the product of the two lengths, per pair. Now put it where it actually gets used — a misspelled query against a lexicon of hundreds of thousands of entries, on every keystroke — and it is dead on arrival. So production spell correction never scores the lexicon. It goes the other way: generate the small edit neighbourhood around what the user typed — every string reachable in one edit, and if you can afford it, two — and intersect that with the lexicon. Intersecting a few thousand candidate strings with a hash set is a lookup, not a scan, and the grid is never filled at all.

When the budget of edits grows past what enumeration can carry, the second route is precisely the pruning from the metric-axioms beat. Levenshtein is a metric, triangle inequality included, so a metric index over the lexicon — a tree whose children are grouped by their distance to a pivot word — can bound how close anything in a subtree could possibly be, and discard the whole subtree without measuring a single word inside it. That is the boring axiom column cashing out on strings: the reason it mattered whether a ruler obeys the fourth rule was so that a structure could refuse to look.

And be clear where this sits in the engine, because that decides how it is judged. String distance is a query-side repair: it runs before retrieval and changes what you ask, not how you score. Which means distance alone must never choose the correction — a candidate is ranked by its edit distance together with how frequent it is, how close the substituted letters sit on a keyboard, and whether the rewrite actually returns results. And it means the failure is asymmetric and silent: quietly rewriting a rare-but-correct query hands the user a fluent page about something else, and the logs will record a session that looks satisfied. The ruler ranks candidates. It does not get to choose.

Which measure, when

Time to lay the whole toolbox on the bench. The similarity family, by what each captures, its range, whether it's a true metric, and its pitfall: Cosine — angle, \([-1, 1]\), not a metric (the angular distance \(\arccos(\cos)/\pi\) is), pitfall: the zero vector and anisotropy. Dot — angle + magnitude, \((-\infty, \infty)\), not a metric, pitfall: length artifacts (its strength in MIPS). Euclidean — straight-line gap, \([0, \infty)\), a true metric, pitfall: scale and high-d concentration. Jaccard — set overlap, \([0, 1]\), distance is a metric, pitfall: set-size disparity. Edit / Levenshtein — string ops, a metric, pitfall: \(O(nm)\), short strings only. KL / JS — distribution gap, KL is \([0, \infty)\) and not a metric (it's asymmetric; \(\sqrt{\mathrm{JS}}\) is a metric), pitfall: zero bins need smoothing. Pearson — centered correlation, \([-1, 1]\), not a metric — and the lovely fact students miss: Pearson is literally cosine after mean-centering, which is why it removes per-user rating bias in recommendation.

From the family falls a rough decision guide (heuristic, not law — the real answer depends on how your embeddings were trained): text / embeddings where direction matters but length shouldn't → cosine; continuous features on the same scale → Euclidean \(L_2\); continuous features on different scales or with outliers → Manhattan \(L_1\); categorical / sets by member overlap → Jaccard; ordinal ranks by monotonic agreement → Spearman; correlated, mean-centred multivariate → Pearson. The deeper fork underneath it is sparse-sets vs dense-embeddings: sparse, mostly-zero set representations (shingled docs, user–item pairs) want Jaccard + MinHash + LSH; dense learned vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) want cosine or dot plus an ANN index (HNSW, Malkov & Yashunin 2018, or IVF-PQ). Pick the wrong family — cosine on raw sparse counts, or Jaccard on dense floats — and the ranking is meaningless.

And tied to actual jobs: semantic search / RAG → cosine (normalized) or dot for trained dual encoders; near-duplicate / dedup → Jaccard via MinHash; plagiarism → shingle containment then edit-distance alignment; clustering → squared-Euclidean (k-means) or cosine (spherical k-means); recommendation / collaborative filtering → Pearson or a learned dot; drift monitoring → KL / JS / Wasserstein over distributions. Measure choice is task-driven and tied to the representation — never a default you reach for out of habit.

Two kinds of vector, two kinds of bill

The fork underneath the whole toolbox is not a measure, it is a representation, and the two branches disagree about what a coordinate even is. In a sparse vector the coordinates are named: this one is the term space, that one is a product id, and nearly all of them are zero. In a dense vector the coordinates are anonymous learned directions, no one of them is any particular word, and essentially none of them is zero. "Similar" therefore means two different things: overlap of named things on one side, alignment of directions on the other.

The cost models are mirror images, and that is what decides architecture rather than taste. A sparse comparison costs what the non-zeros cost — you never visit a coordinate where both sides are empty, which is exactly how an inverted index answers a query by walking a few posting lists and never looking at the rest of the collection at all. A dense comparison has no zeros to skip: every candidate costs a full sweep over every coordinate, so you cannot afford to compare against everything, and the entire approximate-neighbour industry exists to avoid having to. Sparse buys its speed from the data being mostly empty; dense pays for its reach by never being empty.

What each one cannot do is the part worth memorizing. Sparse matching cannot connect two texts that share no term — no overlap, no score, and the Gremlin keeps its wall — but in exchange it never invents a match: if it fired, the words were literally on the page, and you can point at them. Dense matching generalizes across wording, which is the whole point, and pays for that by being able to be confidently wrong in a way you cannot audit by reading the numbers. So the measure follows the representation, not your preference: named-and-mostly-zero wants set measures and hashing; anonymous-and-dense wants angle or dot product plus an approximate index. Cross them — an angle over raw counts, a set measure over learned floats — and the ranking is not so much wrong as meaningless.

The half of the range counts never reach

The family table gives cosine the range \([-1, 1]\), and for count vectors half of that range is unreachable — not by convention, by construction. A count is never negative, so every bag-of-words vector lives in the non-negative orthant, the corner of the space where no coordinate has a sign to give. The dot product of two such vectors is a sum of non-negative terms, hence never negative, so the cosine of any two documents you built out of counts lies in \([0,1]\). The negative half of the instrument is present in the formula and dead in the data.

That missing half is a channel, and it is worth naming what it would have carried. A negative cosine is the geometry's way of saying these point the other way. Without it, "unrelated" and "opposite" collapse into the same neighbourhood of zero, and a much worse thing follows: two texts that contradict each other are not opposite in count space at all — they are nearly identical, because a glowing review and a scathing one about the same film share almost every word. A cosine over counts measures what a text is about and never what it claims. That is not a defect of the measure; the measure faithfully reports the coordinates it was handed, and those coordinates never encoded a stance.

Two readings to carry. Learned embeddings do have negative coordinates, so the sign channel exists again — but existing is not the same as being used, and whether a negative cosine means anything at all depends entirely on what the training objective pushed apart. And the practical one: over counts, zero is the floor — «no shared terms» — not a midpoint, so a threshold chosen as «above the middle counts as similar» is arbitrary in one way on counts and arbitrary in a completely different way on trained vectors. Which suggests the obvious repair for a cloud crushed into one corner of the space: if the origin is in the wrong place, move it.

Pearson is cosine that moved the origin

The last row of the family table makes a claim students read as a coincidence: Pearson correlation is literally cosine after mean-centering. Write it and there is nothing left to be surprised by. Subtract from each vector its own coordinate mean, then take the cosine of what remains: \(\rho(a,b) = \cos(a - \bar{a}\mathbf{1},; b - \bar{b}\mathbf{1})\). No statistics were added to the geometry. One thing happened — the origin moved from the corner of the space to the middle of the data — and a correlation coefficient is what that move is called in the other vocabulary.

Look at what the move buys, because it is exactly the channel the previous beat lost. After centering, coordinates run both ways: a vector can now sit anti-aligned with another, and a negative score finally means something. And the part of a vector that is the same on every axis — its constant offset — is removed, which is the whole reason recommendation reaches for Pearson. A user who rates everything generously and one who rates everything harshly, agreeing perfectly about which items are better, sit far apart in raw ratings and land on top of each other after centering. Their disagreement lived entirely in the constant, and the constant is what the mean took away.

Two cautions, and the second is the one that bites in search. First, whose mean: centering each vector by its own mean and centering each coordinate across the collection are different operations answering different questions — the recommendation case is per-user, while the per-coordinate version is the first step of the whitening this course meets later, when embeddings turn out to be squeezed into a cone. Second, centering is not free on sparse data. Subtract a mean from a mostly-zero vector and every zero becomes minus-the-mean: the vector is now dense, and it costs what dense costs. That is the practical reason lexical retrieval stays in its non-negative corner and fixes loudness with weighting rather than by moving the origin.

Why the histogram narrows

The figure ahead shows a histogram of distances collapsing into a spike as the number of dimensions climbs, and it is worth knowing why before you watch it, because the reason is a two-line argument. Take a query point and a cloud of points whose coordinates are drawn independently. The squared distance to any one of them is a sum over coordinates — one independent contribution per dimension. Sums of many independent contributions behave in a very particular way: the total grows in proportion to how many terms you added, while the spread around that total grows only in proportion to the square root of the count.

Divide one by the other and the phenomenon falls out. The typical distance races ahead while the variation around it barely moves, so the relative spread — the gap between the nearest point and the farthest one, measured against the distance itself — shrinks steadily as dimensions pile up. Every point ends up at roughly the same distance from the query. The nearest neighbour does not stop existing; it stops being distinguished, which is worse, because the ranking now turns on a difference smaller than any noise you have, and a nudge to a single coordinate reshuffles the winner. Beyer et al. (1999) stated it for retrieval, and it hits every \(L_p\) at once — this is the Wraith's opening move.

Two honest footnotes before the picture. First, lowering \(p\) helps a little: the linear penalty of \(L_1\) leaves a bit more contrast standing than the squared penalty of \(L_2\), which is the argument behind fractional norms — a discount, not a cure, and later work is clear that it does not repeal anything. Second, and far more important: the argument assumed coordinates that are independent and structureless. Hold on to that assumption. It is doing all the work, and the rest of this act is about what happens when it is false.

In 2-D, distances between random points spread out nicely — near is clearly near, far is clearly far (cv ≈ 0.48).

Bump it to 10 dimensions and the spread already tightens around the mean (cv ≈ 0.19).

At 100 dimensions the Wraith has flattened the histogram — almost every pair is the same distance apart (cv ≈ 0.06).

At 1000 dimensions it's a spike: nearest and farthest are nearly indistinguishable (cv ≈ 0.02). Raw distance stops meaning anything — so we measure the angle instead.

The Wraith has two more tricks

The histogram you just watched is the Wraith's first trick: distance concentration. As the number of dimensions climbs from 2 to 1000, the spread of pairwise distances collapses toward a single spike — the nearest point becomes barely closer than the farthest, so "nearest neighbour" almost stops meaning anything, and a tiny perturbation flips which point wins. Real embeddings live in hundreds to thousands of dimensions, exactly where this bites. But the Wraith has two subtler tricks, and a working engineer has to recognise both.

Hubness. In high dimensions the geometry quietly elects a few hub points that show up in almost everyone's nearest-neighbour list — not because they're truly relevant, but because of where they happen to sit — while other points (the anti-hubs) appear in nobody's. The symptom is unmistakable in a real system: the same handful of documents keep getting returned for unrelated queries. The fixes rescale the local geometry — mutual proximity, local/global scaling, mutual-kNN graphs — so a point can't be a universal neighbour by accident.

Anisotropy. This one is about the embeddings themselves, not the dimension count. Raw representations out of a model like BERT or GPT-2 don't fill space evenly — they cram into a narrow cone, so even two unrelated tokens already sit at a small angle, and raw cosine reports everything as "pretty similar." Your beautiful angle-measure gets inflated and loses its bite. The effect is layer-dependent (Ethayarajh 2019): lower layers stay fairly isotropic, the squeeze is worst in the upper layers — extreme in GPT-2's final layer, far milder in BERT — so which layer you read embeddings from is itself a calibration choice. The remedy is to push the cloud back toward isotropy — whitening, standardization, or, best of all, contrastive training that deliberately pulls synonyms together and shoves impostors apart. So take it from someone the Wraith has fooled before: never trust raw cosine on out-of-the-box hidden states until you've calibrated them. (Contextual embeddings from BERT/GPT-2 and contrastive training get their proper introduction in Lectures 7–8 — we flag them here only for their effect on cosine.)

What cosine does not promise

Two beliefs about cosine sound like one belief, and both are wrong. The first: cosine and Euclidean rank the same, so it makes no difference which you use. They rank the same only on vectors already pushed onto the unit sphere — that identity had a precondition, and the precondition is the whole content. Step off the sphere and take a query with two candidates lying on the very same ray through the origin, differing only in length. Cosine cannot separate them at all: same direction, same score, a tie at its maximum. Euclidean separates them instantly and strictly prefers the one whose length is closer to the query's. Magnitude decided the ranking; the angle had no vote.

The second belief runs deeper and is newer: a high cosine between two learned embeddings means the model considers them similar. Steck et al. (2024) made the uncomfortable observation on regularized linear models — those objectives carry a rescaling symmetry, so you can stretch some coordinates and squash others in a coordinated way that leaves every dot-product prediction exactly unchanged while moving the cosine between the same two vectors wherever you like. A quantity you can change without changing a single prediction is not a property of the model; it is a property of an arbitrary choice nobody wrote down. Cosine on learned embeddings is well defined only once something pins that choice.

The repair is the FAISS rule arriving from the other direction: score with the geometry the model was trained under. A model trained directly against cosine pins the choice itself and is fine. A model trained against the dot product should be read with the dot product. A raw encoder trained against neither wants its space fixed first — whitening, or contrastive training — before you read anything absolute off a cosine at all. Which is the last thing to internalize: a cosine has no absolute scale. It supports comparisons within one space. It is not a probability, not a percentage of shared meaning, and a threshold tuned on one model transfers to another only by luck.

Why a few points become everyone's neighbour

Hubness arrived as a symptom with a shrug attached — a handful of items keep coming back for unrelated queries, because of where they happen to sit. The shrug is unnecessary. The mechanism is two sentences long, it makes hubness a consequence of the concentration you already understand rather than a second unrelated curse, and — the part that pays — it names which repair to reach for.

Start from the fact the last beat established: in many dimensions every pairwise distance crowds toward one typical value, so the differences that decide a nearest-neighbour list are tiny. Now add the only other ingredient. A point's expected distance to a random other point is not the same for every point — it depends on how far that point sits from the middle of the cloud. Sit near the middle and you are, on average, slightly closer to everything; sit out at the edge and you are, on average, slightly farther from everything. That gap is small. But the differences deciding the list are small too, and here is the asymmetry that settles it: the centre-ward advantage is the same for every query, while genuine relevance differs from query to query. A systematic small edge beats a random small one, every time you run the comparison.

So the central points win a place in list after list — not because they answer each query, but because they are marginally closer to all of them — and the far-out points lose the same comparison every time and appear in nobody's list. That is hubness, and note what it is not: it is not a separate pathology standing beside concentration. It is what concentration does once you admit that a real cloud has a middle.

Which immediately names the fixes, and explains why the two families of them are the same idea applied at opposite ends. If the offence is proximity to the centre, remove the centre: subtract the mean, and if the cloud is stretched unevenly, rescale the axes too — that is whitening, and it is why centering keeps turning up as advice with no explanation attached. Or leave the geometry alone and repair the comparison: judge a candidate not by its raw distance to the query but by that distance relative to how close the candidate usually sits to anything at all — the family of local and global scalings and mutual-neighbour rules. Both remove a bias that belongs to the point rather than to the pair. And before either, run the diagnostic, because it costs one pass over a query log: count how often each item appears in the top list of many different queries, and look at the shape of those counts. A healthy index gives a boring, tight distribution. A hub-ridden one gives a long right tail, with a few items sitting in a large share of all the lists — and that tells you the strange results are a geometry problem, not a relevance problem.

Why retrieval works anyway

Take the concentration argument at face value and vector search should not work at all. It does, at web scale, every day — so the argument must be missing something, and it is: its own assumption. Distances concentrate when the coordinates are independent and the cloud is structureless. Real embeddings are the opposite of structureless. They form clusters, they crowd near a curved surface with far fewer degrees of freedom than there are columns in the matrix, and it is that smaller number — the intrinsic dimensionality — that the geometry actually reacts to. The Wraith's theorem is about the space you allocated; your data lives in a much smaller room inside it.

So contrast survives exactly where the data has structure and dies exactly where it does not — which is also why approximate indexes are possible at all. A graph index is a bet that a neighbour's neighbour is likely to be a neighbour; a cell index is a bet that the cloud clumps. Both bets are hopeless on uniform noise and mostly right on trained embeddings, which is why algorithms that look absurd in a textbook example run production search. Concentration has not been repealed. It has been outrun by structure, and the Knights hold the field because the ground under them is not flat.

Two things to carry out. Normalizing onto the sphere is still worth doing, but be precise about what it buys: it removes length as a confound so that the comparison is honest — not immunity from concentration, which is a different disease. And the only trustworthy statement about your space is a measured one. Look at whether the nearest neighbours are genuinely separated from the rest on your data, at your dimension, before you trust any ranking built on top. If that gap has already closed, no measure downstream will re-open it, and the repair belongs upstream — in how the vectors were trained.

In practice: match the metric to the training

One last thing before the Knights take their bow, because it's the bug that bites real systems hardest. The library everyone reaches for is FAISS (Johnson, Douze & Jégou 2019) — and FAISS has no native cosine metric. It offers METRIC_L2, which returns squared \(L_2\) (no square root — cheaper, and the ordering is identical anyway), and METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT for the dot product. To get cosine you L2-normalize your vectors first, then use an inner-product index (IndexFlatIP). Forget to normalize, and your "cosine" search silently becomes a dot-product search that rewards long vectors — the single most common production retrieval bug, and it fails quietly, returning plausible-looking nonsense.

The governing rule is one line: match the index metric to how the embedding model was trained. Most sentence models are trained against cosine, so normalize and use inner product; some retrievers (the dual encoders from the dot-product beat) are trained against dot, so feed them raw. A cosine-trained model scored under raw dot — or vice versa — behaves oddly for no obvious reason. And two numerical guards, because the edge cases will find you: cosine is undefined for a zero vector (the norm is zero, so you divide by zero — add a tiny \(\varepsilon\), or store unit vectors and skip empties), and KL divergence blows up on empty bins (a zero in the denominator) so it needs smoothing before you compare two distributions. Normalize once, store unit vectors, guard the zero — and the geometry stays honest at serving time.

The Knights hold the sphere

So tally the mission. We turned the alien's raw text into countable units — the Tokenosaurus' sub-words, learned by BPE merges, bounded against Heaps' endless tail. Then we built the first Instrument: cosine, which measures meaning by direction, immune to the loudness that fooled Euclidean. And when the Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith tried to melt every distance into mush, the trick held — because on the unit sphere, where only direction survives, the Wraith's concentration loses its grip and Sir Cosine still stands. The machine can finally judge when two meanings are close. First Contact established.

But a question is already glowing on the console. Those vectors — where do their directions actually come from? So far the coordinates were just word counts, and counts don't know that couch and sofa mean the same thing; the Knights can compare directions beautifully, but only if something first points the words the right way. That's where we're headed later in the course. We're going to learn where meaning gets its coordinates — where couch and sofa finally land next to each other on the map: embeddings.
