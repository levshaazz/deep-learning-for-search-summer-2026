Part 1 · From answering to judging

Judging the Oracle

The Oracle learned to speak — now it stands trial. A correct answer and a fabricated one sound identical: both fluent, both confident. How do you grade an Oracle that sounds confident whether it is right or wrong? With no gold answer in hand, we must build the courtroom from scratch — and an old trickster is already waiting in the gallery.

Cold open / hero plate (features Séréga). Catchphrase opens: "How do you grade an Oracle that sounds confident whether it's right or wrong?" Goodhart the Trickster appears in the gallery — foreshadowing his climax. The Oracle (from L10) is now on trial.

Confident either way

a fluent answer reveals nothing about whether it is true

Two answers, one voice

  • Answer A — every claim traces to a retrieved passage. True.
  • Answer B — one sentence is invented, the rest borrowed. False.
  • From the outside — same fluency, same confidence, same citations-shaped prose. Indistinguishable.

The judgment problem

Confidence is not a truth signal — the Oracle has no internal “I'm unsure” light. So the question is not does it sound right? but can we measure whether it is right? Without a measurement, every confident answer is a coin flip we cannot see.

Hook payoff. A fluent answer reveals nothing about truth — A (true) and B (one hallucinated sentence) are indistinguishable from the outside. Sets up the need for measurement, not vibes. Confabulous (L10's phantom) is the unseen cause; here we name the symptom.

Why the old metrics don't fit generation

recall@k graded a ranked list — but there is no key for free-text

What broke

  • No gold answer. For a real question there is rarely one canonical paragraph to diff against.
  • Many right answers. The same fact can be phrased a hundred valid ways — exact-match scores them all zero.
  • Two failure modes at once. An answer can drift off-topic and invent facts; one number can't separate them.

What we need instead

Metrics that grade a generated answer against the retrieved context and the question — not against a reference key. That is exactly the gap RAGAS fills: reference-free scores that decompose RAG quality into faithfulness, relevance, and both sides of retrieval.

problem-no-gold. Classic IR metrics (recall@k, MRR — L1/L9) graded a ranked list against a known relevant set. Generation has no gold answer, many valid phrasings, and two simultaneous failure modes — so exact-match/BLEU don't fit. Motivates reference-free metrics (RAGAS) next.
Act 1 · Grade without a key

RAGAS — four reference-free metrics

If you can't compare to a gold answer, compare to what you retrieved. RAGAS scores a RAG answer with four numbers, no reference required: faithfulness (is it grounded?), answer relevance (does it address the question?), context precision and context recall (did retrieval do its job?). Each is a fraction we can check by hand.

turn-ragas. RAGAS (Es et al., 2023, arXiv:2309.15217): four reference-free metrics. The trick is to grade against the question + retrieved context rather than a gold answer. Each metric is a hand-checkable fraction in data/l11-ragas.json.

The RAGAS scorecard

four metrics, two about generation, two about retrieval
Definition · RAGAS (arXiv:2309.15217)
Four numbers in \([0,1]\), each from the question, retrieved context, and generated answer — no gold reference.
  • ·Faithfulness & answer relevance grade the generation: is every claim supported, and does it address the question?
  • ·Context precision & recall grade the retrieval: are relevant chunks ranked high, and were the needed facts retrieved at all?
  • ·A low number says which stage failed. We compute all four on one example: “How does the heart pump blood?”
ragas-overview. Name the four metrics and split them: two grade generation (faithfulness, answer relevance), two grade retrieval (context precision, recall). The running example "How does the heart pump blood?" carries through the climb + four by-hand slides. Each metric is diagnostic — a low score localises the failure.

Faithfulness, by hand

supported claims ÷ total claims · here, with 4 claims, one hallucination costs a quarter (1/N in general)
$$ \text{faithfulness} = \frac{\#\{\text{claims supported by the context}\}}{\#\{\text{claims in the answer}\}} $$ $$ \text{4 claims: pumps blood }\checkmark,\ \text{contracts }\checkmark,\ \text{systole ejects }\checkmark,\ \text{Harvey 1628 }\times $$ $$ \text{faithfulness} = \frac{3}{4} = \mathbf{0.75} $$

Break the answer into atomic claims, then ask of each: is it entailed by a retrieved passage? Three of four are; the fluent “William Harvey, 1628” is not — nothing we retrieved says it. Here, with four claims, one unsupported sentence drops the score a full quarter (1/N in general). This is the metric that catches hallucination directly.

faithfulness by-hand. l11-ragas: supportedClaims 3 / answerClaimCount 4 = 0.75 (gated). The Harvey claim is the planted hallucination. Lesson: faithfulness = grounding; it is the direct hallucination detector.

Answer relevance, by hand

reverse-engineer questions from the answer · mean cosine to the original
$$ \text{answer relevance} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos\!\big(q,\ \tilde{q}_i\big), \qquad \tilde{q}_i = \text{question the answer seems to answer} $$ $$ \cos\text{ to original } q:\quad [\,0.92,\ 0.88,\ 0.31\,] $$ $$ \text{answer relevance} = \operatorname{mean}[0.92, 0.88, 0.31] = \frac{2.11}{3} = \mathbf{0.7033} $$

Generate the questions the answer could be answering, embed them, and average their cosine to the real question. The first two paraphrases stay on-topic (0.92, 0.88); the third — born of the hallucinated Harvey claim — drifts to 0.31 and drags the mean down. Relevance measures “does the answer address the question?”, not whether it is true. It reads only the question and the answer — never the retrieved context — so it is blind to grounding; and RAGAS additionally penalises noncommittal answers (an evasive “I don’t know” yields off-topic reverse-questions).

answer-relevance by-hand. l11-ragas.reverseQuestionCos [0.92,0.88,0.31]; mean = 0.7033 (gated answerRelevance). The drifting third reverse-question is caused by the hallucinated claim. Relevance ≠ faithfulness: an answer can be on-topic but unfaithful, or faithful but off-topic.

Context precision, by hand

are the relevant chunks ranked high? · precision@k over relevant ranks
$$ \text{context precision} = \frac{1}{\#\text{relevant}} \sum_{k:\,\text{rel}_k} \text{precision@}k $$ $$ \text{ranks: } \underbrace{c_1}_{\text{rel}},\ \underbrace{c_2}_{\text{noise}},\ \underbrace{c_3}_{\text{rel}},\ \underbrace{c_4}_{\text{noise}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{p@1}=\tfrac{1}{1}=1,\ \ \text{p@3}=\tfrac{2}{3} $$ $$ \text{context precision} = \frac{1 + \tfrac{2}{3}}{2} = \mathbf{0.8333} $$

Walk the retrieved list; at each relevant rank, take precision@k (how clean the top-k is so far), then average over the relevant items. The first relevant chunk sits at rank 1 (perfect), but the second is buried under noise at rank 3 (only 2/3 clean). Relevant-but-low-ranked passages cost precision — this grades the retriever's ordering. (Here #relevant = 2 = the relevant chunks that actually appear in the retrieved list; chunks never retrieved are recall's problem, not precision's.)

context-precision by-hand. l11-ragas.precisionAtK: relevant at ranks 1 (p@1=1) and 3 (p@3=0.6667), 2 relevant total → (1 + 2/3)/2 = 0.8333 (gated contextPrecision). Noise at rank 2 between the relevants is what costs precision. Ranking-aware, like MAP from L1.

Context recall, by hand

did retrieval bring back the facts the answer needed?
$$ \text{context recall} = \frac{\#\{\text{ground-truth claims present in the retrieved context}\}}{\#\{\text{ground-truth claims}\}} $$ $$ \text{3 truths: pumps blood }\checkmark,\ \text{systole ejects }\checkmark,\ \text{diastole refills }\times $$ $$ \text{context recall} = \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{0.6667} $$

The mirror of precision: take each ground-truth claim and check whether any retrieved chunk supports it. Two of three are covered; “diastole refills the heart” was never retrieved, so the generator could not have used it. A missing fact caps the best possible answer — the recall floor from L1/L9, now at the context level.

context-recall by-hand. l11-ragas: groundTruthInContext 2 / groundTruthCount 3 = 0.6667 (gated contextRecall). The un-retrieved "diastole" truth is the recall floor: retrieval caps generation. Precision (ranking) and recall (coverage) are the two sides of retrieval quality.

“RAGAS is objective truth”

applicability boundaries — the metrics lean on a model too
What students think

“RAGAS gives reference-free numbers, so the scores are objective ground truth — if faithfulness is 0.75, that is simply how faithful the answer is.”

Every RAGAS metric is computed by an LLM: a model decides which claims are “supported,” generates the reverse-engineered questions, and judges relevance. The fractions are crisp, but the judgments behind them inherit the model's mistakes and biases. RAGAS is a fast, cheap, repeatable proxy — excellent for tracking regressions and comparing systems — but it is not a reference. The score also depends on the decomposition: a different judge model (or temperature) splits the answer into different claims, so faithfulness is not comparable across judge backends — fix the judge model and version before tracking it over time. Calibrate it against human labels on a sample, and never report a single decimal as if it were measured with a ruler. The decomposition is the real gift; the absolute number is a proxy.

Reference-free does not mean judgment-free. The same caveat is about to bite much harder when we let a model grade whole answers head-to-head.

ragas-boundaries misconception. RAGAS metrics are LLM-computed (claim entailment, reverse-question generation, relevance scoring) — fast/cheap/repeatable proxy, not ground truth; calibrate vs human labels. Foreshadows the LLM-as-judge biases (and Goodhart) in Act 2/3.
Act 2 · Hire a judge

LLM-as-a-judge

If grading free text needs judgment, use a model that has it. Hand a strong LLM a rubric and ask it to score answers — or to pick the better of two. It is fast, cheap, and correlates surprisingly well with human raters (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023). It is also the moment we hand Goodhart the verdict pen: the judge is a metric, and metrics can be gamed.

turn-llm-judge. LLM-as-judge (Zheng et al., MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena, arXiv:2306.05685): a model scores or compares answers via a rubric. Strong human correlation — but it is itself a metric, which sets up Goodhart in Act 3.

The judge's rubric

three criteria, a 1–5 scale, one number out
Setup · the rubric we hand the judge
Score each answer on three criteria — relevance, grounding, completeness — on a 1–5 scale, then average to one pointwise score.
  • ·relevance — does it answer the actual question? grounding — is each claim supported by the context? completeness — does it cover what matters?
  • ·The rubric mirrors RAGAS in spirit — relevance ≈ answer relevance, grounding ≈ faithfulness — but here a model reads the answer and assigns the numbers, no fractions to compute.
  • ·A clear rubric is what makes the score reproducible and explainable: the judge must justify each criterion, not emit a vibe. The next slide steps two answers through it.
judge-rubric setup. l11-judge.rubric: criteria relevance/grounding/completeness, scale 1..5. Mirrors RAGAS axes but model-assigned. A clear rubric → reproducible, explainable scoring. Feeds the llm-judge climb.

Pointwise scoring, by hand

average the rubric criteria · then call the winner
$$ \text{score}(x) = \frac{1}{3}\big(\text{relevance} + \text{grounding} + \text{completeness}\big) $$ $$ A = \operatorname{mean}[5, 4, 3] = \frac{12}{3} = \mathbf{4.0} \qquad B = \operatorname{mean}[4, 2, 2] = \frac{8}{3} = \mathbf{2.6667} $$ $$ A\ (4.0) \;>\; B\ (2.6667) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{pairwise winner} = \mathbf{A} $$

Each answer is judged alone, criterion by criterion, then averaged. A is strong across the board (5/4/3); B trails on grounding and completeness (4/2/2). The pointwise gap (4.0 vs 2.6667) and the side-by-side pairwise verdict agree: A wins. Clean and decisive — which is exactly why we are about to distrust it.

pointwise by-hand. l11-judge.answers: A mean[5,4,3]=4.0, B mean[4,2,2]=2.6667; pairwiseWinner A (all gated). Pointwise = judge each alone; pairwise = side-by-side. They agree here. The "too clean" feeling is the setup for the bias slides.

Pointwise vs pairwise · reference-free vs reference-based

two design choices, four corners
mode asks the judge strength weakness use when…
pointwise“score this answer 1–5”one absolute number per answer; cheapthe scale drifts run-to-runtracking one system over time
pairwise“which is better, A or B?”no shared scale to drift; more reliableO(n²) comparisons to rank manycomparing two candidate systems

Pairwise is usually the safer default — a relative verdict has no absolute scale to wander. And if you have gold answers, reference-based judging is stronger; if you don't (the RAG-in-production case), reference-free is what you've got. The choice is about what ground truth you can afford.

Anatomy of a judge prompt

what you actually send the model

The four parts

  • Role + rubric — “You are an expert grader. Score relevance, grounding, completeness 1–5.”
  • The inputs — the question, the retrieved context, and the answer(s) to grade.
  • Reason then score — require a short justification before the number (chain-of-thought lifts agreement).
  • Structured output — JSON per criterion, so scores are parseable and auditable.

Design rules that buy reliability

Make the scale concrete (anchor each point with a description, not just a digit). Demand evidence — “quote the supporting span.” Force a verdict (no “both are good”). And keep the judge model separate from the model being judged, to dodge self-enhancement. Every one of these is a defence against the biases on the next three slides.

judge-prompt anatomy. Four parts: role+rubric, inputs (q/context/answer), reason-before-score (CoT lifts agreement, Zheng et al.), structured JSON output. Design rules: anchored scale, demand evidence/quote, force a verdict, separate judge from judged (self-enhancement). Each defends against the upcoming biases.
Act 3 · The trickster's climax

Goodhart returns

We met him in L1 and again in L4 — now he takes the verdict pen. The instant the judge's score becomes the thing we optimise, models learn to write for the judge, not the reader. Longer, more confident, padded — and the score climbs while quality does not. This is Goodhart's law, and the LLM judge is its purest stage yet.

turn-goodhart — THE VILLAIN'S CLIMAX. Goodhart (L1→L4→here) takes the verdict pen. The metric (judge score) becomes a target → models optimise for the judge (longer/confident/padded), score climbs while quality doesn't. Plate features Goodhart AND Séréga.

“If the judge score went up, the answers got better”

Goodhart the Trickster — the metric becomes a target
What students think

“We optimised our system against the LLM-judge score and it rose from 4.0 to 4.25 — so the new answers are better.”

Watch the flip, on a fresh pair of candidates (not the A/B from the rubric example): an honest answer A and a padded answer C. An honest judge prefers the genuinely better answer: A scores 4.3333, the padded answer C only 4.0. Now over-weight “completeness” — a stand-in for length — and C climbs to 4.25 while A sits at 4.0: the worse, verbose answer wins. Nothing about C got truer; we just rewarded length, and the system learned to pad. The moment a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring quality. Goodhart isn't a hypothetical — he is what every leaderboard climb risks becoming.

A rising score is necessary, not sufficient. Before you trust the climb, prove the judge wasn't the thing you optimised.

catch-goodhart misconception — THE CLIMAX. l11-judge.goodhart: honest A 4.3333 > gamed C 4.0; length-biased C 4.25 > A 4.0 (the flip, both gated). The metric became a target. Goodhart illustration. This resolves the Measure-villain arc opened in L1.

The Goodhart flip, by hand

re-weight one criterion and the winner inverts
$$ \text{honest mean: }\ A = \operatorname{mean}[5, 5, 3] = \mathbf{4.3333} \;>\; C = \operatorname{mean}[4, 3, 5] = \mathbf{4.0} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{winner } A $$ $$ \text{over-weight completeness } \times 2\ (\text{a proxy for length}): $$ $$ A = \frac{5 + 5 + 2\cdot 3}{4} = \mathbf{4.0} \;<\; C = \frac{4 + 3 + 2\cdot 5}{4} = \mathbf{4.25} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{winner } C $$

A fresh pair of candidates here (not the A/B from the rubric slide): an honest answer A and a padded answer C. Honest judging picks A (4.3333 > 4.0). Now treat “completeness” as twice as important — a stand-in for rewarding length — and C, the padded answer, wins (4.25 > 4.0). Nothing about C got truer; we just changed what the score rewards. The metric became a target — Goodhart in three lines of arithmetic.

goodhart-flip by-hand. l11-judge.goodhart: goodScores [5,5,3]→honest A 4.3333, gamedScores [4,3,5]→C 4.0; length-biased (completeness ×2) A 4.0 < C 4.25 (all gated). The flip is the metric becoming a target. Three-line arithmetic version of the catch slide.

Position bias — measured on a real judge

on a genuine tie, the first slot wins · llama3.1:8b
$$ \text{position-follow rate on ties} = \frac{\#\{\text{picks the answer in slot 1}\}}{\#\{\text{tie cases}\}} = \frac{4}{6} = \mathbf{0.6667} $$ $$ \text{but when quality is CLEAR:}\quad \text{accuracy} = \frac{6}{6} = \mathbf{1.0} $$

We froze a real judge (llama3.1:8b, temp 0, seed 42) and fed it genuine ties. It anchored to whichever answer sat in slot 1 two times out of three (0.6667) — a coin that lands heads because of where the answer was printed, not what it said. The same judge is perfect (1.0) when one answer is clearly better. The bias hides exactly where it matters most: the close calls. Position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias were named and measured by Zheng et al. (MT-Bench, 2023); we re-measure them here on a small open judge.

position-bias, measured. l11-judge.real: positionFollowRateTie 0.6667 (4/6 ties anchored to slot 1), accuracyClear 1.0 (6/6) — both gated. Real frozen llama3.1:8b. The bias only bites on ties (the close calls); when quality is clear it's accurate. Mitigation (swap order) on the mitigations slide.

Verbosity bias — the longer answer wins, every time

the killer empirical finding · llama3.1:8b
$$ \text{verbosity-preference rate} = \frac{\#\{\text{picks the longer answer}\}}{\#\{\text{trials}\}} = \frac{10}{10} = \mathbf{1.0} $$ $$ \text{across } 5 \text{ paired prompts} \times 2 \text{ orderings} = 10 \text{ trials},\ \text{the longer answer won } \textbf{every} \text{ time} $$

Held content quality fixed and only varied length: re-measured on a small open judge, it preferred the longer answer in 10 of 10 trials (5 prompts × 2 orderings) — a perfect 1.0 on this probe. Verbosity bias is a strong tendency, not literally always; but here it is Goodhart made measurable: optimise against this judge and your model learns one lesson — write more. Padding is a free win. It is the single most dangerous bias because it rewards exactly the behaviour that erodes a good search answer.

verbosity-bias, measured — THE killer finding (Saito et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.10076 names it). l11-judge.real: verbosityPreferenceRate 1.0, verbosityLongerWins 10 (5 prompts × 2 orderings), gated. Longer wins EVERY time → optimising the judge teaches the model to pad. This is the concrete Goodhart mechanism.

The rest of the judge's blind spots

position and verbosity are not the only ones

More known biases

  • Self-enhancement — a judge rates answers from its own model family higher. Never let a model grade itself.
  • Authority / formatting — confident tone, headings, bullet lists and fake citations read as “quality” regardless of truth.
  • Sycophancy — an answer that agrees with the prompt's framing scores higher than one that corrects it.

The one bias the judge dodged

It is not all bad news. On our planted hallucination — an answer with one invented fact — the same frozen judge caught it and marked the answer down on grounding. So the judge is a real faithfulness signal; it just has thumbs on the scale for length, position, and self. Know which is which: trust the grounding verdict, audit the rest.

other-biases. Self-enhancement (judge favours own family — never self-grade), authority/formatting, sycophancy. But l11-judge.real.faithfulnessCaughtPlanted=true: the same judge DID catch the planted hallucination on grounding. So the judge has real signal (grounding) + thumbs on the scale (length/position/self). Nuanced, not nihilistic.

The order-swap fix, by hand

judge both orderings, keep only the agreements
$$ \text{single ordering on ties: } \text{follow-slot-1 rate} = \frac{4}{6} = \mathbf{0.6667} \quad(\text{position bias}) $$ $$ \text{judge }(A,B)\text{ and }(B,A);\ \ \text{verdict} = \begin{cases} \text{the agreed winner} & \text{if both agree} \\ \text{tie / abstain} & \text{if they disagree} \end{cases} $$

Run the judge twice with the answers in both orders. When position bias would have decided it, the two runs disagree — so we call it a tie instead of crowning slot 1. The biased 0.6667 on ties collapses toward an honest coin flip, at the cost of one extra judge call. The cheapest defence is symmetry.

order-swap mitigation by-hand. l11-judge.real.positionFollowRateTie 0.6667 (gated). Judge (A,B) and (B,A); keep agreements, treat disagreements as ties — symmetrising cancels position bias for the price of one extra call. Concrete version of the mitigations table's top row.

Goodhart's law, formally

the Measure-villain arc resolves (callback L1 → L4)
Law · the trickster, named at last
“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” — Goodhart (Strathern's formulation)
  • ·L1 introduced him with click-through and ranking metrics; L4 showed proxy losses drifting from the goal. Here he reaches the generative era: the judge score is the proxy, and optimising it teaches the model to pad, not to improve.
  • ·The defence is structural: keep evaluation and optimisation honest by holding out human-labelled data, rotating judges, and never reporting a single decimal as truth. A metric you optimise against must be audited, not trusted.
  • ·Every metric in this lecture — RAGAS fractions and judge scores alike — is a proxy. They are indispensable for catching regressions; they are not the territory. Respect the gap, and Goodhart loses his hook.
goodhart-law formalised — the climax callback (NOT a re-intro). Strathern's formulation. Trace L1 (CTR/ranking metrics) → L4 (proxy loss drift) → L11 (judge score as proxy; optimising it → padding). Defence is structural: held-out human labels, rotated judges, no single-decimal-as-truth. All metrics are proxies, not the territory. Resolves the Measure-villain arc.
Act 4 · The Ship critiques itself

Agentic RAG

If we can grade an answer, the Ship can grade its own — mid-flight. Stop treating retrieval as a single shot. Let the model loop: reason about what's missing, act to retrieve it, observe, and repeat until the question is answered. ReAct improvises the loop; Self-RAG and CRAG make it a discipline. The judge moves inside the pipeline.

turn-agentic. The evaluation machinery from Acts 1–3 moves inside the loop: the model grades its own retrieval/output and corrects. ReAct (free-form), Self-RAG (reflection tokens), CRAG (retrieval grader). This is the L10 self-correcting loop, now driven by the agent.

Why a loop, not a single shot

multi-hop questions hide the answer behind a fact you don't have yet

The single-shot wall

  • Two-hop question: “What did the founder of Acme Corp study?” One retrieval can't reach it — you must first learn who the founder is.
  • Stuffing more chunks doesn't help: the bridging fact isn't phrased like the question, so it never ranks.
  • The answer lives behind an intermediate fact the first query can't name.

The agentic fix

Iterate: reason about what's missing → act (retrieve the next fact) → observe → repeat, and grade each result before trusting it. The loop turns a flat retriever into something that plans, checks its own work, and repairs bad retrievals — the same self-correction we built in L10, now driven by the model itself.

self-correcting-overview. The single-shot wall: multi-hop questions need an intermediate fact the first query can't name; more chunks don't help. The fix is the reason→act→observe loop with grading — the agent plans + self-corrects. Motivates the agentic-loop climb (the two-hop "founder of Acme Corp" example).

Why the loop works — recall@1, by hand

the bridging fact only arrives on hop 2
$$ \text{recall@1 by step} = [\,0,\ 0,\ 1\,] $$ $$ \text{step 0 (lookup founder): } 0 \quad\to\quad \text{step 1 (still no education): } 0 \quad\to\quad \text{step 2 (founder} \to \text{study): } \mathbf{1} $$

Track whether the top-1 retrieval holds the answer at each step. The first lookup returns the founder's name — useful, but not what was asked, so recall@1 stays 0. Only after feeding that name into the second lookup does the answer appear: recall@1 jumps to 1. A single shot stops at step 0 (recall 0); the loop closes the chain. But the loop can also fail: a wrong first lookup poisons every later hop, and with no self-grading ReAct can loop forever or hallucinate an Observation — exactly the failure mode Self-RAG/CRAG add a grader to catch.

react-recall by-hand. l11-agentic.react.recallByStep [0,0,1] (gated), hops 2, solved true. recall@1 stays 0 until the bridging fact (founder name) feeds hop 2 — a single-shot retriever would stop at recall 0. The arithmetic behind the agentic-loop climb.

ReAct, formally

interleave reasoning and acting in one trace
Definition · ReAct (arXiv:2210.03629)
Alternate Thought (reason) → Action (call a tool, e.g. lookup[entity]) → Observation (the result), looping until finish[answer].
  • ·Reasoning guides acting: the Thought picks the next retrieval; the Observation updates the next Thought. Reasoning-only can't fetch; acting-only can't plan multi-hop.
  • ·Retrieval is a tool the model calls when needed, not a fixed prelude. The loop is the agent (Yao et al., ICLR 2023).
  • ·Free-form: improvised in natural language, so it works on any LLM — but with no guarantee it grades its own retrievals. Self-RAG and CRAG fix that.
react-formally. Cite Yao et al., "ReAct", ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2210.03629). Thought→Action→Observation loop until finish[]. Reasoning guides acting; retrieval is a tool the model calls. Free-form: works on any LLM but no built-in self-grading → motivates the structured cousins (Self-RAG, CRAG).

Self-RAG, formally

bake the critique in — reflection tokens (callback L10)
Definition · Self-RAG (arXiv:2310.11511)
Train the LM to emit reflection tokens — Retrieve · IsRel · IsSup · IsUse — that gate retrieval and let it grade its own output as it generates.
  • ·Retrieve — decide per segment whether retrieval is even needed (skip what the model already knows).
  • ·IsRel / IsSup — is the passage relevant, and is each claim supported by it? This is the RAGAS faithfulness check, now a token the model emits.
  • ·IsUse — is the response useful (1–5)? The judge from Act 2 is now inside the decoder — Asai et al. (ICLR 2024).
self-rag definition (callback L10). Cite Asai et al., "Self-RAG", ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2310.11511). Reflection tokens Retrieve/IsRel/IsSup/IsUse. IsSup = faithfulness as a token; IsUse = the judge inside the decoder. Contrast with CRAG on the next slide (bolt-on vs baked-in).

the L10 self-correcting loop, now inside the agent (←/→)

CRAG grades each retrieval correct / ambiguous / wrong and branches — refine & use, web-augment, or discard & rewrite. The same loop we built in L10 (rag-control-flow), now the agent's self-correction step. The judging from Acts 1–3 became control flow.

Adaptive-RAG

match the loop's effort to the question's complexity
Definition · Adaptive-RAG (arXiv:2403.14403)
A lightweight complexity classifier routes each query to the cheapest strategy that can answer it: no retrieval · single-step RAG · multi-step (iterative) agentic RAG.
  • ·Simple / known facts → answer directly, no retrieval (don't pay for what the model already knows).
  • ·Single-hop → one retrieve-then-generate pass — the classic loop.
  • ·Multi-hop / compositional → spin up the full ReAct loop (decompose, retrieve, reason, repeat). The router spends agent-loop compute only where the question earns it — Jeong et al. (NAACL 2024).

Figure recaps the structured cousins — Self-RAG & CRAG (slides 39–40) — whose self-correction Adaptive-RAG sits above: here the new idea is the complexity router that decides how much of that machinery to run (no-retrieval / single-step / multi-step).

adaptive-RAG definition. Cite Jeong et al., "Adaptive-RAG", NAACL 2024 (arXiv:2403.14403). Complexity classifier → no-retrieval / single-step / multi-step(agentic). The meta-lesson: match loop effort to question complexity — don't run a multi-step agent on a one-hop fact. Pairs with the don't-over-engineer boundary slide.

Act 5 · Putting it together

Choosing how to judge

Three ways to grade, two axes to grade on, one rule to remember. RAGAS, the LLM judge, and human raters each trade off cost, speed, and trust. Faithfulness vs relevance — two axes, not one

the question they answer is different (callback L10)
Definition · the two independent axes of a RAG answer
Faithfulness: is every claim supported by the context?  ·  Relevance: does the answer address the question?
  • ·They are independent: an answer can be faithful but irrelevant (true but off-topic) or relevant but unfaithful (on-topic but invented — our 0.75 faithfulness, with the Harvey claim).
  • ·RAGAS gives each a number; Self-RAG gives each a token (IsSup = faithfulness, IsUse = usefulness). Grade them separately, report them separately.
  • ·The trap: a confident answer can be high on one axis and zero on the other. Collapse them into one score and you can't tell which way it failed.
faithfulness-vs-relevance (callback L10's deferred axes — now measured). “More loops always help”

applicability boundaries — don't over-agent
What students think

“If a self-correcting loop improves quality, run as many iterations and graders as possible — more reflection must mean more reliability.”

Each loop iteration is another LLM call: more latency, more cost, and another place to fail — a grader can mis-grade, a reflection token can mis-fire, and a loop can wander. For a simple one-hop fact, an agentic loop is pure overhead; Adaptive-RAG exists precisely to not run it. Diagnose the failure first — is it grounding, ranking, coverage, or multi-hop? — then add the one mechanism whose lift matches. And remember Goodhart: more self-grading against a biased judge just teaches the model to game that judge faster. Start simple; earn each loop with a measured, human-anchored win.

The agentic toolkit is a set of scalpels, not a hammer. Match the loop to the question; let the cheapest path that answers it win.

applicability-boundaries misconception (don't over-agent). Each loop = an LLM call (latency/cost/failure point); a one-hop fact needs no agent (Adaptive-RAG). Diagnose first, add the one mechanism. Goodhart callback: more self-grading vs a biased judge → faster gaming. Start simple, human-anchor each win. Pairs with the agentic menu + decision map.

The judge is not the ground truth

the one rule that survives this whole lecture
What students think

“Now that we can auto-grade RAG with RAGAS and an LLM-judge, we don't need humans — the metrics tell us what's true.”

Every number we computed today — the 0.75 faithfulness, the 0.7033 relevance, the judge's 4.0 — is a proxy produced by a model, and models are biased (position 0.6667 on ties, verbosity 1.0). The metrics are indispensable: they let you catch regressions cheaply, diagnose which stage failed, and let the Ship grade itself in the loop. But they are instruments, not the territory. Keep a small human-labelled set as the anchor, report axes separately, and audit any score you optimise against. Used that way, you get the speed of automation and the trust of human judgment — and Goodhart goes home empty-handed.

Measurement made the Oracle accountable — but only if we remember the map is not the territory. That humility is the engineering discipline.

catch — the judge is not ground truth (the lecture's final guard). Every score (0.75 / 0.7033 / 4.0) is a model-made proxy; models are biased (0.6667 / 1.0). Indispensable but instruments, not truth. Keep a human anchor, report axes separately, audit optimised metrics. Resolves the Goodhart arc into a discipline.
