You can't fly past every star to find one.
You need a catalog.
the spine of the course · get data → measure similarity → rank
The catchphrase that opens and closes the lecture. Last time we turned text into tokens and vectors and learned to measure closeness. But a galaxy of documents is too large to compare one by one at query time. The catalog — the inverted index plus a scorer — lets us jump straight to the few records that matter, then rank them. That is classical information retrieval.
Part 01

The Star Catalog

One record in a billion. You cannot read them all at query time.

Serega is a star-cartographer now. The sky is the corpus; each star is a document. A user asks for one specific star out of a billion. He cannot visit each one and check. The astronomer's answer is the same as the search engineer's: build a catalog indexed by what you are looking for, so a query lands you on the right page instantly.

Scanning every document is hopeless

Obstacle · brute force does not scale

A linear scan costs \(O(N)\) per query. At \(N\approx 10^{9}\) documents that is far over any latency budget. The fix is to invert the problem: precompute, for each term, which documents contain it.

The arithmetic kills brute force. A billion documents at a microsecond each is roughly a thousand seconds per query, against a budget of about a hundred milliseconds. You are off by four orders of magnitude. No faster CPU saves you, because the cost grows linearly with the corpus. The only way out is to not look at most documents at all — which is exactly what an index buys you.
Part 02

The Inverted Index

Flip the map: instead of doc → words, store word → which docs.

The reveal. A normal document maps doc to its words. The catalog flips it: for every term, store the list of documents that contain it — a postings list. Now a query term is a single lookup, not a scan. This is the inverted index, the foundation of every full-text search engine for fifty years.

Key term

Definition
Definition
Inverted Indexpostings lists

A map from each term to its postings list — the documents (and often positions / counts) in which that term appears.

"Inverted" because it reverses the natural document → terms map into a terms → documents map. A query term becomes a single dictionary lookup; boolean queries become merges of postings lists. This is the data structure behind Lucene, Elasticsearch, and every classical engine.

Emphasize the inversion. Documents naturally list their words; the index stores the opposite. The term dictionary gives you, in one step, every document containing a word — and only those. Postings often also store the term frequency and positions, which BM25 and phrase queries need. Build it once offline; query it millions of times.

"
Matching is binary. Relevance is a spectrum.
Stop asking "does it contain the word?" Start asking "how well does it fit?"
from boolean retrieval to ranked retrieval
The pivot of the lecture. Boolean retrieval answers yes or no, and an empty result set is a real, common failure. Ranked retrieval scores every document that contains any query term and sorts them, so the best partial match still surfaces. That score is what TF-IDF and BM25 provide. The rest of the lecture is about computing that score well.
Part 03

Scoring: TF-IDF → BM25

We have which docs match. Now put them in the right order.

BM25 is Serega's brass sextant — the instrument that measures how well a document fits a query. We build up to it: first the intuition of term frequency and inverse document frequency, then the two fixes BM25 adds — saturating term frequency and normalizing for document length. Every number on the coming slides is computed on the same 8-doc corpus, so the ranking is real.

TF-IDF: frequent in the doc, rare in the corpus

the first ranking weight
$$ w(t,d) = \underbrace{tf(t,d)}_{\text{term frequency}} \cdot \underbrace{\operatorname{idf}(t)}_{\text{rarity}}, \qquad \operatorname{idf}(t) = \ln\!\Big(\frac{N - df(t) + 0.5}{df(t) + 0.5} + 1\Big) $$
\(tf(t,d)\)
Term frequency
count of \(t\) in \(d\)
\(df(t)\)
Doc frequency
docs containing \(t\)
\(N\)
Corpus size
here \(N=8\)

On our corpus both query terms have \(df=4\), so \(\operatorname{idf}(\texttt{space})=\operatorname{idf}(\texttt{team})\approx 0.69\). A word in half the docs is only mildly informative; a word in one doc would score far higher.

Two intuitions. Term frequency: a document that uses the query word more is probably more about it. Inverse document frequency, due to Spärck Jones: a word that appears in almost every document tells you little, so down-weight it. The smoothed idf here is the Lucene / BM25 form. On this small corpus both query terms appear in exactly four of eight documents, so their idf is identical at about 0.69 — which is convenient, because it means the ranking is driven entirely by term frequency and length, the very things BM25 handles cleverly. The weakness of plain TF-IDF: term frequency enters linearly, so a doc that repeats a word ten times scores ten times as high, which is rarely ten times as relevant.

BM25 fixes exactly these two things next.

BM25: saturating TF + length normalization

the workhorse of classical IR
$$ \operatorname{BM25}(q,d) = \sum_{t\in q} \operatorname{idf}(t)\cdot \frac{tf\,(k_1+1)}{tf + k_1\big(1 - b + b\,\tfrac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\big)} $$
\(k_1\)
TF saturation
here \(k_1=1.5\)
\(b\)
Length penalty
here \(b=0.75\)
\(\tfrac{|d|}{\mathrm{avgdl}}\)
Relative length
\(\mathrm{avgdl}\approx 50.6\)

As \(tf\to\infty\) the fraction tends to \(k_1+1\): extra occurrences add less and less. Setting \(b=0\) removes the length penalty; \(b=1\) applies it fully.

Read the formula as TF-IDF with two repairs. The numerator and denominator together form a saturating function of term frequency: as tf grows the ratio approaches k1 plus 1, so the first occurrence matters most and the tenth barely moves the score. The k1 parameter, here 1.5, sets how fast it saturates. The b term, here 0.75, scales term frequency by how long the document is relative to the average length avgdl, which is about 50.6 tokens here — a long document no longer wins just by accumulating words. Robertson and Zaragoza derive this from the probabilistic relevance framework; in practice these two fixes are why BM25 beats TF-IDF on almost every benchmark.

Diminishing returns on term frequency

TF-IDF is linear · BM25 saturates

Going from \(tf=1\) to \(tf=2\) adds a lot; from \(tf=9\) to \(tf=10\) adds almost nothing. TF-IDF would keep climbing linearly forever. Saturation is the single most important difference between the two scorers.

A picture of saturation. TF-IDF is a straight line through the origin — the hundredth occurrence is worth the same as the first. BM25 is concave and bounded: it rises steeply for the first few occurrences and then flattens toward a ceiling proportional to k1 plus 1. This matches intuition — once a document clearly is about a topic, more repetitions tell you little. Combined with length normalization from the previous slides, this is why BM25 has stayed the strong classical baseline for decades.
Part 04

Rank Fusion

One ranker isn't enough. Combine several votes into one robust list.

Different rankers see different things — BM25 looks at exact terms, a vector cosine looks at direction in embedding space. Each produces its own ranked list, on its own incomparable score scale. Rather than trust one, we fuse them: a document that several rankers like should rise. The robust, parameter-light way to do this is Reciprocal Rank Fusion.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion

combine by rank, not by score
$$ \operatorname{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r} \frac{1}{k + \operatorname{rank}_r(d)}, \qquad k = 60 $$
\(\operatorname{rank}_r(d)\)
Position of \(d\)
in ranker \(r\)'s list (1 = top)
\(k\)
Damping constant
standard value 60
\(\sum_r\)
Sum over
all rankers

It uses only the rank, never the raw score — so it fuses BM25 and cosine even though their scales are incomparable. The constant \(k\) softens the dominance of the very top positions.

The key idea, from Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher 2009: throw away the raw scores and keep only the position each ranker assigns. A document at rank 1 contributes one over k plus one; at rank 2, one over k plus two; and so on. Summing these reciprocal ranks across rankers rewards documents that several systems place near the top, and it is immune to the fact that BM25 scores and cosine scores live on totally different scales. The constant k, conventionally 60, prevents the first position from completely dominating. Simple, no training, and remarkably hard to beat — it is the default fusion method in modern hybrid search.

Common misconception

Bust the myth
What students think

“BM25 understands what the query means.”

BM25 matches exact terms, not meaning. A query for couch scores zero on a document that only says sofa — same concept, different string, no overlap. This is the lexical gap: classical IR is blind to synonyms, paraphrase, and spelling variants.

The Lexical Gremlin. It wedges a wall between couch and sofa so no postings list ever connects them. Stemming and query expansion patch a few cases, but the wall stands. To tear it down we need embeddings — vectors where couch and sofa sit close even though the strings differ. That is the next lecture.

The catch beat. Students over-trust BM25 because it works so well on exact terms. But it is a string matcher with clever weighting — it has no notion of meaning. Couch and sofa share no characters, so they share no postings, so BM25 scores the match at zero. The Lexical Gremlin is the cartoon villain who keeps the two words walled apart. Classical patches — stemming, synonym dictionaries, query expansion — help at the margins but do not generalize. The real fix is dense embeddings, where semantically close words have close vectors regardless of spelling. That is precisely why the course moves to embeddings next, and it is the headline weakness every neural retriever is designed to beat.
