Key term

Definition
Definition
Natural Language ProcessingNLP

The field concerned with getting computers to process, understand, and generate human language.

It sits between linguistics and machine learning: the raw material is messy human text, and the goal is to make it computable — and useful — for tasks like search, translation, and question answering.

NLP is our toolkit for the first-contact problem. Note the three verbs: process (clean it), understand (represent meaning), generate (produce language back). Today is mostly about the first two.

Pixels interpolate; symbols do not

Obstacle 1 · discreteness

A grey halfway between black and white is meaningful; a "word halfway between cat and dog" is not. Symbols have no inherent metric — that is the core motivation for representation learning.

This is the deepest obstacle. Pixel space is continuous and already has a metric; you can interpolate and the result is valid. Symbol space is discrete: "cat" and "dog" share no letters with their meanings, and there is no valid point "between" them. So we cannot read distance off the strings — we must manufacture a vector space where similar meanings sit close. Hold that thought; it is exactly Part 4.

Ambiguity lives at every level

Obstacle 2 · one string, many meanings
  • ·Lexical — "bank" (river / money); "bat" (animal / sport).
  • ·Syntactic / attachment — "I saw the man with the telescope." Who has the telescope?
  • ·Semantic / scope — "Every student read a book." One book, or one each?
  • ·Referential — the Winograd schema below.
!
Winograd schema

"The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." — it = the trophy.

Flip one word: "…because it was too small." — now it = the suitcase.

The string barely changed; the meaning of the pronoun flipped. Resolving this needs world knowledge, not just the symbols.

Ambiguity is not an edge case; it is the normal state of language. The Winograd schema is the cleanest demo: a single adjective flip moves the referent. No purely lexical system can solve it — you need a model of the world (big things don't fit in small containers). This is why "understanding" is hard and why context-sensitive embeddings matter later.

Zipf's law: a few words do all the work

Obstacle 3 · the long tail

The frequency of the \(r\)-th most common word is \(\propto r^{-1}\). On 20 Newsgroups (~3.7 M tokens), the empirical log–log slope is −1.02: the top 10 words cover 20.6% of the corpus, the top 100 cover 47.3%, and the top 1000 cover 71.4% — leaving a vast tail of words seen once (hapax legomena).

Zipf is steep. "the", "of", "and" eat enormous probability mass; then a power-law tail of rare words, most of which appear only once in a given corpus. The slope and coverage numbers cited here are not invented — they are computed by `_research/data/zipf.py` on sklearn's 20 Newsgroups corpus (public domain, headers/footers/quotes stripped). Empirical slope on the top-1000 is −1.018, which is textbook Zipf within rounding. Practical consequence: you can never collect enough text to see every word "enough" times. This is the empirical setup for the punchline two slides on.

Heaps' law: the vocabulary never saturates

The argument for subwords

As you read more text \(N\), the number of distinct types \(V\) keeps growing as \(V \propto N^{\beta}\) — sublinear, but unbounded. Measured on 3.66 M tokens of 20 Newsgroups: \(\beta \approx 0.59\), still 94 287 distinct types and climbing (\(R^2=0.998\)). No finite word list ever covers everything.

Heaps and Zipf are two views of the same fact. The punchline — say it loud: a fixed WORD vocabulary can never cover real text. New names, typos, compounds, hashtags, code identifiers arrive forever. That is the direct, non-negotiable argument for SUBWORD tokenization: bound the vocabulary, and rebuild any unseen word from pieces. Everything in Part 2 follows from this slide. Numbers grounded by _research/data/heaps.py: β≈0.59, K≈12.7, R²=0.998, 94 287 types on 3.66 M tokens of 20 Newsgroups (same corpus as the Zipf slide — the type counts match).

"Split on spaces" is an English-centric assumption

Obstacle 4 · morphology & the world's scripts

Morphology stacks meaning

Turkish, agglutinative:

i
evlerinizden = ev + ler + iniz + den

"from your houses" — one written word, four morphemes. A word vocabulary would need a separate entry for every such combination.

No canonical word boundaries

  • ·~7000 languages; many scripts; right-to-left (Arabic, Hebrew).
  • ·Chinese has no spaces: 今天我很高兴 ("I am happy today") — segmentation is learned and ambiguous.
  • ·Thai: no spaces. Japanese: mixes kanji / hiragana / katakana.
  • ·Even English: "New York", "can't", URLs, #hashtags.
Most of the planet does not put a space between every meaning-unit. Agglutinative languages glue morphemes; CJK and Thai have no spaces at all. So "tokenize = split on whitespace" is parochial. This pushes us toward a method that learns its units from data and works on any script — which is exactly what subword tokenizers (especially SentencePiece) do.

Normalization: invisible differences that break matching

Unicode · case · accents

Same glyph, different bytes

!
"é" has two encodings

NFC: U+00E9 (one code point). NFD: e + U+0301 (combining accent). Visually identical; byte-different → they fail to match.

Forms: NFC (compose, storage), NFD (decompose), NFKC/NFKD (fold look-alikes: "ﬁ"→"fi", full-width "Ａ"→"A", "²"→"2"; aggressive, lossy).

The over-normalization trap

  • ·Lowercasing collapses "US" (country) and "us" (pronoun); "Apple" and "apple".
  • ·NFKC rewrites chemistry / math superscripts (\(x^{2}\) → "x2") — destroys signal.
  • ·Modern LLM tokenizers (GPT-2/4) do almost no normalization (byte-level, case-preserving). BERT-uncased lowercases + strips accents.

Pattern: NFC + minimal folding for generation; heavy NFKC + casefold only for lexical search-index keys.

Normalization is a double-edged knife. Too little and "café" written two Unicode ways never matches; too much and you erase the very distinctions you need (U.S. vs us, code, superscripts). The right amount depends on the job: a generation model preserves nearly everything; a BM25 search index folds aggressively because recall beats precision on surface form. This tension recurs in Part 3 when we discuss query/doc mismatch.

Stemming vs. lemmatization

collapsing word forms to a root

Stemming

A crude, rule-based chopping of suffixes (e.g. the Porter stemmer). Fast and language-light — but the output is a stem, not always a real word.

i
studies → studi · caring → car

Can over-merge: universe, university both → univers.

Lemmatization

Maps a word to its dictionary lemma using a vocabulary + morphology (often with the part of speech). Slower, but the output is always a valid word.

i
studies → study · drove → drive · better → good

Needs POS: saw → see (verb) or saw (noun).

Both shrink open / opened / opens / opening to one form so a query matches every variant — the classic fix for the lexical gap. Sparse retrieval (BM25) still relies on them; neural / subword models usually skip them and let the tokenizer + embeddings handle morphology.

The two classic ways to normalize word forms (after the character-level normalization on the previous slide). Stemming is a fast heuristic that just lops off endings — cheap, but it can produce non-words and over-merge unrelated terms. Lemmatization is principled: it uses a lexicon and morphology (and ideally the POS tag) to return the true dictionary form. The payoff is the same — a search for "running" should match "ran" — which is exactly the lexical-gap problem from Lecture 1. Key modern caveat: deep models rarely stem or lemmatize; subword tokenization plus learned embeddings absorb morphology, so this lives mostly on the classical-IR/BM25 side of the course.

Part 02

Breaking the code:
Tokenization

The problem: a string is one long blob. The fix: cut it into units the model can reuse.

Rocky and Grace cannot trade whole speeches — they trade atoms of meaning and rebuild from there. A machine needs the same: a finite, reusable inventory of pieces. How we cut decides everything downstream.

Key term

Definition
Definition
Token · Tokenizationtoken · tokenization

Tokenization splits raw text into tokens — the atomic units the model consumes.

A token may be a word, a subword, or a single character / byte. The choice of granularity is a design decision with real trade-offs — that is the next few slides.

Stress that a "token" is not the same as a "word". This trips students constantly. The model never sees words — it sees a sequence of token ids, whatever we defined a token to be. Rule of thumb for English: roughly 4 characters per token, ~0.75 words per token (100 tokens ≈ 75 words).

Tokens become integer ids

"students opened their books"

"students opened their books" → tokens → ids 11 | 298 | 34 | 567. The id is just a lookup index; the meaning is in the embedding it points to.

Concrete and reassuring: tokenization plus a vocabulary is just a dictionary from token-string to integer. The integers are arbitrary — what matters is that the same token always gets the same id, so the embedding lookup is consistent. The semantics live entirely in the embedding vectors, which we learn later.

The granularity trade-off triangle

vocab size ↔ sequence length ↔ OOV

Real vocab sizes: GPT-2 50,257; cl100k (GPT-3.5/4) ~100k; o200k (GPT-4o) ~200k; BERT 30,522; LLaMA / T5 (SentencePiece) 32k. English ≈ 4 chars/token.

You cannot win on all three corners. Push toward characters: vocabulary shrinks to ~256 bytes and OOV vanishes, but sequences blow up ~4–5×, which is brutal under O(n²) attention. Push toward words: sequences are short, but the vocabulary is unbounded (Heaps) and OOV is severe. Subword sits in the interior — a bounded vocab of 30k–200k, moderate sequence length, near-zero OOV. Bigger vocab → fewer tokens per text → shorter sequences and lower cost, but a bigger embedding matrix and softmax.

Walk it one row at a time. Character-level: trivially no OOV, but a 20-word sentence becomes 100+ tokens and the model must learn spelling. Word-level wastes the vocabulary — and still breaks

inflection blow-up · OOV → [UNK]

Word-level treats open / opened / opens / opening as four unrelated types, yet still collapses anything unseen to [UNK]. Subword fixes both at once.

Two complaints in one slide. First, morphological waste: the four forms of "open" share an obvious stem, but word-level gives them independent embeddings, so the model relearns "open-ness" four times. Second, the OOV cliff: a rare Welsh place name has no entry and collapses to UNK — all information lost. Subword pieces ("open" + "##ed") solve both: shared stems, and unseen words rebuilt from known pieces.

Key term

Definition
Definition
Byte-Pair EncodingBPE

Start from single characters; repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol, recording each merge rule, until the vocabulary reaches a target size.

Origin: Philip Gage, "A New Algorithm for Data Compression" (1994). Brought to NLP by Sennrich, Haddow & Birch (2016) for machine translation. Greedy, frequency-driven, deterministic.

BPE began as a compression trick: replace the most common byte pair with an unused byte, repeat. Sennrich realized the same loop gives an open-vocabulary tokenizer for NMT. Two phases: TRAIN (learn an ordered list of merges from a corpus), and ENCODE (apply those merges in order to any new text). The next two slides are worked examples of the training loop. Picture Tokenosaurus — the friendly dino who snips long words into reusable chunks; BPE is just his rule for deciding where to bite.

Byte-level BPE: UNK becomes impossible

GPT-2 and friends

Start from 256 bytes

There are ~138,000 Unicode code points — too many to enumerate. GPT-2 instead starts BPE from the 256 raw bytes and merges upward.

i
Any byte sequence is representable

Emoji, Chinese, source code, binary garbage — all decode to bytes, so [UNK] is mathematically impossible.

Whitespace as a token

A regex pre-tokenizer keeps the leading space attached, so " the" is a single token distinct from "the".

This makes encoding reversible (you can reconstruct the exact bytes) and is why a trailing space can change a model's behaviour — a corner case we return to in Part 3.

Byte-level BPE is the elegant fix to OOV: by grounding the base vocabulary in bytes, the worst case for any input is "fall back to bytes", never UNK. The leading-space trick (" the" vs "the") preserves reversibility and word boundaries without a language-specific pre-tokenizer. Remember the trailing-space gotcha — it comes straight from this design.

Common misconception

Bust the myth
What students think

“A word has one correct BPE tokenization.”

In principle a word can be cut many ways — "linear" could be li + near or li + n + ea + r; "algebra" could be al + ge + bra or al + g + e + bra. BPE makes it deterministic only by fixing the order of learned merges and applying them greedily. Change the corpus (and thus the merge order) and the segmentation changes. The tokenization is a property of the trained merge table, not of the word.

Worked example. Word: "singer".
Merge table A: {e+r→er, sin+g→sing}  →  [sing, er] (2 tokens).
Merge table B: {s+i→si, n+g→ng}  →  [si, ng, e, r] (4 tokens).
Same word, two trained tokenizers, different segmentation, different token count, different model inputs.

Students assume segmentation is intrinsic to the word. It is not — it is induced by the ordered merge list. BPE breaks the ambiguity by always applying merges in the order they were learned, greedily. This matters: two models with different tokenizers will cut the same word differently, which is one reason you cannot mix tokenizers across models, and it foreshadows Unigram's probabilistic, multi-segmentation view.

WordPieceBERT's tokenizer

Like BPE, but it chooses each merge by a likelihood score rather than raw frequency, and marks word-internal pieces with a ## continuation prefix.

Schuster & Nakajima (2012), for Japanese/Korean voice search; adopted by BERT. "playing" → play + ##ing; "tokenization" → token + ##ization.

WordPiece is BPE's cousin. The single conceptual difference: instead of merging the most FREQUENT pair, it merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram model — i.e. pairs that co-occur more than chance. The ## marker tells the model "this piece continues the previous word", which preserves word boundaries for tasks like NER. Next slide: the exact score.

Dividing by the marginal counts favours pairs that co-occur more than chance — a common piece like ##s is penalised unless it sticks to one specific neighbour.

Read it as pointwise mutual information without the log. A pair of two very common pieces (say "th" + "e") has a big numerator but an even bigger denominator, so its score is modest. A pair where a rare piece always follows a specific piece scores high. This is why WordPiece tends to keep meaningful morphemes together. Note: some textbook variants multiply by |V|; with our toy corpus |V| works out so the headline scores are 29, 30, 31.

WordPiece on unseen words

reuse known pieces, else [UNK]

Greedy longest-match recycles pieces from training (jumps → ju ##mp ##s reused for "leaps"); whatever matches nothing collapses to [UNK].

This contrasts WordPiece with byte-level BPE. WordPiece can still emit [UNK] when no subword matches — its base alphabet is characters of the training data, not all bytes. But notice the partial win: "leaps" wasn't seen, yet its pieces ju/##mp/##s were learned from "jumps", so it is handled. The fully unseen words here (fastest, hare, sleepy, cat) fall to [UNK] in this tiny corpus.

Unigram LM & SentencePiece

prune, don't merge

Top-down, probabilistic

Kudo (2018): start from a large seed vocabulary, fit a unigram language model, then iteratively prune the tokens whose removal least hurts corpus likelihood — until the target size.

Opposite direction to BPE/WordPiece (which build up). Enables subword regularization: sample alternative segmentations as data augmentation.

SentencePiece, the wrapper

  • ·Treats whitespace as an ordinary character, rendered ▁ (U+2581): "Hello world" → ▁Hello ▁world.
  • ·Fully reversible and language-agnostic — no pre-tokenizer, so it works on CJK and Thai out of the box.
  • ·Used by T5, ALBERT, XLNet, LLaMA, Mistral.
Two ideas that are often conflated. Unigram is an ALGORITHM (prune a big vocab by likelihood loss). SentencePiece is a TOOLKIT that can run either BPE or Unigram and, crucially, treats the space as a normal symbol (▁), which removes the English-centric "split on spaces" assumption from Part 1. That reversibility is why SentencePiece dominates multilingual models.

The crispest summary of Part 2's machinery. All three end up with a bounded subword vocabulary; they differ in how they choose the inventory and how they mark continuations. If you remember one thing: frequency vs likelihood-gain vs likelihood-loss.

Part 03

Where tokenizers
break

The clever scheme has sharp edges — digits, code, other languages, glitch tokens.

Tokenization looks solved until you poke it. This part is the most memorable: every corner case is a real failure that costs accuracy, money, or fairness. The thread: a tokenizer is only as good as the distribution it was trained on, and it quietly imposes that distribution on everything downstream.

Numbers: a frequency artifact, not logic

why LLMs are bad at arithmetic

Train a byte-level BPE on 20 Newsgroups and the literal split shifts with vocab size: at 1k vocab "327" → 3 | 2 | 7 (3 tokens); at 4k and 16k, "327" → 32 | 7 (2 tokens). Place values stop lining up — and arithmetic suffers.

This is one of the most counter-intuitive results for students: LLMs are bad at arithmetic partly because of TOKENIZATION, not just reasoning. The split shown is not invented — it is the literal output of `_research/data/bpe_merges.py`, which trains a huggingface byte-level BPE on sklearn's 20 Newsgroups at three vocab sizes and records how "327" gets sliced. LLaMA and PaLM force single-digit tokens to fix this; Singh et al. 2024 show right-to-left comma grouping helps, and multiplication collapses to ~0 accuracy by about six digits.

Code & whitespace

why GPT-4 got better at code

Indentation is semantic

In Python, leading whitespace is the block structure. Early tokenizers spent one token per space, fragmenting indented code and wasting context.

i
Space-run tokens

GPT-3.5/4 added single tokens for runs of 4 / 8 / 16 spaces — a concrete reason they handle code far better.

Format choice = token cost

YAML usually needs fewer tokens than JSON for the same data — less punctuation (no braces, fewer quotes/commas).

Prompt-engineering tip: for token-tight prompts, prefer YAML or compact formats; every saved token is cheaper inference and more usable context.

Two practical levers. First, indentation: if every space is its own token, a deeply nested function explodes in length and the model loses structure — so adding multi-space tokens directly improved code ability. Second, serialization format matters: JSON's braces, quotes and commas cost tokens that YAML avoids. These are cheap wins once you know the tokenizer is the bottleneck.

One glyph, many tokens

emoji ZWJ & CJK

A single emoji can be several byte tokens (joined by zero-width joiners); one Chinese character is typically 2–3 byte-level tokens. Visual atom ≠ token atom.

"One character = one token" is a comfortable lie. Emoji are sequences of code points glued by zero-width joiners; a family emoji is really man+ZWJ+woman+ZWJ+girl. And under UTF-8, a Chinese character is 3 bytes, so byte-level BPE spends 2–3 tokens on it. This directly feeds the fertility / token-tax slide coming up.

Compounds & non-concatenative morphology

German · Arabic

German compounds

Donaudampfschifffahrts­gesellschaftskapitän

→ Donau ##dampf ##schiff ##fahrt ##gesellschaft ##kapitän

One orthographic word, six morphemes ("Danube steamship company captain"). Subwords recover the parts a word vocabulary would miss.

Arabic: root + pattern

The triliteral root k-t-b ("write") interleaves with patterns:

  • ·kataba — he wrote
  • ·kattaba — he made (someone) write
  • ·iktataba — he subscribed

Meaning is woven through the root, not appended — so simple left-to-right merging fits poorly.

Two stress tests for subword merging. German concatenates freely, so words can be arbitrarily long — subwords handle this gracefully by recovering morphemes. Arabic is non-concatenative: the same root k-t-b appears with vowels and affixes woven inside it, so the "shared piece" is discontinuous and contiguous-merge tokenizers can't capture it cleanly. This is a known weak spot for BPE/WordPiece on Semitic languages.

The multilingual "token tax"

fertility = tokens per word

Same content, more tokens in many languages. Train a 16k BPE on 20 Newsgroups (mostly English) and the word «привет» (Russian "hello") takes 12 byte-level tokens; English "tokenization" takes 3. \(2\times\) fertility → roughly \(4\times\) training cost (\(O(n^{2})\)), ~\(2\times\) API price and latency, and a smaller effective context window.

This is a fairness story, not just an efficiency one. Fertility is tokens-per-word. A tokenizer trained mostly on English is cheap for English and expensive for Hindi, Telugu, Turkish — and Russian. The fertility numbers in the caption are not hand-waved: `_research/data/bpe_merges.py` trains a 16k-vocab byte-level BPE on 20 Newsgroups and reports the exact token-counts for English vs Russian demo strings. The Token Tax (2025) work: each extra token/word costs roughly 8–18 accuracy points and explains 20–50% of cross-lingual accuracy variance. Because attention is O(n²), 2× fertility is ~4× training compute; at inference it is ~2× the API bill, ~2× latency, and less room in the context window. Low-resource speakers literally pay more for worse service on identical information.

Common misconception

Bust the myth
What students think

“Every token in the vocabulary has a sensible meaning.”

Some tokens are glitch tokens — e.g. SolidGoldMagikarp (a scraped Reddit username). It earned a vocabulary slot because it appeared while training the tokenizer, but was rare or absent when training the model, so its embedding stayed essentially untrained. Prompting it produces bizarre behaviour: the model repeats "distribute", refuses, or hallucinates. Lesson: the tokenizer's vocabulary and the model's training data must share the same distribution.

Concrete anchor. GPT-2 BPE token id 28666. Rumbelow & Watkins (LessWrong, Feb 2023) documented that asking GPT-3.5 to repeat the string would return "distribute" or other nonsense; the same vocabulary entry is inert in many later models because the username vanished from filtered training data.

A vivid, famous bug. The tokenizer and the language model are trained on different (or differently filtered) corpora. A string can win a token slot during tokenizer training, then almost never appear during model training — leaving a token whose embedding never moved from initialization. Feed it in and the model behaves erratically. The takeaway is general: distribution mismatch between vocabulary and training data creates dead, dangerous tokens.

The model sees tokens, not letters

trailing space · spelling

Trailing-space sensitivity

" the" and "the" are different tokens. A prompt ending in a trailing space lands off-distribution and degrades the completion.

!
Watch your prompt endings

Trim trailing whitespace unless you specifically want the "continuation" token.

Why spelling is hard

"How many R's in strawberry?" and "reverse this string" are hard because the model never sees individual letters — only opaque token chunks like straw + berry.

Character-level operations require the model to have memorized the letter content of each token. It is not looking at letters at run time.

Both quirks come from the same fact: the model's atoms are tokens, not characters. " the" ≠ "the" because the leading-space token is a distinct id (byte-level BPE design from earlier). And counting letters in "strawberry" fails because the model sees "straw"+"berry", not s-t-r-a-w-... — it would have to have learned the spelling of each token. This is why letter-counting and string-reversal trip up otherwise strong models.

Why tokenization matters for search

query / doc mismatch

Vocabulary mismatch

  • ·Under exact BM25, query "ensembling" won't match document "ensemble" — plurals and tenses miss.
  • ·"car" ≠ "automobile"; BM25 needs per-language tokenization, stop-words, and stemming.

Cross-lingual unit mismatch

"euthanasia" is one BM25 term, but XLM-R splits it into several subwords. Sparse (BM25) and neural retrievers then disagree on units → hurts hybrid fusion.

Multilingual fertility (Part 3) also drives truncation risk and uneven recall — a search-fairness problem across languages.

This is the explicit bridge to the IR weeks. Sparse retrieval (BM25) matches surface terms, so it is acutely sensitive to tokenization, stemming and stop-words — "ensembling" vs "ensemble" is a real miss. Worse, sparse and dense systems often tokenize differently (one BM25 term vs many subwords), so when you fuse them the units don't line up. And fertility means non-English queries are more likely to be truncated and under-retrieved. Tokenization is not a preprocessing detail; it shapes recall.

How the three relate

on the unit sphere they agree
$$ \lVert x-y\rVert^2 = \lVert x\rVert^2 + \lVert y\rVert^2 - 2\,x\cdot y \;\xrightarrow{\;\lVert x\rVert=\lVert y\rVert=1\;}\; 2\,(1-\cos\theta) $$

On L2-normalized vectors, ranking by cosine \(\uparrow\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) dot \(\uparrow\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) squared-Euclidean \(\downarrow\) — identical nearest-neighbour ordering.

But NOT unit-norm: \(a=(1,1),b=(10,10)\) → \(\cos=1\) (identical direction) yet \(\lVert a-b\rVert=\sqrt{162}\approx 12.73\). Magnitude is where they part ways.

The single most important relationship in vector search. Expand the squared distance; on the unit sphere it collapses to 2(1−cosθ), so cosine, dot, and (negative) Euclidean produce the SAME ranking — which is precisely why systems L2-normalize and then run a fast dot-product index. The counterexample shows where it breaks: a=(1,1), b=(10,10) are perfectly aligned (cos=1) but Euclidean says they are ~12.73 apart. cos = 20/(√2·√200) = 20/√400 = 20/20 = 1; ‖a−b‖ = √(81+81) = √162 ≈ 12.73. Both numbers are computed independently by `_research/data/cosine.py` (cosine_examples.json) so the worked arithmetic in this slide is reproducible. The same script also shows the effect on real text: TF-IDF on two 20-Newsgroup sci.space posts vs one rec.sport.hockey post — the same-topic cosine is HIGHER than the cross-topic cosine, which is the punchline of every dense-retrieval lecture.

Jaccard, MinHash & SimHash

the sparse / set regime

\(J(A,B)=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|\in[0,1]\); Jaccard distance \(1-J\) is a true metric. MinHash estimates \(J\) for set dedup (collision probability \(=J\)); SimHash estimates cosine for dense near-duplicates.

For sets and bags — shingled documents, user-item interactions, categorical features — Jaccard is the natural measure. The beautiful part is hashing: MinHash (Broder, AltaVista) builds signatures so that the probability two sketches collide equals their Jaccard, giving sublinear web-scale dedup still used to clean LLM training data. SimHash (Charikar) is the cosine analogue via random hyperplanes. Rule of thumb: MinHash ↔ Jaccard (sets); SimHash ↔ cosine (dense).

If a student wants the grid, the bottom-right cell of the 6×8 DP table is 3.

Sparse sets vs dense embeddings

which measure, when

Sparse / sets

  • ·Shingled documents, user-item pairs, categorical features.
  • ·Jaccard + MinHash for dedup; LSH banding → sublinear near-duplicate search.
  • ·You care which terms appear, not how dense the vector is.

Dense embeddings

  • ·Learned sentence / document vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\).
  • ·Cosine (most sentence models) or dot (trained dual encoders) + ANN (HNSW, IVF-PQ).
  • ·Magnitude may carry signal — see the dot-vs-cosine debate ahead.
The practical fork. Sparse, high-dimensional, mostly-zero set representations want Jaccard and hashing (MinHash/SimHash + LSH) — that is how web-scale dedup and classic shingling work. Dense, low-dimensional learned embeddings want cosine or dot plus approximate nearest neighbour indexes. Picking the wrong family (e.g. cosine on raw sparse counts, or Jaccard on dense floats) gives meaningless rankings.

When high dimensions
break intuition

Concentration · hubness · anisotropy — why naive nearest-neighbour can mislead.

A short, sharp coda. The measures are clean in 2D; in the hundreds-to-thousands of dimensions where real embeddings live, three effects quietly degrade them. The knights' nemesis appears here — the Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith, who flattens every distance until far and near look the same. Knowing these three effects separates someone who can plug in cosine from someone who can debug a retrieval system.

Distance concentration

curse of dimensionality

As \(d\to\infty\), the spread of pairwise distances collapses: nearest and farthest points become almost equidistant, so "nearest neighbour" loses meaning (Beyer et al. 1999). Affects all \(L_p\).

Concentration of measure: in very high dimensions, the histogram of distances from a query to all points narrows to a spike. The ratio (max−min)/min tends to zero, so the nearest neighbour is barely closer than the farthest — and tiny perturbations flip which point "wins". This is why high-d retrieval is fragile and why lower-p norms (Aggarwal 2001) retain a bit more contrast (though Mirkes 2020 shows fractional norms don't truly cure it).

Hubness

a few points in everyone's kNN list

In high \(d\), the distribution of k-occurrences becomes right-skewed: a few hubs appear in almost everyone's nearest-neighbour list; anti-hubs in none (Radovanović et al. 2010).

A subtler high-d pathology than concentration. Some points become "popular" neighbours purely because of geometry — they sit in many kNN lists regardless of true relevance — while others are never retrieved. This skews kNN classification, retrieval, and recommendation. Fixes rescale local geometry: mutual proximity, local/global scaling, mutual-kNN graphs. Worth naming so students recognize the symptom (the same handful of documents keep getting returned).

Anisotropy of contextual embeddings

a narrow cone inflates cosine

BERT / GPT-2 representations occupy a narrow cone, so two random tokens already have high cosine (Ethayarajh 2019) — raw cosine is inflated. Fix: whitening / standardization, or contrastive training.

The embeddings themselves are the problem here. Contextual reps from BERT/GPT-2 are squeezed into a narrow cone in space, so even unrelated tokens sit at a small angle and cosine reports them as "similar". This makes raw cosine on out-of-the-box LM embeddings misleading. Remedies push the cloud toward isotropy: whitening, BERT-flow, standardization, or contrastive objectives (sentence-transformers). Practical lesson: don't trust raw cosine on hidden states without calibration.

Common misconception

Bust the myth
What students think

“Cosine and Euclidean always rank the same — and cosine is always 'about similarity'.”

They agree only on L2-normalized vectors. Clean counterexample: query \(q=(1,0)\); candidates \(a=(0.9,0)\) and \(c=(2,0)\). Both have \(\cos=1\) with \(q\) (cosine cannot separate them), but Euclidean gives \(0.1\) vs \(1.0\) — it strictly prefers \(a\). And Steck et al. (2024): for regularized matrix factorization a diagonal "gauge" leaves dot-product predictions unchanged while making cosine values arbitrary — so cosine of learned embeddings can be non-unique. Fix: train against cosine, or prefer dot, or whiten first.

Worked counter-example (grounded · _research/data/cosine_examples.py, classic_pairs[0]). Vectors \( u=(1,1) \) and \( v=(10,10) \) point the same direction.
Cosine: \( u\!\cdot\!v / (\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert) = 20 / (\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{200}) = 20/20 = \mathbf{1.0} \) — identical.
Euclidean: \( \lVert u-v\rVert = \sqrt{(1\!-\!10)^2 + (1\!-\!10)^2} = \sqrt{162} \approx \mathbf{12.73} \) — far apart.
Same two vectors, ranked the same under cosine, ranked very differently under Euclidean. Steck et al. (2024) push the point further: under regularized MF a diagonal gauge leaves dot-product predictions invariant while making cosine arbitrary — so "cosine of learned embeddings" can be ill-defined unless the gauge is fixed (L2-normalize, or train against cosine, or whiten).

Two myths in one. First the ranking myth: q=(1,0), a=(0.9,0), c=(2,0) — same direction so cosine ties at 1.0, but Euclidean is 0.1 vs 1.0 and strictly prefers a. Magnitude, not angle, decided. Always L2-normalize first if you want them to agree. Second, the deeper Steck 2024 critique: in a regularized linear MF model you can rescale dimensions (a diagonal gauge) without changing dot-product predictions, yet cosine similarities change arbitrarily — so "cosine of learned embeddings" is not well-defined unless you control the gauge. Good class debate; the 2026 rebuttal argues normalization removes the freedom.

In practice: FAISS, vector DBs, numerics

match the metric to the training

FAISS has no native cosine

  • ·METRIC_L2 returns squared \(L_2\) (no sqrt).
  • ·METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT for dot.
  • ·Cosine = L2-normalize, then IndexFlatIP. Forget to normalize → silently wrong.

Match & guard

Pick the vector-DB metric to match how the embedding model was trained: most sentence models → cosine; some retrievers → dot.

!
Numerics

Cosine is undefined for a zero vector (add \(\varepsilon\)); KL needs smoothing for zero bins; pre-normalize once and store unit vectors.

The deployment reality. FAISS returns squared L2 (cheaper, same ordering) and has no cosine metric — you L2-normalize and use inner product. The single most common production bug is forgetting to normalize, which silently turns your "cosine" search into dot-product search that rewards long vectors. And always match the index metric to the model's training objective; a cosine-trained sentence model behaves oddly under raw dot. Numerics: guard the zero vector with ε, smooth KL's zero bins.
