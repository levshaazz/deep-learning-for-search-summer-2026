Under the Binary Independence Model, with no relevance feedback, the odds collapse to this sum of per-term weights — the Robertson–Spärck-Jones weight, idf's probabilistic twin. In practice we clamp it with \(+1\) (slide 32's \(\ln(1+\cdot)\)) so it can't turn negative for common terms — that clamped form is the idf every worked number here uses. The gap either way: it is presence-only; it ignores how often \(t\) occurs.

The Binary Independence Model represents a document as a set of present-or-absent terms and, under independence, the odds of relevance factorise into a sum of per-term weights. With no relevance judgments to estimate from, that weight reduces to the Robertson–Spärck-Jones form — the probabilistic relative of idf. Note the subtlety: the raw RSJ weight can go negative when a term sits in more than half the corpus (and on a tiny collection like cat/dog/mouse it would), so in practice — and in every worked example in this deck — we use the +1-clamped form from slide 32, ln(1 + (N−n+0.5)/(n+0.5)), which stays non-negative. This is BM1. Its limitation is glaring once stated: it only asks whether a term is present, never how many times — so it cannot tell a document that mentions "shuttle" once from one that is entirely about shuttles. Fixing that is the next step.
