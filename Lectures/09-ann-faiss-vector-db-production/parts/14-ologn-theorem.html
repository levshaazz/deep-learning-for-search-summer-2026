Why the walk is \(O(\log N)\), not \(O(N)\)

the layered graph, as a proposition with a proof
Proposition · logarithmic search
On an HNSW graph over \(N\) points where each layer keeps a \(1/m\) fraction of the one below (\(\approx \log_m N\) layers) and greedy search does \(O(1)\) expected work per layer, the expected distance evaluations to reach the nearest neighbour is \(O(\log N)\) — far cheaper than the \(O(N\cdot d)\) brute-force scan.
Proof sketch
Keep each point on the next layer up with probability \(1/m\). The expected layer count is \(\mathbb{E}[\text{layers}]\approx\log_m N\). At the top, greedy search reaches the locally-nearest hub in \(O(1)\) hops. One layer down, the graph is denser by a factor \(m\), but small-world connectivity keeps the expected hops \(O(1)\) again. Summing \(O(1)\) over \(\log_m N\) layers gives \(\sum_{\ell}O(1)=O(\log_m N)=O(\log N)\). The parameter \(M\) sets \(m\); larger \(M\), denser layers, higher recall, more memory. \(\blacksquare\)
Caveat: this is the empirical / expected complexity, not a worst-case guarantee. The \(O(1)\)-hops-per-layer step assumes the graph stays navigable, unproven for general data — adversarial or clustered inputs can degrade greedy search toward linear.

Malkov & Yashunin (arXiv 2016; IEEE TPAMI 2020).
