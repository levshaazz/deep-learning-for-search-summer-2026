The Wraith, quantified

300 uniform points · \(\mathrm{cv}=\sigma/\mu\) of pairwise distance
$$ d=2:\ \mu=0.5171,\ \sigma=0.2474 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2474}{0.5171}=\mathbf{0.4784} $$ $$ d=10:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2451}{1.2688}=\mathbf{0.1932};\qquad d=100:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2445}{4.0620}=\mathbf{0.0602} $$ $$ d=1000:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2410}{12.9023}=\mathbf{0.0187} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{near}\approx\text{far} $$

The spread \(\sigma\) barely moves; the mean \(\mu\) keeps growing, so the coefficient of variation collapses \(0.4784 \to 0.0187\). At \(d=1000\) every pair of points is almost the same distance apart — "the nearest" stops meaning anything, so exact nearest-neighbour becomes both fragile and expensive — the case for approximate search (next). Note this is not escaped by switching metric — cosine concentrates too (pairwise cosines shrink like \(1/\sqrt d\) toward orthogonality); we rank by angle for semantic reasons, not because angle is immune. The \(\mathrm{cv}=\sigma/\mu\) here is a related diagnostic — not identical — to the classic Beyer–Goldstein–Ramakrishnan–Shaft (1999) relative contrast \((d_{\max}-d_{\min})/d_{\min}\to 0\) the Book uses: cv is a population spread ratio, relative contrast an extreme-order-statistic ratio, but both collapse for the same reason — spread grows like \(\sqrt d\) while scale grows like \(d\).

Distance-concentration by hand. 300 uniform-random points in [0,1]^d (seed 0). cv = std/mean of the pairwise Euclidean distances: 0.4784 (d=2) → 0.1932 (d=10) → 0.0602 (d=100) → 0.0187 (d=1000). All four μ/σ/cv triples are verbatim from data/l2-highd.json dims. The std stays ≈0.24 while μ grows from 0.52 to 12.90, so cv → 0.
