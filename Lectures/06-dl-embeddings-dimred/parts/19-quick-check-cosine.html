Показать ответReveal explanation
A. \(\cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert}\) делит на обе нормы, поэтому длина сокращается — остаётся только угол. Евклидово расстояние, наоборот, растёт вместе с длиной, и частое (длинное) слово казалось бы «далёким» от редкого синонима. Именно поэтому Сэр Косинус — правильная линейка карты смысла.\(\cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}{\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert}\) divides by both norms, so length cancels and only the angle survives. Euclidean distance, by contrast, grows with magnitude, making a frequent (long) word look "far" from a rare synonym. That is exactly why Sir Cosine is the right ruler for the map of meaning.
A cheap retention beat right after the cosine table and before we do arithmetic on meaning. The trap is option B — students who half-remember "vectors live in space" reach for Euclidean distance. Push them to commit out loud before revealing. The point: cosine divides out both norms, so it measures direction only; that magnitude-invariance is precisely what we want, because a rare word and a frequent word with very different vector lengths can still be synonyms if they point the same way. This sets up the analogy climb — the gender direction is a direction, not a distance.
