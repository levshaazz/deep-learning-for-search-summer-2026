Why exact search dies

the Wraith returns — cost and geometry

The cost

Comparing a query against \(N\) vectors of dimension \(d\) is \(O(N\cdot d)\) — linear in the corpus, every single query. Fine at a thousand documents; ruinous at a billion.

The geometry

The Curse-of-Dimensionality Wraith (L2) returns: in high dimensions distances concentrate — nearest and farthest sit at almost the same distance, so "the nearest" turns fragile and the brute-force scan you paid for buys less than you think.

So we strike a bargain: give up a sliver of recall you can't feel, buy a mountain of speed — approximate nearest-neighbour search. The next two slides quantify both halves of the curse.

problem-exact-dies. Doomed twice: O(N·d) cost + high-d concentration (Wraith callback to L2). Motivates ANN. The following by-hand + histogram quantify the concentration.

The Wraith, quantified

300 uniform points · \(\mathrm{cv}=\sigma/\mu\) of pairwise distance
$$ d=2:\ \mu=0.5171,\ \sigma=0.2474 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2474}{0.5171}=\mathbf{0.4784} $$ $$ d=10:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2451}{1.2688}=\mathbf{0.1932};\qquad d=100:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2445}{4.0620}=\mathbf{0.0602} $$ $$ d=1000:\ \mathrm{cv}=\tfrac{0.2410}{12.9023}=\mathbf{0.0187} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{near}\approx\text{far} $$

The spread \(\sigma\) barely moves; the mean \(\mu\) keeps growing, so the coefficient of variation collapses \(0.4784 \to 0.0187\). At \(d=1000\) every pair of points is almost the same distance apart — "the nearest" stops meaning anything, which is why we rank by angle, not raw distance. The \(\mathrm{cv}=\sigma/\mu\) here is one lens on the same phenomenon the Book measures with the classic Beyer–Goldstein–Ramakrishnan–Shaft (1999) relative contrast \((d_{\max}-d_{\min})/d_{\min}\to 0\).

Distance-concentration by hand. 300 uniform-random points in [0,1]^d (seed 0). cv = std/mean of the pairwise Euclidean distances: 0.4784 (d=2) → 0.1932 (d=10) → 0.0602 (d=100) → 0.0187 (d=1000). All four μ/σ/cv triples are verbatim from data/l2-highd.json dims. The std stays ≈0.24 while μ grows from 0.52 to 12.90, so cv → 0. This is the geometric half of why exact NN dies; the next slide shows the histogram collapse.

Before we go fast, decide what "near" means

three ways to compare two vectors
Definition
Distance / similarity metricL2 · cosine · inner product

For vectors \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}^d\), three standard ways to score nearness:

L2 distance \(\;\lVert a-b\rVert\) — straight-line displacement; smaller is nearer. Inner product \(\;a\cdot b\) — grows with both alignment and magnitude; larger is nearer. Cosine \(\;\dfrac{a\cdot b}{\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert}\) — the inner product of the unit vectors; pure direction, magnitude thrown away.

FAISS exposes METRIC_L2 and METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT directly; cosine is just inner product on \(L_2\)-normalized vectors — normalize once, then use inner product. ANN structures are built around a metric, so this choice comes first.

three-metrics def-card. Sets up the metrics arc. The key production note: cosine ⇔ inner product on normalized vectors, and FAISS only ships METRIC_L2 / METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT — so cosine is a normalize-then-IP recipe. The index is built around a metric, hence we pick before building. The next two slides work all three on the same numbers.

Lane 1 · The navigable graph

Trade a sliver of recall for a mountain of speed

The lane, not the straight line. Give up a sliver of recall you can't even feel — occasionally missing a true neighbour — and buy a mountain of speed. That bargain is approximate nearest-neighbour search. First lane: a graph you walk.

turn-ann / HNSW lane. The exact→approximate bargain. Three lanes ahead: navigable graph (HNSW), partitioned cells (IVF), compression (PQ).

From skip-list to navigable graph

three ideas stacked into one structure
Definition
HNSWHierarchical Navigable Small World

A multi-layer proximity graph that you greedily walk toward the query. It stacks three ideas:

Skip-list — a sorted list with express lanes above it, so search skips long stretches in \(O(\log N)\). NSW (Navigable Small World) — connect each point to a few near neighbours plus a few long-range links, so any node is a short greedy walk from any other. HNSW — give the small-world graph the skip-list's layers: sparse long-range hubs on top, dense neighbourhoods at the base.

Enter at the top, take long hops where the graph is sparse, descend layer by layer, and refine on the dense base. Greedy descent on a layered small-world graph — no training, incremental inserts.

HNSW definition def-card. The lineage: skip-list (layered express lanes, O(log N)) + NSW (near links + long-range links → short greedy paths) → HNSW (layer the small-world graph). Entry at the sparse top, descend to the dense base. Sets up the O(log N) theorem next and the two-layer climb after.

Why the walk is \(O(\log N)\), not \(O(N)\)

the layered graph, as a proposition with a proof
Proposition · logarithmic search
On an HNSW graph over \(N\) points where each layer keeps a \(1/m\) fraction of the one below (so there are \(\approx \log_m N\) layers) and greedy search does \(O(1)\) expected work per layer, the expected number of distance evaluations to reach the nearest neighbour is \(O(\log N)\) — exponentially cheaper than the \(O(N\cdot d)\) brute-force scan.
Proof sketch
Build layers by keeping each point on the next layer up with probability \(1/m\). The expected layer count is the largest \(\ell\) with a non-empty layer: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{layers}]\approx\log_m N\). At the top layer (a handful of hub nodes) greedy search reaches the locally-nearest hub in \(O(1)\) hops. Descending one layer, the graph is denser by a factor \(m\), but small-world connectivity keeps the expected hops to the next locally-nearest node \(O(1)\) again. Summing \(O(1)\) work over \(\log_m N\) layers gives a geometric-style total \(\sum_{\ell}O(1)=O(\log_m N)=O(\log N)\). The construction parameter \(M\) (edges per node) sets \(m\); larger \(M\) means denser layers, higher recall, more memory. \(\blacksquare\)

Malkov & Yashunin (IEEE TPAMI 2018) — the layered small-world graph turns a linear scan into a logarithmic walk.

O(log N) theorem thm-card (statement + proof). Layers keep a 1/m fraction each → ≈log_m N layers; O(1) expected hops per layer (small-world property) → O(1) summed over log_m N layers = O(log N). M sets the per-node degree m: more edges → denser → higher recall, more memory. Cited Malkov & Yashunin TPAMI 2018 (arXiv:1603.09320), matching data/l9-bench.json hnsw.complexity 'O(log N) search'. No invented decimals — purely asymptotic.

HNSW — greedy search on a small-world graph

Greedy descent: from the entry node, hop to the closest neighbour until a local minimum — that node is the nearest neighbour. The ef knob widens the candidate list; M sets connectivity.

Greedy descent, by hand

walk to the closest neighbour until a local minimum
$$ d(n_0,q)=4.53,\quad d(n_1,q)=2.55,\quad d(n_3,q)=4.30 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{move to } n_1 $$ $$ d(n_2,q)=\mathbf{0.7071},\quad d(n_4,q)=2.92,\quad d(n_5,q)=1.58 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{local minimum, stop} $$ $$ \text{path } n_0 \to n_1 \to n_2 = \mathbf{2}\text{ hops},\quad n_2 = \text{brute-force NN} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{recall@1} = \mathbf{1.0} $$

Recall vs ef — the knee

reveal the curve left→right (←/→) · 30-node worst case

On a 30-node worst case (start farthest from \(q\)): \(\textrm{ef}=1\) keeps one candidate and traps at recall \(\mathbf{0.0}\) (5 candidates evaluated); \(\textrm{ef}\ge 2\) keeps a wider list and reaches \(\mathbf{1.0}\). On one query recall@1 is binary (\(0\) or \(1\)), so it steps; the smooth recall-vs-ef knee appears only when averaged over a query set. Past the knee, recall stays \(1.0\) but cost climbs (15 → 26) — pure overhead, the real curve here.

recall-curve, series 'ef', maxStep 4. data/l9-hnsw.json efSweep: ef 1→0.0 (5 cands), ef 2/4/8→1.0 (15 cands), ef 16→1.0 (26 cands). The knee is at ef=2: the smallest ef that already gives full recall. ef is the recall↔work dial; read it to the knee and stop. The cost number under each point = candidates evaluated.

The two knobs: M and ef

build-time connectivity vs search-time breadth
Knob When Turn it up → Turn it down →
M — edges per node build time (fixed in the index) denser graph, higher recall, more robust less memory & faster build, lower recall
efConstruction build time better-quality graph, higher recall ceiling faster build, sloppier neighbour lists
efSearch (ef) query time (free to retune) wider candidate list, escapes local-min traps faster but can trap (ef = 1 → recall 0.0)
memory — grows with M (edges) — the graph overhead ef costs nothing in memory, only time

\(M\) and \(\textrm{efConstruction}\) are baked in at build; efSearch is the live dial you slide to the recall knee per query. We just saw \(\textrm{ef}=1\to\) recall \(0.0\), \(\textrm{ef}\ge2\to 1.0\).

ef/M cmp-table. M = per-node degree (build-time, sets graph density → recall & memory); efConstruction = build-time graph quality; efSearch = the query-time breadth dial (retunable, costs time not memory). The ef=1→0.0 / ef≥2→1.0 contrast references the recall-curve slide (gated l9-hnsw efSweep). Knobs list matches data/l9-bench.json hnsw.knobs [M, efConstruction, efSearch].

When to reach for HNSW

strengths, costs, and the catch
Applicability
HNSWthe in-memory, low-latency default

Reach for it when: you need the lowest query latency at high recall, the index fits in RAM, and inserts arrive incrementally (no batch retrain). \(O(\log N)\) search, recall tuned live via efSearch.

The costs: high build time, and memory \(=\) the raw vectors plus the graph (\(\approx M\) edges per node) — it stores everything uncompressed.

So HNSW alone is RAM-hungry at a billion vectors. That is exactly why production pairs it with PQ (HNSW-PQ) — the graph for speed, the codes for memory. The next lanes, IVF and PQ, attack the memory wall directly.

HNSW applicability def-card. Use when: lowest latency at high recall, fits RAM, incremental inserts. Costs: high build, memory = vectors + graph overhead (no compression). The billion-scale catch motivates IVF (partition) and PQ (compression), and HNSW-PQ as the combined production index. Bridges to the IVF lane.
Lane 2 · The partition

Carve the galaxy into sectors

A graph walks; a partition sorts. Run \(k\)-means to pick centroids, carving space into Voronoi cells. File every vector under its nearest centroid — once, offline. At query time, probe only the cells near the query.

turn-partition. IVF = coarse quantizer + inverted lists. nprobe is the dial.

The coarse quantizer

file once, probe a few
Definition
IVFInverted File index

Run \(k\)-means to pick nlist centroids, carving the space into Voronoi cells. The coarse quantizer maps any vector to its nearest centroid; every database vector is filed under its cell — once, offline. (Same inverted-list idea as the L3 postings index — but the "term" is a centroid.)

At query time, find the nprobe cells whose centroids are nearest the query and scan only those — not all \(N\) vectors. Search cost \(\approx O(\textrm{nprobe}\cdot \tfrac{N}{\textrm{nlist}}\cdot d)\).

More cells (larger nlist) → fewer vectors per cell → faster, but a true neighbour is likelier to fall in a cell you didn't probe. nprobe trades recall for speed.

Coarse-quantizer / IVF def-card. k-means → nlist centroids → Voronoi cells; coarse quantizer = nearest-centroid map; file vectors once. Query probes the nprobe nearest cells: cost O(nprobe·N/nlist·d) (matches l9-compare search-complexity row). Callback to L3 inverted file (term → postings; here centroid → list). nprobe is the dial. Sets up the climb + 20-pt sweep.

IVF — probe the nearest cells

scrub nprobe (←/→)

Probe only the cells nearest the query. nprobe=1 finds 2 of 3 true neighbours (recall@3 = 0.6667); nprobe=2 also probes the second-nearest cell and recovers the third (recall@3 = 1.0).

9 points · 3 cells · query in \(c_0\)
$$ \text{nprobe}=1:\ \text{probe } c_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{find } 2/3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{recall@3} = \mathbf{0.6667} $$ $$ \text{nprobe}=2:\ \text{probe } c_0, c_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{find } 3/3 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{recall@3} = \mathbf{1.0} $$

When to reach for IVF

cheap training, the boundary-neighbour catch
Applicability
IVFpartition for large, cheaply-trained corpora

Reach for it when: \(N\) is large, training must be cheap (just \(k\)-means for the centroids), and inserts are easy (assign to a cell, append to its list). nprobe retunes recall live without rebuilding.

The catch: the boundary neighbour — a true neighbour just across a cell border is missed until you probe that cell. Raising nprobe recovers it but scans more.

IVF still stores full vectors, so it does not solve the memory wall on its own — which is why production stacks it with PQ as IVF-PQ: the cells for speed, the codes for RAM. That is the third lane.

IVF applicability def-card. Use when: large N, cheap training (k-means only), easy inserts, nprobe retunable. Catch: boundary neighbours stranded in unprobed cells. Still full vectors → no memory win alone → IVF-PQ in production. Bridges to the PQ lane.
Lane 3 · The compression

Fold the star-charts

Graphs and cells make search fast — but the vectors still have to fit in RAM. At a billion vectors that is the wall. Don't store the full float vector; store a handful of codebook indices that approximate it.

turn-compress. PQ trades a little recall for a lot of memory.

The "product" in product quantization

\(m\) small codebooks, \(k^m\) effective centroids
Definition
PQProduct Quantization

Split each vector into \(m\) subvectors and quantize each independently with its own little codebook of \(k\) centroids. A full vector's code is the tuple of \(m\) sub-codes — one point in the Cartesian product of the \(m\) subspaces.

The payoff is combinatorial: you store only \(m\cdot k\) centroids, but the code can represent \(k^m\) distinct reconstructions. With \(m=8,\ k=256\): store \(8\times 256 = 2048\) centroids, address \(256^{8}\approx 1.8\times 10^{19}\) points.

A single flat codebook covering that many regions would need \(k^m\) centroids — impossible to train or store. Splitting into a product of subspaces is what makes the compression affordable.

Subquantizer decomposition def-card. PQ splits a vector into m subvectors, each quantized with its own k-centroid codebook; the code is the tuple = a point in the Cartesian product. Store m·k centroids (8·256=2048) but represent k^m reconstructions (256^8 ≈ 1.8e19). A flat codebook of k^m is infeasible — the product structure is the trick. Sets up the climb + memory math + ADC.

Training one subspace codebook

Lloyd's \(k\)-means · 6 subvectors · \(k=2\) · inertia falls
$$ \text{init } c_0=(1,1),\ c_1=(2,1):\quad \text{assign }[0,1,0,1,1,1],\quad \text{inertia }=\textstyle\sum\lVert x-c\rVert^2=\mathbf{284.0} $$ $$ \text{recompute } c_0=(1.0,1.5),\ c_1=(6.75,6.5):\quad \text{reassign }[0,0,0,1,1,1],\quad \text{inertia}=\mathbf{20.6875} $$ $$ \text{recompute } c_0=(1.3333,1.3333),\ c_1=(8.3333,8.3333):\quad \text{inertia}=\mathbf{2.6667} $$

Each Lloyd iteration assigns points to the nearest centroid, then recomputes each centroid as its cluster mean. Inertia (total squared error) is monotone non-increasing: \(284.0 \to 20.6875 \to 2.6667\). This runs once per subspace, offline — the training cost PQ pays for its compression. (TurboQuant removes it entirely; next slide.)

Inertia sequence 284.0 → 20.6875 → 2.6667 (monotone non-increasing), centroids (1,1)/(2,1) → (1.0,1.5)/(6.75,6.5) → (1.3333,1.3333)/(8.3333,8.3333). ADC — distance from a table lookup

trace one code per subspace (←/→): ADC 35 vs exact 37

Split the query into \(m=4\) subqueries and precompute an \(m\times k\) table of squared sub-distances. A database vector stored as codes \([2,0,3,1]\) costs \(\hat d^2=\sum_j \textrm{table}[j][\textrm{code}_j]=2+0+20+13=\mathbf{35}\) — four lookups, no full-vector math. The exact distance is \(37\); the gap of \(2\) is the quantization error.

pq-quantize variant 'adc', maxStep 3 (one subspace row traced per step). ADC formula d̂²=Σ_j d(q_j, c_{j,code_j})². Codes [2,0,3,1] → table[0][2]=2, table[1][0]=0, table[2][3]=20, table[3][1]=13 → ADC=35 vs exactDistance=37, gap 2 = the quantization error. All numbers verbatim from data/l9-pq.json adcWorked. The point of PQ: near-exact distance computed cheaply from table lookups on the codes.

The memory↔recall dial, across configs

reveal a config per step (←/→): more codes vs fewer bits

A 768-d vector (3072 B): \(m=96,k=256\) → 96 B/vec (\(\mathbf{32\times}\), 96 GB @ \(10^9\)); double to \(m=192\) → 192 B (\(\mathbf{16\times}\), finer codes, more RAM); drop to \(k=16\) (4 bits/code) → 48 B (\(\mathbf{64\times}\), 48 GB). A 128-d vector at \(m=8,k=256\) → 8 B (\(\mathbf{64\times}\)). More subvectors or more bits = higher recall and more memory; fewer = the opposite.

Frontier — TurboQuant

data-oblivious, online, no codebook

PQ's biggest production pain is training the codebook. TurboQuant (Zandieh et al., 2025) removes it: it is data-oblivious and online — randomly rotate the vector (concentrating its coordinates into a Beta distribution), then apply a per-coordinate optimal scalar quantizer; a two-stage MSE + 1-bit QJL residual gives an unbiased inner-product estimate.

  • Lands within a small (\(\approx 2.7\times\)) constant of the information-theoretic distortion bound.
  • Reports better recall with near-zero indexing time, outperforming existing PQ techniques.
  • We keep FAISS PQ as the taught baseline; TurboQuant is where the field is going.
Frontier nod folded into the PQ climb. Verified claims: data-oblivious/online/no codebook; random-rotation → Beta → scalar quantizer; ≈2.7× from the bound; MSE + 1-bit QJL residual → unbiased inner product. NOT a FAISS-kernel head-to-head. arXiv:2504.19874.

When to reach for PQ

the memory wall, and what you give up
Applicability
PQcompression for RAM-bound, billion-scale corpora

Reach for it when: memory, not compute, is the wall — a billion full vectors won't fit in RAM. PQ folds each vector to a few bytes (32×–64×), and ADC keeps search to cheap table lookups.

The cost: distances are approximate (quantization error), so recall drops — a representative PQ-m4 recall@1 is \(\approx 0.70\). You must also train codebooks offline and retrain as the data drifts.

PQ is rarely used alone: it is the compression layer inside IVF-PQ or HNSW-PQ. The partition or graph gives speed; PQ gives the RAM. The comparison matrix next lays all five options side by side.

Part 3 · Ship it

FAISS · vector DBs · the serving path

These structures don't float in the void — they live in software. A library you call in-process, or a database you query over the network. And a query is now a request with a budget.

turn-production. FAISS (library) vs vector DB (service); the latency budget; the Iceberg of operations.

Library or service?

FAISS vs a vector database

FAISS — a library

Build an index in your own process and call it: IndexFlat (exact), IndexIVFPQ, IndexHNSWFlat. Use it when the index fits one process you control.

Vector DB — a service

The index plus storage, metadata filtering, updates, replication, and an API — queried over the network. Use it when you need it managed, filtered, and shared.

Rule of thumb: FAISS when it fits one box you own; a vector DB when it must be operated, filtered, and shared.

turn-production detail. Library vs service. Both consume the same ANN structures.

The FAISS index-factory grammar

Python · faiss · one string builds the whole index
Python · build_index.pyfaiss
import faiss

# A comma-separated recipe: [transform,] coarse-quantizer , encoding
d = 768

# IVF with 4096 cells, each vector PQ-encoded to 96 bytes
idx = faiss.index_factory(d, "IVF4096,PQ96")

# A pure HNSW graph, M = 32 edges per node (no compression)
idx = faiss.index_factory(d, "HNSW32")

# OPQ rotation → IVF (16384 cells, HNSW coarse quantizer) → PQ 64 bytes
idx = faiss.index_factory(d, "OPQ64,IVF16384_HNSW32,PQ64")

idx.train(xb)          # learn centroids + codebooks (offline)
idx.add(xb)            # file every vector under its cell / code
idx.nprobe = 16        # the recall<->work dial, set at query time

Read it left to right: an optional transform (OPQ64), then the coarse quantizer (IVF4096, or IVF16384_HNSW32 = an HNSW-indexed quantizer), then the encoding (PQ96, Flat, …). The whole index from one string.

Index-factory code slide. The grammar: [transform,] quantizer, encoding. "IVF4096,PQ96" = 4096 cells, 96-byte PQ codes. "HNSW32" = HNSW graph, M=32, uncompressed. "OPQ64,IVF16384_HNSW32,PQ64" = OPQ rotation → IVF with an HNSW coarse quantizer (16384 cells) → PQ 64 bytes. train/add/nprobe are the lifecycle. nprobe set at query time is the recall↔work dial. Why ANN is the difference between shipping and not

the search hop, exact vs approximate
$$ \text{exact scan of } 10^{6}\times 768\text{-d}:\ \text{search hop} \approx \mathbf{520}\text{ ms} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{already} > 200\text{ ms SLA, alone} $$ $$ \text{ANN over quantized vectors}:\ \text{search hop} = \mathbf{12}\text{ ms} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{the full path totals } 89\text{ ms} < 200 $$ $$ \frac{520}{12} \approx 43\times \text{ faster on the hop that matters} $$

An exact brute-force search of a million 768-d vectors costs \(\approx 520\)ms for the search hop alone — it blows the \(200\)ms budget before the encoder or reranker even runs. ANN turns that \(520\) into \(\mathbf{12}\), quantization keeps the index in RAM, and caching makes repeats nearly free. That is the whole bargain.

Exact-vs-ANN budget proof. exactScanMs ≈ 520 (search hop of 10⁶×768-d brute force) vs ANN search hop = 12ms — both verbatim from data/l9-latency.json (exactScanMs=520, budget step 3 lat=12). 520 alone exceeds the 200ms SLA; the full ANN path is 89ms < 200. 520/12 ≈ 43×. These are representative serving figures (the SUM 89 is the gated exact computation in the cold sequence). The point: ANN is not an optimization, it is the difference between shipping and not.

Quick check — the graph lane

ef and the local-minimum trap
On our 30-node worst case, \(\textrm{ef}=1\) gave recall@1 \(=0.0\) but \(\textrm{ef}=2\) gave \(1.0\). Why does a wider efSearch rescue recall?
A It adds more edges to the graph, changing its structure at query time
B It keeps a wider candidate list, so a single local-minimum trap no longer stops the search
C It increases M, the number of neighbours stored per node
Reveal explanation
B. efSearch is a query-time dial — it changes neither the edges nor \(M\) (those are baked in at build). A wider candidate list keeps several promising nodes in play, so greedy descent can back out of a local minimum instead of halting there. That is exactly why \(\textrm{ef}=1\to 0.0\) but \(\textrm{ef}\ge 2\to 1.0\); past the knee, cost rises (15 → 26 candidates) with no recall gain.

nprobe and the boundary neighbour

C. All five true neighbours sat in the three nearest cells \(c_0, c_4, c_1\), so by \(\textrm{nprobe}=3\) recall is already \(1.0\). Quick check — the compression lane

ADC and the quantization error
In the ADC worked example, the lookup gave \(\hat d^2 = 35\) but the exact squared distance was \(37\). What is the gap of \(2\)?
A A rounding error in the table arithmetic that disappears at full precision
B The quantization error — ADC measures distance to the reconstruction, not the true vector
C The cost of building the \(m\times k\) table, amortized into the distance
Reveal explanation
B. The database vector is stored as codes \([2,0,3,1]\) — the nearest centroid per subspace, not the exact subvector. ADC sums the table cells \(2+0+20+13=35\), which is the distance to that reconstruction; the true vector is at \(37\). The \(2\) is the quantization error — the price of compression. It is exact arithmetic, not rounding (A), and the table cost is separate (C). This is why a representative PQ-m4 recall@1 is \(\approx 0.70\), not \(1.0\).
