How we learned to cut words

Timeline

From rule-based word splits to raw bytes. Each step kept the wins and patched one scar of the last.

  1. 1993
    Penn Treebank

    Rule-based word split

    Regex on whitespace & punctuation. Clean for English — but a fixed word vocabulary can never cover real text (Zipf).

  2. 1994
    Gage

    BPE — for compression

    Repeatedly merge the most frequent byte pair. Born to shrink files, not to tokenize.

  3. 2012
    Schuster & Nakajima

    WordPiece

    Merge by likelihood gain, not raw frequency. Built for Japanese/Korean voice search.

  4. 2016
    Sennrich et al.

    BPE → NLP

    Subwords crack rare/unknown words in translation. Open-vocabulary at last.

  5. 2018
    Kudo

    Unigram + SentencePiece

    Probabilistic subwords; language-agnostic, treats whitespace as a symbol (▁).

  6. 2019
    GPT-2 · Radford et al.

    Byte-level BPE

    Operate on raw bytes — literally zero OOV, any language, any emoji.

  7. 2024+
    tiktoken · frontier

    Fast BPE at scale

    Production LLM tokenizers; the frontier explores token-free byte models (ByT5).

The historical spine for Part 2. Note the 22-year gap between BPE-as-compression (Gage 1994) and BPE-as-tokenizer (Sennrich 2016) — the algorithm waited two decades for the right problem. Two pivots: Sennrich 2016 (subwords enter NLP) and GPT-2 2019 (byte-level kills OOV forever). The arc mirrors the lecture: word → subword → byte. Dates are grounded against the original papers.
