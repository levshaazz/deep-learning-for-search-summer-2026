WordPiece merge criterion

Likelihood, not raw frequency
$$ \mathrm{score}(A,B) = \frac{\mathrm{freq}(AB)}{\mathrm{freq}(A)\cdot \mathrm{freq}(B)} $$
\(\mathrm{freq}(AB)\)
Pair count
how often \(A,B\) are adjacent
\(\mathrm{freq}(A)\)
Unigram count
of the left piece
\(\mathrm{freq}(B)\)
Unigram count
of the right piece

Dividing by the marginal counts favours pairs that co-occur more than chance — a common piece like ##s is penalised unless it sticks to one specific neighbour. The original WordPiece picks the merge that most increases corpus likelihood under a unigram LM; this ratio is the common practical approximation of that objective.

Read it as pointwise mutual information without the log. A pair of two very common pieces (say "th" + "e") has a big numerator but an even bigger denominator, so its score is modest. A pair where a rare piece always follows a specific piece scores high. This is why WordPiece tends to keep meaningful morphemes together. Note: real WordPiece uses exactly this freq(AB)/(freq(A)·freq(B)) ratio with no |V| factor. On the next slide's toy corpus every pair-count is 1, so the honest score is 1 for all pairs (a tie); the ×|V| we display there (29, 30, 31) is a legibility-only re-scaling, not a real variant.
