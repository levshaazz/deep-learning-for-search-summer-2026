Four words with corpus frequencies (superscripts). </w> marks end-of-word.

$$\text{count pairs, weighted by freq}$$

The pair e s appears in newest (\(6\)) and widest (\(3\)) \(\Rightarrow 9\), the maximum.

$$\texttt{e}+\texttt{s}\to\texttt{es}\;(9),\quad \texttt{es}+\texttt{t}\to\texttt{est}\;(9)$$

Merges 1–2. Both have count \(9\) (from newest+widest).

$$\texttt{est}+\texttt{</w>}\to\texttt{est</w>}\;(9)$$

Merge 3. Still \(6+3=9\) — bind the suffix to the word boundary.

$$\texttt{l}+\texttt{o}\to\texttt{lo}\;(7),\quad \texttt{lo}+\texttt{w}\to\texttt{low}\;(7)$$

Merges 4–5. Count \(5+2=7\) (from low+lower).

$$\texttt{lowest}\;(\text{unseen})\to\texttt{low}\;\texttt{est</w>}$$

Result. A word never in training is built from learned pieces — no [UNK].

overview
The flagship. Five merges, all arithmetic on the right and self-consistent: es (9), est (9), est</w> (9), lo (7), low (7). The es/st tie at 9 is broken by a fixed rule (here es first). The payoff at step 6: "lowest" was never in the corpus, yet it tokenizes cleanly as low · est</w> from learned pieces — no UNK. That generalization is the whole reason subword tokenization won.
