“Cosine and Euclidean always rank the same — and cosine is always 'about similarity'.”

They agree only on L2-normalized vectors. Clean counterexample: query \(q=(1,0)\); candidates \(a=(0.9,0)\) and \(c=(2,0)\). Both have \(\cos=1\) with \(q\) (cosine cannot separate them), but Euclidean gives \(0.1\) vs \(1.0\) — it strictly prefers \(a\). And Steck et al. (2024): for regularized matrix factorization a diagonal "gauge" leaves dot-product predictions unchanged while making cosine values arbitrary — so cosine of learned embeddings can be non-unique. Fix: train against cosine, or prefer dot, or whiten first.

Worked counter-example (grounded · _research/data/cosine_examples.py, classic_pairs[0]). Vectors \( u=(1,1) \) and \( v=(10,10) \) point the same direction.
Cosine: \( u\!\cdot\!v / (\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert) = 20 / (\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{200}) = 20/20 = \mathbf{1.0} \) — identical.
Euclidean: \( \lVert u-v\rVert = \sqrt{(1\!-\!10)^2 + (1\!-\!10)^2} = \sqrt{162} \approx \mathbf{12.73} \) — far apart.
Same two vectors, ranked the same under cosine, ranked very differently under Euclidean. Steck et al. (2024) push the point further: under regularized MF a diagonal gauge leaves dot-product predictions invariant while making cosine arbitrary — so "cosine of learned embeddings" can be ill-defined unless the gauge is fixed (L2-normalize, or train against cosine, or whiten).

Two myths in one. First the ranking myth: q=(1,0), a=(0.9,0), c=(2,0) — same direction so cosine ties at 1.0, but Euclidean is 0.1 vs 1.0 and strictly prefers a. Magnitude, not angle, decided. Always L2-normalize first if you want them to agree. Second, the deeper Steck 2024 critique: in a regularized linear MF model you can rescale dimensions (a diagonal gauge) without changing dot-product predictions, yet cosine similarities change arbitrarily — so "cosine of learned embeddings" is not well-defined unless you control the gauge. Good class debate; the 2026 rebuttal argues normalization removes the freedom.
