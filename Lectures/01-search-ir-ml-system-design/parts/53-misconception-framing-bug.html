The most expensive bug

Bust the myth
Where we think the risk is

"The hard, risky part is the modeling — pick the wrong architecture and everything fails."

The most expensive bug is in problem framing, not modeling. If the objective is misaligned with the goal, a technically perfect model makes things worse — a flawless CTR-maximiser that quietly tanks retention. You can always swap the model; you rarely notice you optimised the wrong thing until the business metric has already moved against you.

Worked case: YouTube ranker objective (Covington, Adams & Sargin, RecSys 2016 §3 — "Modeling Expected Watch Time"). If you train the ranker on \( P(\text{click}\mid\text{shown}) \), the model learns the proxy: it favours thumbnail-bait and short clickbait — CTR up, satisfaction down. The paper instead trains a weighted logistic: positives are weighted by observed watch-time, negatives by 1. The odds \( e^{Wx+b} \) then approximate \( E[\text{watch time}\mid\text{shown}] \) — a different quantity than click probability. Same two-tower architecture, same logged data  ⇒  the ranker now prefers videos people finish, not videos they start. Architecture didn't change; the framing of "good" did.

The closing systems lesson and the reason step 01 (framing) leads the framework. A perfect model on the wrong objective is not neutral — it is actively harmful, and it fails silently because the proxy metric looks great. This is why we spend a whole framework step on framing and pair every proxy with a guardrail. It also rhymes with Goodhart: the danger is not a bad model, it is a well-optimised wrong target. Anchored to Covington, Adams & Sargin 2016 "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations" (RecSys'16) — they explicitly describe the click→watch-time reframe as a larger product win than any subsequent architecture change.
