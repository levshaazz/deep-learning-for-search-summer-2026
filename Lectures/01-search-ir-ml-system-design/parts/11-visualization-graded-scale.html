Why we rank instead of filter

Graded relevance · the 5-point scale

Filtering says yes/no; ranking says how good, and in what order — which matches how users read top-down.

The pedagogical payoff of graded relevance: if there are degrees of "good", a yes/no filter is lossy. Users read top-down and rarely past the first few results, so the order is the product. This is the bridge to ranking metrics — \(\text{nDCG}@10\) rewards putting grade-4 above grade-2 — and it sets up the quiz at the end of Part 01. Making the arrow concrete: \(\text{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{g_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)}\), where \(g_i\) is the grade at rank \(i\); the gain \(2^{g_i}-1\) is divided by a position discount \(\log_2(i+1)\). Normalise by the ideal ordering: \(\text{nDCG}@k = \text{DCG}@k / \text{IDCG}@k \in [0,1]\). Worked example: a grade-4 doc gives gain \(2^4-1=15\); at rank 1 it contributes \(15/\log_2 2 = 15\), but at rank 2 only \(15/\log_2 3 \approx 9.46\) — that drop is exactly the "discount by position" the deck claims.
