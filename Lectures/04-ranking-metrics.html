На масштабе качество на глаз не оценишьYou can't eyeball quality at scale

зачем нужно числоwhy we need a number

Прочитать одно ранжирование глазами — нормально. Оценить \(10^{4}\) запросов глазами — нет, и «выглядит хорошо» нельзя усреднить, сравнить или отследить во времени. Нужны три вещи: метка релевантности на каждый результат (ground truth), метрика, превращающая ранжирование в одно сравнимое число, и статистика, чтобы отличить сигнал от шума.Reading one ranking by eye is fine. Judging \(10^{4}\) queries by eye is not — and "looks good" cannot be averaged, compared, or trended over time. We need three things: a relevance label per result (ground truth), a metric that turns a ranking into one comparable score, and statistics to tell signal from noise.

The core problem. With one query you can skim the list and form an opinion. But a real evaluation set has thousands of queries, and opinions don't aggregate: you cannot average "looks good", you cannot tell whether yesterday's change helped, and you cannot compare two systems fairly. The fix is a number — but a number needs ground truth to compare against, and a difference between two numbers needs a significance test before you believe it. Labels, metric, statistics: that is the spine of the lecture.

Пулинг & парадигма Cranfield / TRECPooling & the Cranfield / TREC paradigm

как строятся тестовые коллекцииhow test collections are built
МетодMethod
ПулингPoolingdepth-\(k\) pool

Запустите много систем на каждом запросе; возьмите топ \(k\) из каждой; объедините их в пул; пусть асессоры оценивают только пул.Run many systems on each query; take the top \(k\) from each; union them into a pool; have assessors judge only the pool.

Парадигма Cranfield (1960-е) установила переиспользуемые тестовые коллекции (оценённые почти исчерпывающе); пулинг появился в 1970-е как техника выборки, а TREC (1992–) индустриализировал его: переиспользуемая тестовая коллекция = запросы + пулинговые qrels. Документ вне топ-\(k\) каждой системы считается нерелевантным — обычно безопасно, потому что релевантные документы обычно всплывают у вершины какой-нибудь системы.The Cranfield paradigm (1960s) established reusable test collections (judged near-exhaustively); pooling arrived in the 1970s as a sampling technique and TREC (1992–) industrialised it: a reusable test collection = queries + pooled qrels. A document outside every system's top-\(k\) is assumed non-relevant — usually safe, because relevant docs tend to surface near the top of some system.

Cranfield in the 1960s established the recipe that still rules: fixed queries, fixed documents, fixed relevance judgments, so any system can be scored offline and compared — Cranfield itself judged near-exhaustively. The trick that makes large collections affordable is pooling, introduced as a sampling technique in the 1970s: instead of judging all documents, you pool the top-k of every participating system and judge only that union. TREC industrialised it from 1992. The bet is that any genuinely relevant document is found by at least one decent system and lands in the pool. Voorhees showed pooled collections are robust enough to reuse for years.

Распространённое заблуждениеCommon misconception

проблема неоценённого документаthe unjudged-document problem
Что люди предполагаютWhat people assume

«Если документ не был оценён как релевантный, его безопасно считать нерелевантным.»“If a document wasn't judged relevant, it's safe to count it as non-relevant.”

Безопасно только внутри пула. Новая система может извлечь по-настоящему релевантные документы, которые не нашла ни одна пулинговая система — они неоценённые, и «неоценённый = нерелевантный» несправедливо её штрафует. Чем глубже ваше извлечение отличается от пула, тем хуже смещение.Only safe inside the pool. A new system can retrieve genuinely relevant docs that no pooled system found — these are unjudged, and "unjudged = non-relevant" unfairly penalises it. The deeper your retrieval differs from the pool, the worse the bias.

Решение. Используйте меры, устойчивые к неполным оценкам: bpref (считает только оценённые документы) или condensed-list метрики (удалить неоценённые документы, затем оценивать). Они оценивают только по оценённым свидетельствам, так что новаторский ранжировщик не наказывается за нахождение релевантных документов, которые пул пропустил.The fix. Use measures robust to incomplete judgments: bpref (counts only judged docs) or condensed-list metrics (delete unjudged docs, then score). They evaluate on judged evidence only, so an innovative ranker isn't punished for finding relevant docs the pool missed.

This is the subtle trap of reusing a test collection. Within the pool, "unjudged = non-relevant" is fine. But a brand-new system — say, a dense retriever that ranks documents nobody pooled — surfaces relevant docs that were never judged, and the standard convention scores them as wrong. The system gets punished precisely for being different and good. bpref and condensed-list metrics restrict scoring to judged documents, removing the penalty. Buckley and Voorhees introduced bpref for exactly this reason. Modern pooled collections also use inferred-AP estimators (infAP / statAP, Yilmaz and Aslam) that estimate the metric under sampled judgments.

Практическое правило: \(\kappa>0.6\) — существенное, \(>0.8\) — почти эталон. Много судей → Fleiss' \(\kappa\). Низкая \(\kappa\) означает, что ваша «истина» зашумлена и ограничивает разрешающую способность метрики. Остерегайтесь парадокса распространённости: когда одна метка доминирует (большинство пулинговых документов нерелевантны), \(p_e\) высока, и \(\kappa\) может выглядеть низкой несмотря на почти идеальное согласие — сообщайте сырое согласие вместе с \(\kappa\).Rule of thumb: \(\kappa>0.6\) substantial, \(>0.8\) near-gold. Many judges → Fleiss' \(\kappa\). Low \(\kappa\) means your "ground truth" is noisy and caps the metric's resolution. Beware the prevalence paradox: when one label dominates (most pooled docs are non-relevant), \(p_e\) is high and \(\kappa\) can look low despite near-perfect agreement — report raw agreement alongside \(\kappa\).

Ground truth is not handed down from heaven — humans disagree, especially on borderline relevance. Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement you'd get by chance: subtract chance, normalise by the room left above chance. Above 0.6 is usually called substantial, above 0.8 near-gold; Fleiss generalises to more than two judges. The practical lesson: if your annotators only weakly agree, your labels are noisy, and no metric can resolve system differences finer than that noise. Measure agreement before you trust the qrels.

nDCG exists because the counting family ignores two facts. First, relevance is graded: an authoritative, exactly-on-topic document beats a tangential one, and we lose that with 0/1 labels. Second, position is everything: people scan from the top and almost never reach rank 8, so a hit there is nearly worthless compared to a hit at rank 1. nDCG bakes both in — graded gain in the numerator, a position discount that shrinks with depth. Геометрическая модель просмотра: \(P(\text{exam}\mid \text{rank}) = \gamma^{\,\text{rank}-1}\) при \(\gamma=0.94\). Документ на ранге 1 просматривается с вероятностью \(1.0\); на ранге 10 — лишь \(0.57\). Клик означает «релевантно и просмотрено» — поэтому низкие ранги получают меньше кликов независимо от качества.Geometric examination model: \(P(\text{exam}\mid \text{rank}) = \gamma^{\,\text{rank}-1}\) with \(\gamma=0.94\). A doc at rank 1 is examined with prob \(1.0\); at rank 10 only \(0.57\). A click means "relevant and examined" — so low ranks get fewer clicks regardless of quality.

The single biggest reason raw clicks lie. Users examine the top of the list far more than the bottom, so a click conflates two things: was the result relevant, and was it even looked at. The examination (position-based) model formalises this — examination depends only on rank; here we use a geometric form, probability of examining rank r is gamma to the r minus 1, gamma 0.94. Rank 1 is examined with certainty, rank 10 only 57% of the time. So a great result buried at rank 8 earns few clicks not because it's bad but because nobody saw it. Any click-based metric must correct for this. One caveat: this examination model is the simplest of a family. The cascade model (Craswell et al. 2008) makes examination of rank r conditional on not being satisfied by anything above it, so the ranks couple — a strong rank-1 result starves the clicks below it (the same intuition ERR uses on slide 36). And a click also needs the snippet to be attractive: caption/trust bias means users sometimes click on the wording, not the true relevance. So debiasing position alone is incomplete.

overview
Interleaving is the sharpest tool in online evaluation. Instead of showing A to one group and B to another, you blend both rankers into a single list the user sees — team-draft alternates picks like captains choosing a team, tagging each slot with its source. Every click credits the ranker that placed that result. Because both rankers compete within the same list for the same user, position bias largely cancels: each occupies high and low slots equally often. Each round both teams pick, so the slots split evenly; B wins on the clicks. Over five queries A earns 9 credits, B earns 16, preference for B is 16/25 = 0.64, B preferred in four of five queries. Joachims showed interleaving needs far less traffic than A/B to reach the same confidence. Two caveats. First, you don't declare a winner from the bare 0.64 — you run a binomial/sign test on the per-query preferences (the per-query pairing is exactly why interleaving is more sensitive than A/B at equal traffic). Second, team-draft can mis-credit documents the two rankings share, and it has a known tie bias; probabilistic and optimised interleaving (Radlinski & Craswell; Chapelle et al. 2012, already cited on slide 60) fix these.

Inverse Propensity Scoring (оценка по обратной склонности): клики смещены тем, что старая политика решила показать. Разделите каждую награду на её склонность → несмещённая оценка ценности новой политики из логированных данных. Основа несмещённого learning-to-rank. Две загвоздки: малые \(p_i\) заставляют \(r_i/p_i\) взрываться (высокая дисперсия — обрезайте/клипуйте или используйте самонормированный IPS), и каждому оцениваемому элементу нужно \(p_i>0\) (поддержка), иначе оценка смещена, а не просто зашумлена.Inverse Propensity Scoring: clicks are biased by what the old policy chose to show. Divide each reward by its propensity → an unbiased estimate of a new policy's value from logged data. Foundation of unbiased learning-to-rank. Two catches: small \(p_i\) make \(r_i/p_i\) explode (high variance — cap/clip or use self-normalised IPS), and every evaluable item needs \(p_i>0\) (support), or the estimate is biased, not just noisy.

The most modern idea here. Logged clicks are biased twice over — by position (slide 51) and by the logging policy itself, which only ever showed certain documents. Inverse Propensity Scoring corrects this: weight each observed reward by one over the probability that the old policy displayed it, so items the old system rarely showed but a user still clicked get up-weighted. The result is an unbiased estimate of how a new policy would have performed, computed entirely from logs without running a fresh experiment. Joachims and colleagues built unbiased learning-to-rank on exactly this — train and evaluate rankers from biased click logs. It needs accurate propensities, which is the hard part. Two failure modes beyond that. Variance: a rarely-shown item has a tiny propensity, so r over p explodes — the estimator stays unbiased but can be uselessly noisy. The standard fixes are clipping/capping the propensities or self-normalised IPS (SNIPS), which divides by the sum of weights. And support (positivity): every action you want to evaluate must have had p greater than zero in the logs, or IPS is biased, not merely noisy, because some outcomes are never observed.

Goodhart isn't abstract; it's the daily reality of running a search engine. SEO spam games lexical signals; clickbait games CTR; and the nastiest case is the feedback loop — you train a ranker on click logs, which are biased toward whatever the old ranker showed at the top, so the model learns to reinforce position bias rather than relevance, and the next round of logs is even more biased. The defences echo the whole lecture: keep held-out judgments the optimiser never sees, watch guardrails and satisfaction not just the target, debias clicks with IPS before learning from them, and always confirm an offline win online. One honest, untouchable signal is what keeps the rest from rotting.
