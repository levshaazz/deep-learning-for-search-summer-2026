What happens
$$H_0:\ \overline{\Delta}=0 \quad\text{vs}\quad H_1:\ \overline{\Delta}\neq 0$$

Null \(H_0\) — B's edge is chance; we disprove it. \(\overline{\Delta}{=}0.0397,\ \mathrm{sd}{=}0.0676,\ n{=}15\).

$$p_{\text{perm}}=\#\{\text{flips with }|\overline{\Delta}|\ge 0.0397\}/32768=0.03979$$

Permutation — zero prerequisites. If B≈A each query's sign is a coin flip. Over all \(2^{15}{=}32768\) flips, count how often the mean beats \(0.0397\).

$$t=\overline{\Delta}\big/(\mathrm{sd}/\sqrt{n})=0.0397/(0.0676/\sqrt{15})=2.275$$

Paired \(t\) — fast approximation. \(df=n-1=14\) (degrees of freedom). \(p=0.03914\) from the \(t_{14}\) table (diffs ≈ normal).

$$W^+{=}95,\ W^-{=}25\ \Rightarrow\ W=\min=25$$

Wilcoxon — drops normality. Rank the 15 \(|\Delta|\); 4 are negative (ranks 4,6,7,8) summing to 25. \(p=0.04791\) (table value).

$$0.0398,\ 0.0391,\ 0.0479 \;<\; 0.05$$

Verdict. All three reject \(H_0\): B's edge is statistically significant.

overview
I lead with the permutation test on purpose — it needs no statistics background at all. The null hypothesis is just "B and A are really the same, so the sign of each query's difference is a coin flip." Flip all fifteen signs every possible way — 2^15 = 32768 arrangements — and count how often the mean comes out at least as big as the 0.0397 we actually saw: that fraction, 0.03979, is the p-value. Pure counting, no distribution assumed. Only then do I show the two faster approximations. The paired t-test divides the mean by its standard error — t = 2.275 on df = 14 (degrees of freedom, here n minus one, because estimating the mean spends one), p = 0.03914 — but it assumes the diffs are roughly normal. Wilcoxon drops normality by ranking the absolute diffs: W = 25, p = 0.04791. All three land below 0.05. Smucker, Allan and Carterette recommend the randomization test as the most defensible — which is exactly why we started there.
