Common misconception

the multiple-comparisons trap
What teams do

“We tried 20 variants, one hit \(p<0.05\) — that one's a real winner.”

At \(\alpha=0.05\), 1 in 20 random tests is "significant" by chance. Test 20 variants and you expect one false positive even if none is truly better. Cherry-picking the winning p-value is how teams ship noise.

The fix. Correct for the number of comparisons: Bonferroni (test at \(\alpha/m\)) or Benjamini–Hochberg (control the false-discovery rate). Better still: pre-register one hypothesis, or confirm the winner on a fresh query set / an online A/B test.

This is the most common statistical sin in applied ranking work. A p-value below 0.05 means a 5% chance of a false alarm per test. Run twenty tests and the expected number of false alarms is one — so the "winner" you cherry-picked may be pure noise. Bonferroni divides alpha by the number of tests (conservative); Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false-discovery rate (more powerful). The cleanest defence is to confirm an offline winner on held-out queries or, better, in an online experiment — which is exactly where Part 6 takes us.
