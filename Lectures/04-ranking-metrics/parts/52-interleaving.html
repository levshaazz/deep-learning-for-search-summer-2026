What happens
$$A=[a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4],\quad B=[b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4]$$

Query q1: two input rankings. Take turns (coin flip) picking each next slot — like a sports team draft.

$$\text{draft }[b_1,a_1,b_2,a_2,b_3,a_3] \;\text{tagged}\; [B,A,B,A,B,A]$$

Draft. Each round a coin picks who drafts first, then both rankers add their top remaining doc. B won the first pick every round, so it leads each pair — but the six slots still split evenly: 3 from B \((b_1,b_2,b_3)\) and 3 from A \((a_1,a_2,a_3)\). B wins this query on the clicks, not by taking more slots.

$$5\text{ of 6 slots clicked} \Rightarrow \mathrm{credit}_A=2,\ \mathrm{credit}_B=3$$

Credit. Clicks on A-tagged slots \((a_1,a_3)\) score A; B's three slots \((b_1,b_2,b_3)\) all score B; slot 4 \((a_2)\) goes unclicked — this is the q1 row.

$$\text{over 5 queries: } A=9,\ B=16$$

Totals. B preferred in \(4\) of \(5\) queries.

$$\frac{16}{9+16} = 0.64 > 0.5 \Rightarrow B \text{ wins}$$

Credit share. Within-query comparison largely cancels position bias.

overview
Interleaving is the sharpest tool in online evaluation. Instead of showing A to one group and B to another, you blend both rankers into a single list the user sees — team-draft alternates picks like captains choosing a team, tagging each slot with its source. Every click credits the ranker that placed that result. Because both rankers compete within the same list for the same user, position bias largely cancels: each occupies high and low slots equally often. Each round both teams pick, so the slots split evenly; B wins on the clicks. Over five queries A earns 9 credits, B earns 16, preference for B is 16/25 = 0.64, B preferred in four of five queries. Joachims showed interleaving needs far less traffic than A/B to reach the same confidence.
