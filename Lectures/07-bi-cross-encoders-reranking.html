L5 gave words coordinates; L6 gave the encoder a contrastively-trained space and attention. Today we put that encoder to work two ways. A search system can be fast or careful, but not both everywhere — so it splits the crew. Scouts (bi-encoders) encode the query and every document separately, precompute the doc vectors offline, and sweep the whole corpus with cheap dot-products. Judges (cross-encoders) read the query and a document together, with full attention, and score the pair — accurate, but uncacheable, so they only ever see a shortlist. Stage them and you get the neural cascade, the core architecture of modern neural search. Catchphrase: Scouts and Judges.

Frame the tension as a contrast card. The Scout is fast and wide because it encodes the query and the document separately and precomputes the document side offline; at query time it only does cheap dot-products, so it can sweep millions. The Judge is slow and precise because it reads the query and the document together — but that means its score is a property of the pair, not of the document, so nothing can be precomputed and it costs an O of N read per query. The hiring analogy: screen every résumé cheaply, interview only the finalists. You cannot interview everyone — and you cannot run a cross-encoder over the whole corpus.

State the wall. A cross-encoder must read the query and a document together to score them, so its score is a property of the pair, not of the document — there is nothing to precompute and cache. That forces one model read per document, for every query: O of N. At a hundred documents it is instant; at ten thousand it is seconds; at a million it will not finish in any reasonable time, and real corpora are a billion. This is the problem the rest of the lecture solves: send a cheap Scout first.

Если кодировать запрос и документ порознь, вектор документа не зависит от запроса. Значит, все \(N\) векторов документов считаются офлайн, один раз. Дорогой прогон модели \(\times 10^6\) превращается в дешёвое произведение \(\times 10^6\). Это и есть Разведчик — би-энкодер.An expensive model pass \(\times 10^6\) becomes a cheap dot-product \(\times 10^6\). That is the Scout — the bi-encoder.

The fix in one line: encode separately so the document side is query-independent, then precompute it. The expensive part — running the encoder — happens once per document, offline. At query time you run the encoder once on the query and then sweep cheap dot products. That trade, a million model passes down to one model pass plus a million dot products, is the entire reason retrieval is feasible. The architecture that does it is the bi-encoder.
РазвилкаThe fork

Кодировать порознь — или вместе?Encode apart — or together?

Энкодер L6 даёт пространство. Применить его к паре \((q,d)\) можно ровно двумя способами — и на этой развилке держится вся лекция.L6’s encoder gives a space. There are exactly two ways to apply it to a pair \((q,d)\) — and the whole lecture hangs on this one fork.

The pivot. We have an encoder. Apply it to a query-document pair in one of two ways: run it twice and compare the outputs (the bi-encoder), or feed the pair in together and let attention mix them before scoring (the cross-encoder). Separate buys precompute; joint buys interaction. Everything follows from this choice.

Би-энкодер (Разведчики)The Bi-encoder (Scouts)

Запрос и документ кодируются порознь в одно пространство; оценка — косинус. Обе башни обучены в одно пространство, поэтому \(q\) и \(d\) сравнимы; башня документа не видит запрос и работает офлайн — её векторы кэшируются (callback L2: косинус — линейка Сэра Косинуса; L5: слова имеют координаты).Query and document are encoded separately into one space; the score is a cosine. Both towers are trained into one space, so \(q\) and \(d\) are comparable; the document tower never sees the query and runs offline — its vectors are cached (callback L2: cosine is Sir Cosine’s ruler; L5: words have coordinates).

The architecture, redrawn. Two towers, the query encoder and the document encoder, map their inputs into one shared embedding space — and because they are trained into that one space (a single siamese encoder for SBERT, or two separately-weighted ones for DPR), a query vector and a document vector are directly comparable. The score is the cosine between the two output vectors, Sir Cosine's ruler from Lecture 2. The crucial asymmetry: the document tower never sees the query, so it runs offline, once, and its vectors are cached — that is what the label d-cached marks. The next slides mount the live widget and then compute the score by hand.

Две башни, одно пространствоTwo towers, one space

прямой проход; Сэр Косинус читает уголthe forward pass; Sir Cosine reads the angle
The bi-encoder forward pass, as a mounted widget driven by the deck’s arrow keys. Step zero: two separate towers, one for the query and one for the document — crucially, they never attend to each other. Step one: each tower emits a single vector, a point in one shared space — the map from Lecture 5. Step two: the score is the angle between those two vectors; a small angle is a high cosine, which is Sir Cosine’s ruler from Lecture 2. Step three: because the document tower never depends on the query, every document vector is computed once, offline, and cached — so at query time you do one encode and a sweep of cheap dot-products. That precompute is the entire reason the Scouts are fast.

А ещё эти \(N\) произведений можно свести к сублинейному числу через приближённый поиск соседей — это L9 (ANN, джамп-гейты).And even the \(N\) dots can be made sub-linear with approximate nearest-neighbour search — that is L9 (ANN, jump-gates).

The speed case, stated once. Because the document tower is query-independent, you file every document vector in advance, like a card catalog. Searching means flipping cards — cheap dot products — instead of re-reading every book, which would be a full model pass each. Building the catalog is the expensive part, but you do it once, offline. Using it is cheap. The forward link: even the linear sweep of dot products can be made sub-linear with approximate nearest neighbour search, which is Lecture 9.

The recipe box, three moves. (1) Pooling: causal models make the last token (EOS) the only one that attended over everything, so RepLLaMA and E5-mistral read the embedding there; NV-Embed instead trains a latent-attention pooling layer over all token states. (2) Un-mask: LLM2Vec (arXiv:2404.05961) enables bidirectional attention plus masked-next-token-prediction adaptation; NV-Embed simply removes the causal mask during contrastive training. (3) Instructions inherited from the E5/BGE prefix idea, now free-text; LoRA makes the fine-tune affordable. After these three moves the training story is exactly the contrastive objective taught later in this deck — positives, hard negatives, InfoNCE.

Бинарные эмбеддинги — 32× меньше памяти и ~25× быстрее поиск (расстояние Хэмминга), теряя ~4% качества; int8 — 4× меньше, >99%. Так Вики из 41M текстов ищется за ~1 c на CPU.Binary embeddings are 32× smaller and search ~25× faster (Hamming distance) for ~4% quality; int8 is 4× smaller at >99%. The trick: search the quantized vectors, then rescore the top-\(k\) at full precision. That searches Wikipedia’s 41M texts in ~1 s on CPU.

Quantization is the other lever on index cost. A thousand-and-twenty-four-dimension float32 vector is four kilobytes; cast it to int8 and it is one kilobyte, four times smaller, with over ninety-nine percent of retrieval quality. Cast it all the way to one bit per dimension — binary — and it is one hundred twenty-eight bytes, thirty-two times smaller, with search about twenty-five times faster because comparisons become Hamming distance, for roughly a four percent quality cost. The standard recipe recovers most of that: retrieve on the quantized vectors, then rescore the top-k at full precision. Hugging Face showed this searches all forty-one million Wikipedia texts in about a second on a CPU.

Запрос и документ читаются вместе: токены запроса смотрят на токены документа через \([\text{SEP}]\) (callback L6: внимание). Точно, но оценка — свойство пары: кэша нет. Это интервью один-на-один. Accurate, but the score is a property of the pair: no cache. It is a one-on-one interview. Both query and document share one token budget and attention is quadratic in the \((q{+}d)\) length: a long document is truncated, and the right span past the cut is silently scored as a non-answer.

The Judge's architecture. Concatenate CLS, the query tokens, a SEP, the document tokens, and a SEP into one joint input. Run it through the transformer with full self-attention, so query tokens attend to document tokens across the separator — the interaction a bi-encoder can never have. Read the CLS vector through a small linear head and a sigmoid into one relevance score. It is a one-on-one interview: accurate because both are read together, uncacheable for the same reason.

The loss behind every modern retriever, and a callback to Lecture 6. InfoNCE is a softmax over similarities: the numerator is the positive pair, the denominator sums the positive and all the negatives, divided by a temperature. Minimizing it pulls the query toward its true document and pushes it away from the rest — exactly DPR's negative log-likelihood of the positive. Two knobs matter. Temperature sharpens the distribution; a small tau makes hard negatives dominate. And batch size is a lever, because the negatives are largely the other documents in the batch — DPR's accuracy climbs with batch size, and E5 trains at a batch of thirty-two thousand to drown the positive in negatives. The in-batch trick, made live. Step zero: a batch is B matched query-document pairs, the diagonal. Step one: score every query against every document in the batch, a B-by-B grid — and the off-diagonal cells are negatives you got for free, with no extra forward pass. Step two: the diagonal is the positives, the rest of each row are in-batch negatives, so a bigger batch gives more negatives — but they are mostly easy, random ones, which is exactly why slide 37 then mines hard negatives on top. Step three: a row softmax at temperature tau is the InfoNCE target, and training pushes each row's probability mass onto the diagonal positive. This is the picture behind the batch-size lever from the contrastive-objective slide.

Лучше негативы — лучше Разведчик: случайные → BM25-негативы (DPR) → глобальные из ANN-индекса (ANCE) → кросс-батч + денойзинг (RocketQA). Но «сырые» трудные негативы прячут ложные (на деле релевантные) и роняют MRR с \(32{,}4\) до \(26{,}0\); кросс-энкодер их отсеивает → \(36{,}4\).Better negatives, better Scout: random → BM25 negatives (DPR) → global ANN-index negatives (ANCE) → cross-batch + denoising (RocketQA). The whole history of dense retrieval is a history of better negatives. Random and in-batch negatives are easy; DPR added one BM25 hard negative; ANCE mined global hard negatives from an asynchronously refreshed index of the entire corpus; RocketQA pushed cross-batch negatives and, crucially, denoised them. The bar chart is RocketQA's own ablation. Cross-batch lifts MRR slightly over in-batch. But naive hard negatives — the top retrieved documents — secretly contain false negatives that are actually relevant, and training on them regresses MRR by six points, from thirty-two four down to twenty-six. Using a cross-encoder to filter those out turns the same hard negatives into a gain, up to thirty-six four. The lesson: a hard negative that is really a twin of the answer poisons training.

The second axis of modern training, after negatives. A cross-encoder sees query and document together and is far more accurate, but too slow to retrieve. So we use it as a teacher: it scores pairs, and the bi-encoder student learns to reproduce its judgments. Margin-MSE is the clean way to do it — the student matches the teacher's margin between a relevant and a non-relevant document, not the raw score, which makes it robust to the two architectures living on different scales. Soft teacher scores are a much richer signal than binary relevant-or-not labels. This is the recipe inside TAS-B, ColBERTv2, RocketQAv2 and E5, and the listwise version uses KL divergence over a whole candidate list. How a state-of-the-art embedder is actually built — three stages. Hybrid retrieval, and the callback to Lecture 3. A single dense vector is strong on meaning but blurs exact tokens — names, product codes, rare words, typos — which is exactly where lexical BM25 shines. So run both and fuse. You cannot add their scores, because the scales are incompatible, so Reciprocal Rank Fusion combines them by rank: sum one over k plus the rank in each list, with k conventionally sixty. It is the default hybrid in Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Qdrant. The motivation is the honest twist from Lecture 3 — zero-shot, BM25's nDCG of 0.43 often beats DPR's 0.23 on BEIR — so keeping the lexical signal makes retrieval robust. Anthropic reports their contextual hybrid cut retrieval failures by about half, and adding a reranker pushed that to about two-thirds.

The depth dial. Raising k lets the Judges see more of the Scouts' output, so they can recover answers buried deeper — but each extra candidate is another cross-encoder pass, and latency rises roughly linearly with k; batching on a GPU shrinks the per-pair constant, as in the cascade budget, but the growth stays linear. The answer is B. There is no free k: pick the shallowest depth that still captures the recall your retriever provides, and trade the rest against your latency budget. When the retriever already saturates recall at a shallow depth, going deeper buys nothing but cost.

The honest twist before we leave. It is tempting to think a learned dense retriever always beats old BM25 — it does not. On the BEIR zero-shot benchmark, averaged over eighteen out-of-domain datasets, BM25 reaches nDCG at ten of zero-point-four-three while the original DPR checkpoint — trained on question-answering data, not MS MARCO — falls to zero-point-two-three: lexical search wins out of domain, while a late-interaction model like ColBERTv2 climbs back to zero-point-five, and MS MARCO-trained single-vector models sit in between, still below BM25 on average. In-domain the story flips — on MS MARCO, a DPR-style dense bi-encoder's MRR of zero-point-three-three beats BM25's zero-point-one-nine; note the original DPR paper never evaluated MS MARCO, so that figure comes from later DPR-style reproductions. A single dense vector forgets too much; the fix is hybrid retrieval and richer late interaction, which is Lecture 8.

The engineer's checklist, pulling the lecture's practice into one place. One: stage as a cascade — retrieve wide with the bi-encoder, rerank shallow with the cross-encoder, and pick the depth k from your latency budget rather than over-reranking. Two: go hybrid by default — BM25 plus dense fused with RRF survives out-of-domain shifts and rare-term queries that a single vector blurs. Three: shrink the index with Matryoshka truncation and int8 or binary quantization, rescoring the top-k at full precision. Four: match the model to your domain and language, evaluate on your own data, fine-tune or use GPL when it sags, normalize vectors, and respect the role prefixes and the token limit. Five: operate it — re-index when the corpus drifts, cache and warm-start, budget the cross-encoder's cost on long pairs, and A/B test online. That is the whole lecture, as a runbook.
