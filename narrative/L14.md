## Thesis
Embedding spaces are distorted maps. **Raw** language-model vectors crowd into a narrow cone
(**anisotropy** — random words look 0.99-similar *in GPT-2's last layer*; BERT is about half that),
and a few points sit in everyone's neighbour list (**hubness** — a curse-of-dimensionality effect,
the Wraith's cousin from L2). Cosine lies on this map. Two repairs, aimed at two different failures:
re-project the space (center → whiten → contrastive isotropy) fixes the **scale**; tax the crowded
hubs at query time (CSLS) fixes the **order**. And a third thing the lecture now insists on: for a
2026 contrastive embedder, several of these repairs are **negative** — so measure before you treat.

## The three separations the hour turns on
1. **Concentration (L13) vs anisotropy.** Concentration is about *random directions* and scales with
   \(d\); anisotropy is about a *shared mean* and, by \(\mathbb{E}[\cos] = c^2/(c^2+\sigma^2)\), does
   not involve \(d\) at all. Centering turns one regime into the other on our own toy: 0.8985 → −0.0323.
2. **Scale vs order.** Anisotropy breaks thresholds, calibration and score fusion, not the ranking of
   one query's candidates. Hubness breaks the candidate set itself. Different diseases, different cures.
3. **Measure vs name.** The lecture measures anisotropy by mean random-pair cosine, which is exactly
   the measure IsoScore refutes (one cloud, four rotations → 0.216 vs 0.97–0.99). Say "high average
   cosine" (true, and it does break the scale); reserve "anisotropy" for IsoScore.
